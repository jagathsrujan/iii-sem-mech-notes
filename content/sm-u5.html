Strength of Materials · Unit 5

Thin Cylinders and Combined Stresses

The first half of this unit finds the stresses in the wall of a thin cylinder or sphere under internal pressure (boilers, gas cylinders, pipes, air receivers). The second half finds the stresses on any inclined plane when a point carries two normal stresses and a shear stress, and gives the largest normal and shear stresses there — the principal stresses — by formula and by Mohr's circle.

Concepts

Exam pattern. In the old papers this material is Unit II: one 8–10 mark theory part (principal stresses and planes, simple shear, two perpendicular stresses) and one 10–12 mark numerical (stresses on an oblique plane, principal stresses, Mohr's circle). Thin cylinders are new in the 2026 syllabus, so expect one derivation (hoop and longitudinal stress, change in volume) and one numerical on a cylinder or a sphere.

1. Thin and thick pressure vessels

A pressure vessel is a closed container that holds a fluid (gas or liquid) at a pressure different from the outside. Boilers, LPG cylinders, air receivers, pipes and storage tanks are examples.

A vessel is thin when its wall thickness t is small compared with its diameter d. The usual rule is:

\[ \frac{t}{d} < \frac{1}{20} \quad (\text{some books use } t/d < 1/10) \]

t = wall thickness (mm), d = internal diameter (mm).

In a thin vessel, we make three assumptions:

  • The stress is uniform through the thickness of the wall.
  • The radial stress (equal to p on the inside and zero on the outside) is small compared with the other stresses, so we ignore it.
  • The material is homogeneous and isotropic, and the stress stays in the elastic range.

When t/d is larger, the hoop stress changes across the wall (Lamé's theory) and the vessel is thick. Thick cylinders are not in this unit.

2. Hoop and longitudinal stresses in a thin cylinder

Internal pressure p tries to burst the cylinder in two ways. Each way gives one stress in the wall (Fig. 5.1):

  • Hoop (circumferential) stress σc: the pressure tries to split the cylinder along its length (on a longitudinal plane). σc acts along the circumference, tangent to the wall.
  • Longitudinal stress σl: the pressure on the two ends tries to split the cylinder across its length (on a transverse plane). σl acts parallel to the axis.

Hoop stress. Cut the cylinder (length L) on a horizontal diametral plane and take the upper half as a free body (Fig. 5.2). The net upward force of the pressure on a curved surface equals p times the projected area d × L. Two wall strips, each of area t × L, resist it.

\[ p\,d\,L = \sigma_c \,(2\,t\,L) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_c = \frac{p\,d}{2\,t} \]

p = internal pressure (N/mm²), d = internal diameter (mm), t = wall thickness (mm).

Longitudinal stress. Cut the cylinder by a transverse plane (Fig. 5.3). The pressure on the end, over the area πd²/4, pushes the end away. The ring of wall, of area about π d t, resists it.

\[ p\,\frac{\pi}{4} d^2 = \sigma_l \,(\pi\, d\, t) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_l = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t} = \frac{\sigma_c}{2} \]

Both stresses are tensile. The hoop stress is two times the longitudinal stress.

Memory trick. "Hoop is twice." The cylinder always fails first by a crack along its length (a longitudinal split), because σc is the larger stress. A frozen water pipe splits along its length for this reason.

Quick example. d = 1000 mm, t = 10 mm, p = 2 N/mm². Then σc = 2 × 1000 / (2 × 10) = 100 N/mm² and σl = 50 N/mm².

Design for thickness. If the permissible tensile stress is σ, the hoop stress decides the thickness: \( t = \dfrac{p\,d}{2\,\sigma} \).

Maximum shear stress in the wall. σc and σl are principal stresses (no shear acts on these planes). In the plane of the wall, \( \tau_{max} = (\sigma_c - \sigma_l)/2 = pd/8t \). If we include the radial stress (about zero), the absolute maximum is \( \sigma_c/2 = pd/4t \).

3. Efficiency of riveted (or welded) joints

A boiler shell is made from plates joined by rivets or welds. The holes make the joint weaker than the solid plate. The efficiency of a joint η is:

\[ \eta = \frac{\text{strength of the joint}}{\text{strength of the solid plate}} \]

η is less than 1 (for example 0.70 = 70 %).

  • The longitudinal joint runs along the length. The hoop stress acts across it. So use its efficiency ηl with σc: \( \sigma_c = \dfrac{p\,d}{2\,t\,\eta_l} \).
  • The circumferential joint runs round the shell. The longitudinal stress acts across it. So use its efficiency ηc with σl: \( \sigma_l = \dfrac{p\,d}{4\,t\,\eta_c} \).
Common mistake. Do not pair the names by sound. The longitudinal joint goes with the hoop stress, and the circumferential joint goes with the longitudinal stress. Find t from both equations and take the larger value.

4. Change in diameter, length and volume

The wall carries σc and σl together, so each strain has a Poisson's ratio (μ) term:

\[ \varepsilon_c = \frac{\delta d}{d} = \frac{\sigma_c}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_l}{E} = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}\,(2-\mu) \]\[ \varepsilon_l = \frac{\delta L}{L} = \frac{\sigma_l}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_c}{E} = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}\,(1-2\mu) \]

The circumferential strain equals the diametral strain, because the circumference is πd.

The volume is V = (π/4)d²L. A small change gives δV/V = 2 δd/d + δL/L. So the volumetric strain is:

\[ \varepsilon_v = \frac{\delta V}{V} = 2\varepsilon_c + \varepsilon_l = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}\,(5 - 4\mu) \]

δV = εv × (π/4)d²L. E = Young's modulus (N/mm²), μ = Poisson's ratio.

Extra fluid to pump in. To raise the pressure from 0 to p in a full vessel, the extra fluid fills the increase in the vessel volume plus the compression of the fluid already inside: \( \delta V_{total} = \delta V + pV/K \), where K is the bulk modulus of the fluid.

5. Thin spherical shells

Cut a sphere on any diametral plane (Fig. 5.5). The pressure on the projected circle πd²/4 is resisted by the ring of area π d t. Every diametral plane gives the same result, so the stress is the same in all directions in the wall.

\[ p\,\frac{\pi}{4}d^2 = \sigma\,(\pi\,d\,t) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t} \]\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\delta d}{d} = \frac{\sigma}{E}(1-\mu) = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(1-\mu), \qquad \frac{\delta V}{V} = 3\varepsilon = \frac{3\,p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(1-\mu) \]

V = (π/6)d³, so δV/V = 3 δd/d. With a joint, σ = pd/(4tη).

For the same p, d and stress, a sphere needs half the wall thickness of a cylinder. That is why large gas stores are spheres (Horton spheres).

6. Stresses on an oblique plane — sign convention

In a shaft, a beam or a vessel wall, a point often carries normal stresses in two directions and a shear stress together. These are combined stresses. The material may fail on a plane that is inclined to the loads. So we need the stresses on any plane through the point.

Sign convention used in these notes.
• Tensile normal stress is positive; compressive is negative.
• τxy is positive when it acts upward on the right face (and to the right on the top face), as in Fig. 5.8.
• θ is the angle between the oblique plane AC and the plane on which σx acts (the vertical face). This is the same as the angle between the normal of AC and the x-axis, measured anticlockwise.
• τθ on plane AC is positive when it acts down the plane, towards C (Fig. 5.8). This gives the usual textbook (Rajput, Bansal, Bhavikatti) formulas.
Check the angle in the question. "A plane at 30° to the axis of the minor stress" means the plane makes 30° with the line along which σ2 acts. That line is parallel to the face on which σ1 acts, so θ = 30° from the σ1 plane in the formulas. Test yourself: at θ = 0 you must get σn = σ1.

7. Case 1 — uniaxial stress

A bar of area A carries a pull P, so σ = P/A on the cross-section. Take a plane at θ to the cross-section (Fig. 5.6). Its area is A/cosθ, and the full force P acts on it. Resolve P normal and parallel to the plane:

\[ \sigma_n = \frac{P\cos\theta}{A/\cos\theta} = \sigma\cos^2\theta, \qquad \tau_\theta = \frac{P\sin\theta}{A/\cos\theta} = \frac{\sigma}{2}\sin 2\theta \]\[ \sigma_R = \sqrt{\sigma_n^2+\tau_\theta^2} = \sigma\cos\theta \]

σn is largest (= σ) at θ = 0. τθ is largest (= σ/2) at θ = 45°.

Real world. A ductile mild-steel bar in tension fails by shear on planes near 45° (cup-and-cone fracture), because τmax = σ/2 acts there. A cast-iron bar is weak in tension and breaks flat across, at θ = 0.

8. Case 2 — two perpendicular normal stresses

A point carries σx on the vertical faces and σy on the horizontal faces, with no shear (Fig. 5.7). Take the wedge ABC with AC of unit width and length AC. Then AB = AC cosθ and BC = AC sinθ. Resolve the forces normal and parallel to AC:

\[ \sigma_n = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} + \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta \]\[ \tau_\theta = \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta, \qquad \tau_{max} = \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2} \text{ at } \theta = 45^\circ \]

If σy is compressive, put it in as a negative number.

Resultant stress and obliquity. σn and τθ combine into a resultant σR. The angle φ between σR and the normal of the plane is the obliquity:

\[ \sigma_R = \sqrt{\sigma_n^2 + \tau_\theta^2}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{\tau_\theta}{\sigma_n} \]

Quick example. σx = 100, σy = 0, θ = 30°: σn = 50 + 50 × 0.5 = 75 N/mm², τθ = 50 × 0.866 = 43.3 N/mm², σR = 86.6 N/mm² = 100 cos30°, φ = 30°.

9. Case 3 — two normal stresses with shear

Now the faces also carry τxy (Fig. 5.8). By complementary shear, the same τxy acts on the vertical and horizontal faces. The wedge has two more forces: τxy·AB (down on AB) and τxy·BC (to the left on BC). Resolving as before gives the general result:

\[ \sigma_n = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} + \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta \]\[ \tau_\theta = \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta - \tau_{xy}\cos 2\theta \]

Cases 1 and 2 are special cases: put τxy = 0 (and σy = 0 for case 1). Also σn(θ) + σn(θ + 90°) = σx + σy (first invariant).

Simple shear (σx = σy = 0): \( \sigma_n = \tau\sin 2\theta \) and \( \tau_\theta = -\tau\cos 2\theta \). On the diagonal (θ = 45°), σn = τ and the shear is zero. So pure shear is the same as a tension τ and a compression τ on planes at 45° (Fig. 5.9).

Real world. A cast-iron shaft in torsion is in pure shear. It breaks on a 45° helix, perpendicular to the tension diagonal, because cast iron is weaker in tension than in shear. A chalk stick twisted in your fingers shows the same helical break.

10. Principal planes, principal stresses and maximum shear

Principal planes are the planes on which the shear stress is zero. The normal stresses on them are the principal stresses. One is the maximum normal stress at the point (major, σ1) and the other is the minimum (minor, σ2).

Put τθ = 0 (or dσn/dθ = 0, which gives the same equation):

\[ \tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2\,\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y} \]\[ \sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2} \]

The two principal planes are 90° apart (θp and θp + 90°). To find which one carries σ1, put θp into the σn formula.

Maximum shear stress. Put dτθ/dθ = 0. The planes of maximum shear are at 45° to the principal planes:

\[ \tau_{max} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2} = \frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_2}{2} \]

On the planes of maximum shear, the normal stress is not zero: σn = (σx + σy)/2.

Principal strains. In plane stress (σ3 = 0), the strains along the principal directions are the principal strains:

\[ \varepsilon_1 = \frac{\sigma_1}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_2}{E}, \qquad \varepsilon_2 = \frac{\sigma_2}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_1}{E}, \qquad \varepsilon_3 = -\frac{\mu(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)}{E} \]

No shear strain acts along the principal directions. Strain gauges (rosettes) measure strains, and these equations give the stresses back.

11. Mohr's circle for stress

Mohr's circle is a graph of the σn, τ formulas. Each point on the circle shows the normal and shear stress on one plane through the point. A plane at θ on the element is a point at 2θ on the circle. It is a quick graphical method and a check on the formulas.

Mohr's circle convention (the same as the widget). Plot σ along the horizontal axis (tension to the right). Plot the point X for the σx face at (σx, +τxy) — that is, τxy upward. Plot Y at (σy, −τxy). The plane at θ (anticlockwise on the element) is the point P at 2θ clockwise from X. The ordinate of P equals −τθ of Section 9: P below the axis means τθ acts down the plane, as drawn in Fig. 5.8. Many textbooks plot the shear the other way up and turn 2θ anticlockwise; the magnitudes are the same.

Construction — case 1 and 2 (principal stresses σ1, σ2 known, no shear):

  1. Draw the σ axis and the τ axis through O. Choose a scale (e.g. 1 cm = 10 N/mm²).
  2. Mark A at OA = σ1 and B at OB = σ2 (to the left of O if compressive). For uniaxial stress, B is at O.
  3. Draw a circle on AB as diameter. Its centre C is at (σ1 + σ2)/2 and its radius is (σ1 − σ2)/2.
  4. From CA, turn the angle 2θ clockwise and mark P on the circle. Drop PL onto the σ axis.
  5. Measure: σn = OL, τθ = PL, σR = OP, obliquity φ = ∠POL. The top point D gives τmax = radius.

Construction — case 3 (σx, σy and τxy):

  1. Plot X(σx, τxy) above the axis and Y(σy, −τxy) below it.
  2. Join XY. It cuts the σ axis at the centre C.
  3. Draw the circle with centre C and radius CX.
  4. The circle cuts the σ axis at σ1 (right) and σ2 (left). The angle from CX to the σ1 point is 2θp.
  5. For a plane at θ, turn 2θ clockwise from CX to get P. Its coordinates give σn and the shear on that plane.
  6. τmax = radius, at the top and bottom of the circle (planes at 45° to the principal planes).

Special circles: pure shear gives a circle with its centre at O (σ1,2 = ±τ). Equal stresses σx = σy with no shear (the wall of a sphere) give a point: every plane carries the same stress and no shear.

Change σx, σy, τxy and θ below, and watch P move on the circle. Start with 80, −40, 30 and 30° — these are the data of N2 and N3. The widget shows τ = −36.96 on the 30° plane: that is the ordinate of P. N2 gives τθ = +36.96 for the same stress (acting down the plane).

Exam tip. When the question says "verify by Mohr's circle", draw it to a stated scale on the answer sheet, label O, C, X, Y, A, B, P and the angles, and write the measured values next to the calculated ones. Small differences from drawing are acceptable.

Theory questions

Q1. What are principal stresses and principal planes? Derive the expressions for the principal stresses, the position of the principal planes and the maximum shear stress for a member under σx, σy and τxy.

Nov-Dec 2025 Q3(a) · Nov 2024 Q3(a) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q1 · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q1

Definitions, the wedge sketch, the σn, τθ derivation, then put τθ = 0 and dτθ/dθ = 0.

Model answer

Definitions. At a point in a stressed body there are always three mutually perpendicular planes (two in plane stress) on which the shear stress is zero. These planes are the principal planes. The normal stresses on them are the principal stresses. The larger one is the major principal stress σ1 (the maximum normal stress at the point); the smaller one is the minor principal stress σ2 (the minimum normal stress).

Stresses on an oblique plane. Consider the wedge ABC of unit thickness (Fig. Q1.1). Plane AC makes θ with the plane AB of σx. AB = AC cosθ, BC = AC sinθ. Forces on the wedge:

  • On AB: σx·AC cosθ (horizontal) and τxy·AC cosθ (vertical).
  • On BC: σy·AC sinθ (vertical) and τxy·AC sinθ (horizontal).
  • On AC: σn·AC (normal) and τθ·AC (along AC).

Resolve normal to AC and divide by AC:

\[ \sigma_n = \sigma_x\cos^2\theta + \sigma_y\sin^2\theta + 2\tau_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta \]

Use cos²θ = (1 + cos2θ)/2, sin²θ = (1 − cos2θ)/2 and 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ:

\[ \sigma_n = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} + \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta \quad (1) \]

Resolve along AC:

\[ \tau_\theta = (\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\sin\theta\cos\theta - \tau_{xy}(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) = \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta - \tau_{xy}\cos 2\theta \quad (2) \]

Principal planes. On a principal plane τθ = 0. From (2):

\[ \tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2\,\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y} \quad (3) \]

Equation (3) gives two values of 2θp, 180° apart, so the two principal planes are 90° apart. (The same equation comes from dσn/dθ = 0, so the principal stresses are the maximum and minimum normal stresses.)

Principal stresses. From (3), draw a right triangle with sides 2τxy and (σx − σy), so

\[ \sin 2\theta_p = \frac{\pm\tau_{xy}}{\sqrt{(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2})^2+\tau_{xy}^2}}, \quad \cos 2\theta_p = \frac{\pm\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}}{\sqrt{(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2})^2+\tau_{xy}^2}} \]

Put these in (1):

\[ \sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2} \]

Maximum shear stress. Put dτθ/dθ = 0 in (2): (σx − σy) cos2θ + 2τxy sin2θ = 0, so

\[ \tan 2\theta_s = -\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2\,\tau_{xy}} \]

tan2θs = −1/tan2θp, so 2θs = 2θp ± 90° and θs = θp ± 45°. The planes of maximum shear are at 45° to the principal planes. Substitution gives

\[ \tau_{max} = \pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2} = \frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_2}{2} \]

with the normal stress (σx + σy)/2 on these planes (Fig. Q1.2).

Q2. Derive expressions for the normal, tangential and resultant stresses on an oblique plane of a member subjected to two mutually perpendicular direct stresses. Find the obliquity and the maximum shear stress. Explain the use of Mohr's circle for this case.

Aug-Sep 2023 Q3(a) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q1

Wedge sketch, resolve forces, σn, τθ, σR, φ, τmax at 45°, then the Mohr's circle steps.

Model answer

A rectangular block of unit thickness carries the tensile stresses σx (on the vertical faces) and σy (on the horizontal faces), σx > σy. Find the stresses on plane AC, inclined at θ to the plane of σx (Fig. Q2.1).

Forces on the wedge ABC. AB = AC cosθ and BC = AC sinθ.

  • Px = σx · AC cosθ, horizontal, on AB.
  • Py = σy · AC sinθ, vertical, on BC.

Normal stress. Resolve normal to AC:

\[ \sigma_n \cdot AC = P_x\cos\theta + P_y\sin\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_n = \sigma_x\cos^2\theta + \sigma_y\sin^2\theta = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} + \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta \]

Tangential stress. Resolve along AC:

\[ \tau_\theta \cdot AC = P_x\sin\theta - P_y\cos\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \tau_\theta = (\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta \]

Resultant and obliquity.

\[ \sigma_R = \sqrt{\sigma_n^2+\tau_\theta^2}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{\tau_\theta}{\sigma_n} \]

Points to note.

  • σn is maximum (= σx) at θ = 0 and minimum (= σy) at θ = 90°. So σx and σy are the principal stresses.
  • τθ is maximum at θ = 45°: τmax = (σx − σy)/2.
  • If one stress is compressive, put it in with a negative sign. Then τmax = (σx + |σy|)/2.
  • For a single stress σ (σy = 0): σn = σ cos²θ, τθ = (σ/2) sin2θ, τmax = σ/2 at 45°.

Mohr's circle (use). Mohr's circle gives the same results by a drawing and is a check on the calculation. Mark OA = σx and OB = σy on the σ axis. Draw a circle on AB as diameter, centre C. Turn CA through 2θ (clockwise, as in Fig. Q2.2) to get the point P. Then σn = OL (L is the foot of P on the σ axis), τθ = PL, σR = OP, φ = ∠POL, and τmax = radius = (σx − σy)/2.

The direction of turning (and of plotting τ) only changes the sign of τθ, not its value.

Q3. Derive an expression for the stresses induced by a state of simple shear. Show that it is the same as equal tension and compression on planes at 45°.

Nov 2024 Q3(b) · Jan 2025 Q3(b) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q3(a)

Complementary shear, the wedge on the diagonal, σn = τ sin2θ, then principal stresses ±τ at 45°.

Model answer

State of simple shear. An element ABCD of unit thickness carries only shear stress τ on its four faces and no normal stress (Fig. Q3.1). Shear τ on faces AB and CD forms a couple. For equilibrium, an equal and opposite couple must act on faces AD and BC, so the same τ acts there (complementary shear stress).

Stresses on plane AC at θ. Take the wedge ABC with AB = AC cosθ and BC = AC sinθ. The forces are τ·AB (along AB) and τ·BC (along BC). Resolve normal to AC:

\[ \sigma_n \cdot AC = \tau\,AC\cos\theta\sin\theta + \tau\,AC\sin\theta\cos\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_n = \tau\sin 2\theta \]

Resolve along AC:

\[ \tau_\theta \cdot AC = -\tau\,AC\cos^2\theta + \tau\,AC\sin^2\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \tau_\theta = -\tau\cos 2\theta \]

(This is the general result with σx = σy = 0, τxy = τ.)

Diagonal planes.

  • θ = 45° (diagonal AC): σn = +τ (tension), τθ = 0.
  • θ = 135° (diagonal BD): σn = −τ (compression), τθ = 0.

So the diagonal planes are principal planes, with σ1 = +τ and σ2 = −τ. A state of simple shear is equivalent to a tensile stress τ and an equal compressive stress τ on planes at 45° to the shear planes. The maximum normal stress equals the shear stress.

Consequences.

  • The diagonal BD of the element gets longer and AC gets shorter (or the other way, with the sense of τ). This gives the relation E = 2G(1 + μ).
  • A brittle shaft in torsion (pure shear) breaks on a 45° helix, perpendicular to the tensile stress.
  • On Mohr's circle, pure shear is a circle of radius τ with its centre at the origin.

Q4. With neat sketches, describe principal stresses and principal strains.

Jan 2025 Q3(a)

Define both, sketch the principal element, give σ1,2, θp, ε1,2,3 and τmax; mention strain gauges.

Model answer

Principal planes and stresses. At any point in a loaded body there are three mutually perpendicular planes that carry no shear stress. These are the principal planes. The normal stresses on them are the principal stresses σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3. In plane stress (a thin plate, the wall of a vessel) σ3 = 0 and

\[ \sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}, \qquad \tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y} \]
  • σ1 is the largest and σ2 the smallest normal stress at the point.
  • The principal planes are 90° apart.
  • The maximum shear stress (σ1 − σ2)/2 acts on planes at 45° to them.
  • Example: in a thin cylinder the hoop and longitudinal directions are principal directions, σ1 = pd/2t, σ2 = pd/4t.

Principal strains. The linear strains along the principal directions are the principal strains. No shear strain acts in these directions, so a small square drawn along them stays a rectangle. The principal strain is the maximum (or minimum) linear strain at the point. With Hooke's law and Poisson's ratio μ:

\[ \varepsilon_1 = \frac{\sigma_1}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_2}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_3}{E}, \quad \varepsilon_2 = \frac{\sigma_2}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_1}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_3}{E}, \quad \varepsilon_3 = \frac{\sigma_3}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_1}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_2}{E} \]

In plane stress (σ3 = 0): ε1 = (σ1 − μσ2)/E, ε2 = (σ2 − μσ1)/E and ε3 = −μ(σ1 + σ2)/E (the thickness changes even though σ3 = 0).

Use. Stresses cannot be measured directly. Strain gauges (a rosette of three gauges) measure the strains, and the principal strains give the principal stresses back:

\[ \sigma_1 = \frac{E(\varepsilon_1+\mu\varepsilon_2)}{1-\mu^2}, \qquad \sigma_2 = \frac{E(\varepsilon_2+\mu\varepsilon_1)}{1-\mu^2} \]

Designers compare σ1 (brittle materials) or τmax (ductile materials) with the allowable stress.

Q5. Derive expressions for the hoop stress and the longitudinal stress in a thin cylinder under internal pressure. Hence show that the change in volume is δV = (pd/4tE)(5 − 4μ)V. Write the corresponding results for a thin spherical shell.

Syllabus topic

Two free-body sketches, two force balances, then the strains, then δV/V = 2δd/d + δL/L.

Model answer

Thin cylinder. A closed cylinder of internal diameter d, length L and wall thickness t (t/d < 1/20) carries an internal fluid pressure p. We assume that the stresses are uniform through the wall and the radial stress is negligible.

1. Hoop (circumferential) stress σc. The cylinder can burst along a longitudinal plane. Cut it on a horizontal diametral plane and take the upper half (Fig. Q5.1).

  • Bursting force = pressure × projected area = p · d · L.
  • Resisting force = σc × area of two wall sections = σc · 2tL.
\[ p\,d\,L = \sigma_c\,2\,t\,L \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_c = \frac{p\,d}{2\,t} \]

2. Longitudinal stress σl. The cylinder can burst across a transverse plane (Fig. Q5.2).

  • Bursting force on the end = p · (π/4) d².
  • Resisting force = σl × ring area = σl · π d t.
\[ p\,\frac{\pi}{4}d^2 = \sigma_l\,\pi\,d\,t \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_l = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t} = \frac{\sigma_c}{2} \]

3. Strains. Each point of the wall carries σc and σl at 90° to each other:

\[ \frac{\delta d}{d} = \varepsilon_c = \frac{\sigma_c}{E} - \frac{\mu\,\sigma_l}{E} = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(2-\mu), \qquad \frac{\delta L}{L} = \varepsilon_l = \frac{\sigma_l}{E} - \frac{\mu\,\sigma_c}{E} = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(1-2\mu) \]

4. Volumetric strain. V = (π/4)d²L. The final volume is (π/4)(d + δd)²(L + δL). Expand and ignore the products of small quantities:

\[ \delta V = \frac{\pi}{4}\left(2\,d\,L\,\delta d + d^2\,\delta L\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\delta V}{V} = 2\frac{\delta d}{d} + \frac{\delta L}{L} = 2\varepsilon_c + \varepsilon_l \] \[ \frac{\delta V}{V} = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}\left[2(2-\mu) + (1-2\mu)\right] = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(5-4\mu) \]

5. Joint efficiency. With riveted joints: σc = pd/(2tηl) (longitudinal joint) and σl = pd/(4tηc) (circumferential joint).

6. Thin spherical shell. Any diametral section gives p(π/4)d² = σ(πdt), so

\[ \sigma = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t}, \qquad \frac{\delta d}{d} = \frac{p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(1-\mu), \qquad \frac{\delta V}{V} = 3\frac{\delta d}{d} = \frac{3\,p\,d}{4\,t\,E}(1-\mu) \]

The sphere has half the stress of a cylinder of the same d and t, so it needs half the thickness.

Numericals

Oblique plane / principal stresses: (1) write σx, σy (compression negative) and τxy; (2) find θ from the wording (angle of the plane from the σx plane; check θ = 0 gives σx); (3) σn, τθ, σR, φ; (4) σ1,2, θp, τmax; (5) check with Mohr's circle. Thin vessels: check t/d < 1/20; σc = pd/2t, σl = pd/4t (sphere pd/4t); use ηl with σc and ηc with σl; strains with the μ term; δV = εvV.

N5. A thin cylindrical drum is 800 mm in internal diameter, 3 m long and 10 mm thick. It carries an internal pressure of 2.5 N/mm². Find the hoop and longitudinal stresses and the changes in diameter, length and volume. Take E = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm² and μ = 0.25.
