Refrigeration Cycle

A refrigerator moves heat from a cold space to a warm surrounding, and it needs work to do this. This unit covers the vapour compression cycle that runs almost every fridge and air conditioner, the refrigerants it uses, the absorption alternative, and the uses of refrigeration. The numericals use enthalpies from P-h data, so the method is the same for every refrigerant.

1. Principles of refrigeration

Refrigeration is the process of removing heat from a space or a body so that its temperature stays lower than that of the surroundings. The Clausius statement of the second law says that heat cannot flow from a cold body to a hot body by itself. So we must supply work. A machine that does this is a refrigerator (or a heat pump).

A refrigerator and a heat pump are the same machine. They differ in the purpose:

  • Refrigerator: the useful effect is the heat \( Q_L \) taken from the cold space (food, room, cold store).
  • Heat pump: the useful effect is the heat \( Q_H \) given to the warm space (a room in winter, hot water).

By the first law, for one cycle: \( Q_H = Q_L + W \). The heat rejected is always more than the heat absorbed.

A refrigerant is the working fluid. It evaporates at a low temperature and low pressure, and so absorbs its latent heat from the cold space. It condenses at a higher pressure and rejects the heat to the air or water. The common methods of refrigeration are:

  • vapour compression (fridge, air conditioner, cold store),
  • vapour absorption (uses heat instead of electric work),
  • air cycle (aircraft cabins), thermoelectric (Peltier) and ice or dry-ice cooling.

2. Refrigeration effect and ton of refrigeration

The refrigeration effect (RE) is the heat absorbed by 1 kg of refrigerant in the evaporator. Its unit is kJ/kg. The refrigeration capacity is the heat removed per unit time. It equals the mass flow rate times the RE.

\[ Q_L = \dot m \times RE \qquad (\text{kW}) \]

\( \dot m \) = refrigerant mass flow rate (kg/s), RE = refrigeration effect (kJ/kg).

The capacity is given in tons of refrigeration (TR). One TR is the heat that must be removed in 24 hours to freeze 1 ton (2000 lb = 907 kg) of water at 0 °C into ice at 0 °C. The latent heat of ice is about 335 kJ/kg, so 907 × 335 / 24 = 12 660 kJ/h = 211 kJ/min.

3. Coefficient of Performance (COP)

COP is the ratio of the useful effect to the work supplied. It is not an efficiency, because it can be more than 1.

\[ COP_R = \frac{Q_L}{W} = \frac{RE}{W_{comp}} \qquad COP_{HP} = \frac{Q_H}{W} = \frac{Q_L + W}{W} = COP_R + 1 \]

W = net work supplied to the cycle (kJ/kg or kW). \( COP_{HP} \) is always more than \( COP_R \) by exactly 1.

For the ideal (Carnot) machine working between \( T_L \) and \( T_H \) (absolute temperature, K):

\[ COP_{R,Carnot} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L} \qquad COP_{HP,Carnot} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L} \]

No real machine can have a COP higher than the Carnot value between the same two temperatures. A low \( T_H - T_L \) gives a high COP. So a refrigerator works best with a low condensing temperature and a high evaporating temperature.

Common mistake. Always use kelvin in the Carnot formula (K = °C + 273.15). A COP of 5.8 is normal for a refrigerator; do not call it 580 %.

4. Reversed Carnot cycle

The reversed Carnot cycle is the ideal refrigeration cycle. It is the Carnot heat engine run backwards. It has four reversible processes:

  • 1-2: isentropic compression, the temperature rises from \( T_L \) to \( T_H \). Work is supplied.
  • 2-3: isothermal heat rejection at \( T_H \) (condenser).
  • 3-4: isentropic expansion in an expander (turbine), the temperature falls to \( T_L \). Work is produced.
  • 4-1: isothermal heat absorption at \( T_L \) (evaporator). This is the refrigeration effect.

From the T-s diagram, with \( \Delta s = s_1 - s_4 \): \( Q_L = T_L\,\Delta s \), \( Q_H = T_H\,\Delta s \) and \( W = Q_H - Q_L = (T_H - T_L)\,\Delta s \). So \( COP = T_L/(T_H - T_L) \).

Why it is not practical.

  • Point 1 lies in the wet region. The compressor would have to compress a liquid-vapour mixture ("wet compression"). Liquid droplets damage the valves and cylinder (liquid hammer) and cut the volume of vapour drawn in.
  • The expander must handle a liquid-vapour mixture. It is hard to build, and the work it gives is small.
  • Isothermal heat transfer needs a zero temperature difference, so an infinite surface area or an infinite time. Real heat exchangers need a temperature difference of 5 to 15 K.
  • The cycle is very sensitive to the position of point 1: the dryness fraction must be controlled with great accuracy.

The practical VCR cycle fixes these two problems. It compresses dry vapour and it replaces the expander by a throttle valve. The COP is lower than the Carnot value, but the machine is simple and reliable.

5. Simple vapour compression refrigeration (VCR) cycle

The simple VCR cycle has four components. Fig. 5.3 shows them. Refrigerant flows round the loop in the order 1, 2, 3, 4.

  1. Compressor (1-2): takes in saturated (dry) vapour at the low pressure \( p_e \) and compresses it isentropically to the high pressure \( p_c \). The vapour leaves hot and superheated. This process needs work.
  2. Condenser (2-3): the hot vapour rejects heat to air or water at constant pressure. It cools to the saturation temperature, condenses fully and leaves as saturated liquid (state 3).
  3. Expansion valve (3-4): the liquid is throttled to \( p_e \). The enthalpy is constant, \( h_4 = h_3 \). The temperature drops and some liquid flashes to vapour. State 4 is a wet mixture of low dryness.
  4. Evaporator (4-1): the wet mixture absorbs heat from the cold space at constant pressure and constant temperature, and fully evaporates to saturated vapour (state 1).

Two diagrams describe the cycle. Study both.

The P-h diagram is used for all refrigeration work. Pressure is on the vertical axis (log scale) and enthalpy on the horizontal axis. The reason is simple: every energy quantity of the cycle is a horizontal length. Evaporation and condensation are horizontal lines, throttling is a vertical line, and compression is a line that slopes up to the right.

\[ RE = h_1 - h_4 = h_1 - h_3 \qquad W = h_2 - h_1 \qquad Q_{cond} = h_2 - h_3 \]\[ COP = \frac{h_1 - h_4}{h_2 - h_1} \qquad Q_{cond} = RE + W \]

All h in kJ/kg. \( h_3 = h_4 \) because throttling is an isenthalpic process.

For a plant of capacity \( Q_L \) kW:

\[ \dot m = \frac{Q_L}{h_1 - h_4} \qquad P = \dot m\,(h_2 - h_1) \qquad \dot V_1 = \dot m\, v_1 \]

P = compressor power (kW), \( \dot V_1 \) = volume of vapour drawn in per second (m³/s), \( v_1 \) = specific volume at the suction.

Why a throttle and not an expander? The expansion work of a liquid is very small. An expander for a wet mixture is costly and hard to build. A valve (or a capillary tube) is cheap, has no moving part, and lets us control the flow. The loss is a lower RE.

Memory trick. "Compressor adds, condenser drops, valve flat, evaporator rises." On the P-h chart: the compressor line goes up-right, the condenser line goes left, the valve line goes down, the evaporator line goes right.

6. Effect of subcooling and superheating

Subcooling means cooling the liquid below its saturation temperature at the condenser pressure (state 3'). The liquid enthalpy falls from \( h_3 \) to \( h_{3'} \), so the RE rises by \( h_3 - h_{3'} \). The compressor work does not change, because the compression line 1-2 is the same. So the COP increases. Subcooling also avoids flash gas in the liquid line.

Superheating means heating the vapour above its saturation temperature at the evaporator pressure (state 1'). It protects the compressor from liquid drops (dry compression). Two cases:

  • Useful superheating: the vapour is superheated inside the evaporator while it takes heat from the cold space. The RE increases.
  • Useless superheating: the vapour gains heat in the suction pipe after it has left the cold space. There is no extra RE, but the specific volume rises, so the compressor must handle more volume.

With superheating both RE and work increase. The COP may rise or fall; it depends on the refrigerant. Numerical N6 shows a case where it falls. In practice a liquid-suction heat exchanger is used: the cold suction vapour takes heat from the warm liquid. This gives subcooling of the liquid and superheating of the vapour with no outside heat.

7. Actual VCR cycle

The actual cycle differs from the theoretical cycle of Fig. 5.5. Fig. 5.8 shows the main changes.

  • Pressure drops in the evaporator, condenser, pipes and valves. The compressor must work between a lower suction pressure and a higher discharge pressure.
  • Superheat at the evaporator exit (a few kelvin) so that no liquid enters the compressor.
  • Subcooling at the condenser exit so that only liquid reaches the valve.
  • Non-isentropic compression: friction and heat transfer raise the entropy. The discharge enthalpy is higher than \( h_2 \). The isentropic efficiency is \( \eta_s = (h_{2s} - h_1)/(h_2 - h_1) \), typically 0.7 to 0.85.
  • Finite temperature difference: the evaporator temperature is 5 to 10 K below the cold-space temperature. The condenser temperature is 10 to 15 K above the air or water temperature. This lowers the COP.
  • Heat gain in the suction line, valve leakage and the volumetric efficiency of the compressor (clearance volume).
Real world. A home air conditioner often shows its evaporator at 5 to 8 °C for a room at 24 °C, and its condenser at 45 to 55 °C on a hot day. The big temperature lift is the reason why a split AC has a COP of about 3, much less than the Carnot COP of about 7.

8. Refrigerants and their designation

A refrigerant is the fluid that carries heat in a refrigeration cycle. Refrigerants are named by the letter R and a number. This is the ASHRAE designation.

Rule for R-xyz (methane, ethane and propane series):

  • z = number of fluorine atoms.
  • y = number of hydrogen atoms + 1.
  • x = number of carbon atoms − 1 (omit x if it is zero).
  • Chlorine atoms fill the remaining bonds. A letter (a, b) shows an isomer.

Examples: R-12 = CCl2F2 (C − 1 = 0, H + 1 = 1, F = 2). R-22 = CHClF2 (H + 1 = 2, F = 2). R-134a = C2H2F4 (C − 1 = 1, H + 1 = 3, F = 4). Inorganic refrigerants are 700 plus the molar mass: ammonia (17) is R-717, carbon dioxide (44) is R-744, water (18) is R-718. Hydrocarbons use 290 for propane and 600a for isobutane. The 1000 series (R-1234yf) are unsaturated HFOs.

9. Desirable properties of a refrigerant

Thermodynamic properties

  • Low boiling point at atmospheric pressure, so the evaporator pressure is not below the atmosphere (no air leaks in).
  • Moderate condenser pressure (not too high), so the machine is light and safe.
  • High latent heat of vaporisation: a small mass flow gives the required capacity.
  • High critical temperature: heat rejection is by condensation, not at supercritical state.
  • Low freezing point, and low specific volume of vapour (a small compressor).
  • High COP, and a low compression ratio and low discharge temperature.

Chemical properties

  • Non-toxic, non-flammable and non-explosive.
  • Chemically stable, and no reaction with the lubricating oil or the metals of the plant.
  • Non-corrosive and compatible with seals and hoses.

Physical and other properties

  • Good heat transfer (high thermal conductivity) and low viscosity.
  • Easy leak detection, and no effect on food or people (no smell or colour).
  • Low cost and easy availability.
  • Environmental: zero ODP, low GWP and a short atmospheric life.
No ideal refrigerant. Ammonia has excellent thermodynamic properties but is toxic. Hydrocarbons are efficient and green but flammable. So each use has its own best choice.

10. Eco-friendly refrigerants

Two environmental indices rate a refrigerant:

  • Ozone Depletion Potential (ODP): the damage to the ozone layer compared with R-11 (ODP = 1). Chlorine in CFCs and HCFCs breaks ozone in the stratosphere.
  • Global Warming Potential (GWP): the warming caused by 1 kg of the gas over 100 years compared with 1 kg of CO2 (GWP = 1).

The Montreal Protocol (1987) ordered the phase-out of CFCs (such as R-12) and then HCFCs (such as R-22), because of their ODP. HFCs such as R-134a have zero ODP, but a high GWP. The Kigali Amendment (2016) to the Montreal Protocol orders a gradual phase-down of HFCs. India is a party, and its HFC phase-down starts in the early 2030s.

The eco-friendly choices are the natural refrigerants (ammonia R-717, carbon dioxide R-744, hydrocarbons R-290 and R-600a, water) and the new low-GWP HFOs such as R-1234yf. Each has a drawback: ammonia is toxic, CO2 works at very high pressure (above 100 bar in the gas cooler), hydrocarbons burn and HFOs are costly and mildly flammable.

11. Vapour absorption refrigeration (VAR) system

The VAR system uses heat as the main energy input and not electric work. The commonly used pair is ammonia (refrigerant) and water (absorbent). Ammonia is very soluble in water. In Fig. 5.9 the compressor of the VCR system is replaced by an absorber, a pump and a generator. The condenser, the expansion valve and the evaporator stay the same.

Working

  1. Evaporator: liquid ammonia at low pressure takes heat QE from the cold space and evaporates.
  2. Absorber: the ammonia vapour is absorbed by the weak (ammonia-poor) water solution. Absorption gives out heat QA, so the absorber is cooled by water. A cold solution absorbs more ammonia.
  3. Pump: the strong (ammonia-rich) solution is pumped to the condenser pressure. The pump work is very small because it handles liquid, not vapour.
  4. Generator: heat QG (steam, hot gas, solar or waste heat) boils the ammonia out of the solution. Some water vapour also leaves. A rectifier (analyser) removes the water so that nearly pure ammonia goes to the condenser.
  5. Condenser: the ammonia vapour condenses at high pressure and rejects heat QC.
  6. Expansion valve: the liquid ammonia is throttled to the evaporator pressure.
  7. The hot weak solution returns from the generator through a pressure-reducing valve to the absorber. A heat exchanger between the strong and weak solutions saves heat.
\[ COP_{VAR} = \frac{Q_E}{Q_G + W_p} \approx \frac{Q_E}{Q_G} \]

\( W_p \) = pump work, which is very small. A simple ammonia-water system has a COP of about 0.5 to 0.8.

12. Domestic refrigerator

A domestic refrigerator is a small VCR system. Fig. 5.10 shows its parts.

  • Compressor: a sealed (hermetic) unit at the bottom back. The motor and the compressor are in one steel shell.
  • Condenser: a coil of tube on the back (or in the side walls). It is cooled by natural air flow.
  • Filter-drier: removes dirt and moisture from the liquid.
  • Capillary tube: a long, thin copper tube. It is the throttling device. The pressure falls because of friction in the thin tube.
  • Evaporator: the coil in the freezer box (about −18 °C). Cold air falls from it and cools the fresh-food space (3 to 5 °C).
  • Thermostat: switches the compressor on and off to keep the set temperature. Insulation (polyurethane foam) and a door gasket reduce heat gain.

The refrigerant is R-134a in older units and R-600a (isobutane) in new units. Frost-free models add a fan that blows air over the evaporator and an electric heater that melts the frost. The compressor runs in short cycles (on-off control) and takes about 100 to 200 W.

13. Industrial applications of refrigeration

  • Cold storage: fruits and vegetables at 0 to 4 °C; frozen food, meat and fish at −18 to −25 °C. Milk chilling in dairies.
  • Ice plant: ammonia evaporator coils cool brine (salt water) to about −10 °C. Water cans in the brine freeze into ice blocks.
  • Air conditioning: comfort cooling of homes, offices, cars and trains; process cooling in textile, printing and electronic factories.
  • Chemical and process industries: gas liquefaction, separation of gases, cooling of reactors, dehydration.
  • Medical: storage of vaccines, blood and medicines in cold chain.
  • Others: food freezing, refrigerated trucks and ships, ice skating rinks, cooling of concrete in dams, mining of deep mines.
Large plants. Large cold stores and ice plants use ammonia (R-717) because of its high COP and low cost; the plant is kept away from people because ammonia is toxic. Small units use R-134a, R-600a or R-290.

Q1. Explain the reversed Carnot refrigeration cycle with a schematic and a T-s diagram. Derive its COP and say why it is not used in practice.

Syllabus topic

Schematic + rectangle T-s (counter-clockwise), COP = TL/(TH − TL), then 3 or 4 reasons for the practical limits.

Model answer

Definition. The reversed Carnot cycle is the Carnot heat-engine cycle run in the opposite direction. It takes heat QL from a low-temperature body at TL and rejects heat QH to a high-temperature body at TH, with work W supplied. It is the ideal cycle of a refrigerator.

Processes.

  • 1-2: isentropic compression (work in); T rises from TL to TH.
  • 2-3: isothermal heat rejection at TH in the condenser.
  • 3-4: isentropic expansion in the expander (work out); T falls to TL.
  • 4-1: isothermal heat absorption at TL in the evaporator.

COP. From the T-s diagram: \( Q_L = T_L (s_1 - s_4) \), \( Q_H = T_H (s_1 - s_4) \). Net work \( W = Q_H - Q_L = (T_H - T_L)(s_1 - s_4) \).

\[ COP_{R} = \frac{Q_L}{W} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L} \]

This is the maximum COP possible between the two temperatures. It rises when TL is high and TH is low.

Why it is not practical.

  • Compression starts in the wet region (state 1). Wet compression damages the compressor and gives low volumetric efficiency.
  • The expander must work on a liquid-vapour mixture. It is hard to make, and its small work does not repay the cost.
  • Isothermal heat transfer needs zero temperature difference, which means an infinite area. Real exchangers need 5 to 15 K.
  • The cycle is very sensitive to the dryness fraction at state 1.

Conclusion. It is used only as a standard of comparison. The practical cycle is the simple VCR cycle with dry compression and a throttle valve.

Q2. With a neat sketch, explain the simple vapour compression refrigeration cycle. Show it on T-s and P-h diagrams and derive the expression for COP. State how the actual cycle differs from it.

Syllabus topic

Schematic with four components, then P-h with RE and W marked, COP formula, a short list of deviations. The P-h diagram is the key figure.

Model answer

Introduction. The vapour compression cycle uses a refrigerant that changes phase in the evaporator and the condenser. The cycle has four components and four processes.

Processes.

  • 1-2 compression in the compressor: isentropic. Saturated vapour at pe becomes superheated vapour at pc. Work W is added.
  • 2-3 condensation in the condenser: constant pressure. The vapour cools to saturation and condenses to saturated liquid. Heat is rejected.
  • 3-4 throttling in the expansion valve: h3 = h4. Pressure and temperature fall. A little liquid flashes to vapour.
  • 4-1 evaporation in the evaporator: constant pressure and temperature. The refrigerant takes heat from the cold space.

Performance. Apply the steady-flow energy equation to each component (per kg):

  • Refrigeration effect: \( RE = h_1 - h_4 \)
  • Compressor work: \( W = h_2 - h_1 \)
  • Heat rejected: \( Q_c = h_2 - h_3 = RE + W \)
\[ COP = \frac{h_1 - h_4}{h_2 - h_1} \]

For capacity QL, the mass flow is \( \dot m = Q_L/(h_1 - h_4) \) and the power is \( P = \dot m (h_2 - h_1) \).

Actual cycle. The actual cycle differs from the above because of:

  • pressure drops in the evaporator, condenser and pipes;
  • superheat at the evaporator exit and subcooling at the condenser exit;
  • non-isentropic compression (friction, heat transfer), with \( \eta_s \) = 0.7 to 0.85;
  • finite temperature differences in the heat exchangers: Te is lower and Tc is higher than the ideal values, so the COP is lower.

Q3. Explain, with P-h diagrams, the effect of (a) subcooling of the liquid and (b) superheating of the vapour on the performance of a VCR cycle.

Syllabus topic

Two P-h figures with 3' and 1' marked. Say what changes: RE, work, COP. Subcooling always helps; superheating depends.

Model answer

(a) Subcooling. The liquid leaves the condenser below its saturation temperature (state 3'). The condenser pressure is the same. The liquid enthalpy falls to h3' and the valve line moves to the left.

  • RE increases from \( h_1 - h_3 \) to \( h_1 - h_{3'} \). The gain is \( h_3 - h_{3'} \).
  • The compression line 1-2 does not change, so the work does not change.
  • The COP increases. The mass flow for a given capacity falls, so the compressor can be smaller.
  • Subcooling stops flash gas in the liquid line. It is done with extra condenser tubes, cold water or a liquid-suction heat exchanger.

(b) Superheating. The vapour is heated above saturation at the evaporator pressure (state 1'). The compression line moves to the right (1'-2').

  • The vapour is dry when it enters the compressor, so liquid hammer is avoided.
  • If the superheating is useful (inside the evaporator, taking heat from the cold space), RE rises from \( h_1 - h_4 \) to \( h_{1'} - h_4 \).
  • The work rises from \( h_2 - h_1 \) to \( h_{2'} - h_{1'} \), because the specific volume is larger.
  • The COP may rise or fall; it depends on the refrigerant. For the data of numerical N6 it falls.
  • If the superheating is useless (heat gain in the suction pipe), there is no gain in RE, and the COP falls.

Conclusion. Subcool the liquid; superheat only a little, to protect the compressor. A liquid-suction heat exchanger does both.

Q4. What is a refrigerant? Explain the designation of refrigerants. List the desirable properties of a refrigerant. Discuss eco-friendly refrigerants with reference to ODP, GWP and the Montreal and Kigali protocols.

Syllabus topic

Definition; the R-xyz rule with 3 examples; properties in three groups; ODP and GWP definitions; Montreal 1987, Kigali 2016; natural refrigerants.

Model answer

Definition. A refrigerant is the working fluid of a refrigeration system. It absorbs heat at a low temperature by evaporation and rejects it at a higher temperature by condensation.

Designation (ASHRAE). R-xyz: z = number of F atoms; y = number of H atoms + 1; x = number of C atoms − 1 (omitted if 0); the rest of the bonds are Cl. Examples: R-12 = CCl2F2, R-22 = CHClF2, R-134a = C2H2F4. Inorganic fluids: 700 + molar mass (R-717 ammonia, R-744 CO2). Hydrocarbons: R-290 propane, R-600a isobutane. R-1234yf is an HFO.

Desirable properties.

  • Thermodynamic: low boiling point, high latent heat, moderate pressures (above atmosphere in the evaporator), high critical temperature, low freezing point, low vapour specific volume, high COP.
  • Chemical: non-toxic, non-flammable, stable, no reaction with oil or metals, non-corrosive.
  • Physical: good heat transfer, low viscosity, easy leak detection, low cost.
  • Environmental: zero ODP, low GWP, short atmospheric life.

Eco-friendly refrigerants. ODP is the ozone damage relative to R-11. GWP is the 100-year warming relative to CO2.

  • Montreal Protocol (1987): phase-out of CFCs and HCFCs to protect the ozone layer.
  • Kigali Amendment (2016): phase-down of HFCs because of their high GWP.
  • The eco-friendly choices are natural refrigerants (NH3, CO2, hydrocarbons) and HFOs. Their limits: ammonia is toxic, CO2 needs very high pressure, hydrocarbons burn.

Q5. With a neat sketch, explain the working of a vapour absorption refrigeration system. Compare it with the vapour compression system.

Syllabus topic

Sketch with absorber, pump, generator, condenser, valve, evaporator; working in order; COP formula; a comparison table of 5 or 6 points.

Model answer

Principle. The VAR system replaces the mechanical compressor by an absorber, a pump and a generator. It uses a heat source and not work. The working pair is ammonia (refrigerant) and water (absorbent).

Working.

  • Evaporator: liquid NH3 at low pressure absorbs QE from the cold space and evaporates.
  • Absorber: the NH3 vapour dissolves in the weak solution; heat QA is removed by cooling water.
  • Pump: raises the pressure of the strong solution to the condenser pressure (small work).
  • Generator: heat QG boils NH3 out of the solution. A rectifier removes water vapour.
  • Condenser: NH3 vapour condenses at high pressure, rejecting QC.
  • Expansion valve: the liquid NH3 is throttled to the evaporator pressure.
  • The weak solution returns to the absorber through a pressure-reducing valve.

Q6. Describe a domestic refrigerator with a neat sketch. List the industrial applications of refrigeration.

Syllabus topic

Sketch with freezer evaporator, back condenser, compressor, capillary tube; the role of each; 6 industrial uses with temperatures.

Model answer

Domestic refrigerator. It is a small vapour compression system. A hermetic compressor draws vapour from the evaporator in the freezer and delivers it to the air-cooled condenser at the back. The liquid passes through a filter-drier and a capillary tube. The capillary tube throttles it to the evaporator pressure. The cold refrigerant in the freezer coil cools the cabinet. A thermostat switches the compressor on and off.

  • Compressor: sealed motor-compressor; raises the pressure of the vapour.
  • Condenser: tube coil on the back; heat goes to room air.
  • Filter-drier: removes dirt and moisture.
  • Capillary tube: long, thin tube; throttling device.
  • Evaporator: freezer coil at about −18 °C; the fresh-food space is at 3 to 5 °C.
  • Refrigerant: R-134a or R-600a. Polyurethane foam insulation and a door gasket reduce heat gain.

Industrial applications.

  • Cold storage of fruit and vegetables (0 to 4 °C) and frozen food (−18 to −25 °C); milk chilling.
  • Ice plant: brine at about −10 °C freezes water in cans.
  • Air conditioning for comfort and for process control.
  • Chemical industry: gas liquefaction and separation, cooling of reactors.
  • Medical cold chain: vaccines, blood and medicines.
  • Refrigerated transport, ice rinks and cooling of concrete in dams.

Numericals

Method. (1) Read the state points: h1 (leaving evaporator), h2 (leaving compressor), h3 (leaving condenser). Set h4 = h3. (2) RE = h1 − h4. (3) W = h2 − h1. (4) COP = RE / W. (5) Mass flow = QL / RE, with 1 TR = 3.5167 kW. (6) Power = m × W. Heat rejected = m (h2 − h3). (7) Check: heat rejected = QL + power. All enthalpies are given in the problems, so no table is needed. For Carnot problems use kelvin.

Data note. The enthalpy values in these problems are given in the statement, like the values you read from the refrigerant P-h chart in the exam. They are typical in size but they are not table values of one real refrigerant.

N1. A simple VCR plant of 5 TR capacity works between an evaporator and a condenser. The enthalpy of the refrigerant is 395 kJ/kg at the compressor inlet (saturated vapour), 432 kJ/kg at the compressor outlet (after isentropic compression) and 243 kJ/kg at the condenser outlet (saturated liquid). Find the refrigeration effect, the compressor work, the COP, the mass flow rate, the compressor power and the heat rejected in the condenser. Take 1 TR = 3.5167 kW.

Syllabus topic

  1. Throttling is isenthalpic: \( h_4 = h_3 = 243 \) kJ/kg.
  2. Refrigeration effect: \( RE = h_1 - h_4 = 395 - 243 = 152 \) kJ/kg.
  3. Compressor work: \( W = h_2 - h_1 = 432 - 395 = 37 \) kJ/kg.
  4. \( COP = RE/W = 152/37 = 4.11 \).
  5. Capacity: \( Q_L = 5 \times 3.5167 = 17.58 \) kW.
  6. Mass flow: \( \dot m = Q_L/RE = 17.58/152 = 0.1157 \) kg/s.
  7. Power: \( P = \dot m W = 0.1157 \times 37 = 4.28 \) kW.
  8. Heat rejected: \( Q_c = \dot m (h_2 - h_3) = 0.1157 \times 189 = 21.86 \) kW. Check: \( Q_L + P = 17.58 + 4.28 = 21.86 \) kW.

Answer: RE = 152 kJ/kg, W = 37 kJ/kg, COP = 4.11, m = 0.116 kg/s, P = 4.28 kW, Qc = 21.9 kW

N2. An ammonia refrigeration plant has a refrigerating capacity of 40 kW. The enthalpies are: leaving the evaporator 1430 kJ/kg, leaving the compressor 1710 kJ/kg, leaving the condenser (saturated liquid) 370 kJ/kg. Find the mass flow rate of ammonia, the compressor power, the COP and the capacity in TR.

Syllabus topic

  1. \( h_4 = h_3 = 370 \) kJ/kg. \( RE = 1430 - 370 = 1060 \) kJ/kg.
  2. \( W = 1710 - 1430 = 280 \) kJ/kg.
  3. \( COP = 1060/280 = 3.79 \).
  4. \( \dot m = 40/1060 = 0.0377 \) kg/s.
  5. \( P = 0.03774 \times 280 = 10.57 \) kW. Heat rejected \( = 0.03774 \times (1710 - 370) = 50.57 \) kW.
  6. Capacity in TR: \( 40/3.5167 = 11.37 \) TR (with 3.5 kW per TR the answer is 11.43 TR).

Answer: m = 0.0377 kg/s, P = 10.57 kW, COP = 3.79, capacity = 11.37 TR

N3. A reversed Carnot refrigerator works between a cold space at −10 °C and surroundings at 35 °C. Find its COP. Find the power needed for a capacity of 3 TR and the heat rejected. What would be the COP of a Carnot heat pump between the same temperatures? Take 1 TR = 3.5167 kW.

Syllabus topic

  1. \( T_L = -10 + 273.15 = 263.15 \) K, \( T_H = 35 + 273.15 = 308.15 \) K.
  2. \( COP_R = T_L/(T_H - T_L) = 263.15/45 = 5.85 \).
  3. \( Q_L = 3 \times 3.5167 = 10.55 \) kW.
  4. Power: \( W = Q_L/COP_R = 10.55/5.848 = 1.80 \) kW.
  5. Heat rejected: \( Q_H = Q_L + W = 10.55 + 1.80 = 12.35 \) kW.
  6. Heat pump: \( COP_{HP} = T_H/(T_H - T_L) = 308.15/45 = 6.85 \), which equals \( COP_R + 1 \).

Answer: COPR = 5.85, W = 1.80 kW, QH = 12.35 kW, COPHP = 6.85

N4. A heat pump uses the VCR cycle to give hot water at 45 °C from outside air at 2 °C. The enthalpies are 400 kJ/kg (compressor inlet), 438 kJ/kg (compressor outlet) and 250 kJ/kg (condenser outlet). The heat supply needed is 12 kW. Find the COP of the heat pump, the compressor power and the refrigerant flow rate. Compare the COP with the Carnot value between 2 °C and 45 °C.

Syllabus topic

  1. Useful effect (heat to the water): \( q_H = h_2 - h_3 = 438 - 250 = 188 \) kJ/kg.
  2. Work: \( W = h_2 - h_1 = 438 - 400 = 38 \) kJ/kg.
  3. \( COP_{HP} = 188/38 = 4.95 \). (Check: \( RE = 150 \), \( COP_R = 3.947 \), plus 1 gives 4.947.)
  4. Power: \( P = Q_H/COP_{HP} = 12/4.947 = 2.43 \) kW.
  5. Flow rate: \( \dot m = 12/188 = 0.0638 \) kg/s.
  6. Carnot: \( COP_{HP,Carnot} = 318.15/(318.15 - 275.15) = 318.15/43 = 7.40 \). The ratio is \( 4.947/7.399 = 0.669 \), about 67 %.

Answer: COPHP = 4.95, P = 2.43 kW, m = 0.0638 kg/s; the Carnot COP is 7.40, so the cycle reaches 67 % of it

N5. A VCR plant has the enthalpies h1 = 398 kJ/kg and h2 = 436 kJ/kg. The liquid leaves the condenser as saturated liquid with h3 = 244 kJ/kg. If the liquid is subcooled so that its enthalpy is 228 kJ/kg, find the COP before and after subcooling, and the percentage gain. For a capacity of 10 kW, find the compressor power in both cases.

Syllabus topic

  1. Work (same in both cases): \( W = 436 - 398 = 38 \) kJ/kg.
  2. Without subcooling: \( RE = 398 - 244 = 154 \) kJ/kg, so \( COP = 154/38 = 4.05 \).
  3. With subcooling: \( h_{4'} = h_{3'} = 228 \) kJ/kg, \( RE = 398 - 228 = 170 \) kJ/kg, so \( COP = 170/38 = 4.47 \).
  4. Gain: \( (4.474 - 4.053)/4.053 = 10.4 \) %.
  5. Mass flow for 10 kW: \( 10/154 = 0.0649 \) kg/s without subcooling and \( 10/170 = 0.0588 \) kg/s with it.
  6. Power \( = Q_L/COP \): \( 10/4.053 = 2.47 \) kW before and \( 10/4.474 = 2.24 \) kW after.

Answer: COP = 4.05 before and 4.47 after (+10.4 %); power = 2.47 kW and 2.24 kW

N6. A VCR cycle has h1 = 398 kJ/kg (saturated vapour), h2 = 436 kJ/kg and h3 = h4 = 244 kJ/kg. If the vapour is superheated by 5 K in the evaporator, the enthalpies change to h1' = 403 kJ/kg and h2' = 444 kJ/kg. Find the COP in both cases and the change due to superheating.

Syllabus topic

  1. Saturated cycle: \( RE = 398 - 244 = 154 \), \( W = 436 - 398 = 38 \), \( COP = 4.05 \).
  2. Superheated cycle: \( RE = h_{1'} - h_4 = 403 - 244 = 159 \) kJ/kg (useful superheat).
  3. \( W = h_{2'} - h_{1'} = 444 - 403 = 41 \) kJ/kg.
  4. \( COP = 159/41 = 3.88 \).
  5. Change: \( (3.878 - 4.053)/4.053 = -4.3 \) %. RE rises by 3.2 %, but the work rises by 7.9 %.

Answer: COP = 4.05 without superheat and 3.88 with superheat (about -4.3 %)

N7. A refrigeration plant of 8 TR capacity has h1 = 397 kJ/kg and a specific volume at the compressor inlet of 0.0653 m³/kg. The enthalpy after isentropic compression is 431 kJ/kg. The isentropic efficiency of the compressor is 0.80. The liquid leaves the condenser with h3 = 242 kJ/kg. Find the actual discharge enthalpy, the actual and ideal COP, the mass flow rate, the compressor power, the volume of vapour drawn per hour and the heat rejected in the condenser. Take 1 TR = 3.5167 kW.

Syllabus topic

  1. Isentropic work: \( W_s = 431 - 397 = 34 \) kJ/kg. Actual work: \( W = W_s/\eta_s = 34/0.80 = 42.5 \) kJ/kg.
  2. Actual discharge enthalpy: \( h_2 = 397 + 42.5 = 439.5 \) kJ/kg.
  3. \( RE = 397 - 242 = 155 \) kJ/kg. Ideal \( COP = 155/34 = 4.56 \). Actual \( COP = 155/42.5 = 3.65 \).
  4. Capacity: \( Q_L = 8 \times 3.5167 = 28.13 \) kW. Mass flow: \( \dot m = 28.13/155 = 0.1815 \) kg/s.
  5. Power: \( P = \dot m W = 0.1815 \times 42.5 = 7.71 \) kW.
  6. Volume at suction: \( \dot V_1 = \dot m v_1 = 0.1815 \times 0.0653 = 0.01185 \) m³/s \( = 42.7 \) m³/h.
  7. Heat rejected: \( Q_c = \dot m (h_2 - h_3) = 0.1815 \times (439.5 - 242) = 35.85 \) kW. Check: \( 28.13 + 7.71 = 35.84 \) kW.

Answer: h2 = 439.5 kJ/kg, COP = 3.65 (ideal 4.56), m = 0.182 kg/s, P = 7.71 kW, V = 42.7 m³/h, Qc = 35.8 kW
