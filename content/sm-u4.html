Strength of Materials · Unit 4

Torsion, Deflection of Beams and Columns

This unit covers three ways a member can be loaded or fail besides direct stress and bending stress. A shaft twists under torque, a beam deflects under its load, and a slender column buckles sideways under compression. You learn one main equation for each (T/J = τ/R = Gθ/L, EI·d²y/dx² = M and Euler's load) and use it to size shafts, check beam stiffness and find safe column loads.

Concepts

The other unit has deflection and columns: derive EI·d²y/dx² = M or a standard case (cantilever, simply supported beam), a slope/deflection numerical, Euler's assumptions and an Euler derivation (both ends hinged, or one end fixed and the other free), and a Euler/Rankine load numerical. The 2026 syllabus puts all three topics in Unit 4, so learn all of them.

1. Torsion and pure torsion

A shaft is a rotating member, usually of circular section, that transmits power from one machine part to another: an engine to a gearbox, a motor to a pump, a gearbox to a wheel. The power is carried as a torque (twisting moment) T.

When a torque acts on a shaft, each cross-section turns a little relative to the next one. The shaft is in torsion. Shear stresses act on every cross-section.

  • Torque (twisting moment) T: the moment of a force about the axis of the shaft. T = force × perpendicular distance from the axis (N·mm or N·m).
  • Pure torsion: a member is in pure torsion when only a torque acts on it, with no bending moment, axial force or shear force. Every section then carries the same torque and only shear stress.
  • Angle of twist θ: the angle (in radian) through which one end section of the shaft turns relative to the other end.
  • Shear strain φ: the angle through which a straight line on the surface, parallel to the axis, turns after the twist (Fig. 4.1).

2. Assumptions of the torsion theory

The torsion equation is exact only when these assumptions hold. Learn them; the examiner gives 2–3 marks for them in every derivation.

  1. The material of the shaft is homogeneous, isotropic and obeys Hooke's law (stress stays within the elastic limit).
  2. The shaft is straight and of uniform circular section (solid or hollow).
  3. The shaft is in pure torsion: the torque acts in a plane perpendicular to the axis.
  4. Plane cross-sections stay plane after twisting (no warping), and every radius stays a straight line.
  5. Each cross-section rotates as a rigid disc; the angle of twist is small.
  6. The twist is uniform along the length: every section turns by the same angle per unit length.

3. The torsion equation

Look at Fig. 4.1. A shaft of length L and radius R is fixed at one end. A torque T at the other end turns the end section through θ. A line AB on the surface moves to AB′. The arc BB′ is the same whether you measure it from the side (angle φ at A) or from the end (angle θ at O):

\[ \text{arc } BB' = L\,\varphi = R\,\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad \varphi = \frac{R\,\theta}{L} \]

φ is small, so tan φ ≈ φ (radian).

Shear strain φ = τ/G (Hooke's law in shear). So the shear stress at the surface is τ = GRθ/L. A layer at radius r has strain rθ/L, so

\[ \frac{\tau}{R} = \frac{q}{r} = \frac{G\,\theta}{L} \]

q = shear stress at radius r. Shear stress is proportional to the radius: zero at the centre, maximum at the outer surface (Fig. 4.2).

Now find the torque. Take a thin ring of radius r and width dr (Fig. 4.2a). Its area is dA = 2πr·dr. The shear force on it is q·dA, and its moment about the axis is q·dA·r. Add the moments of all rings:

\[ T = \int_0^R q\,(2\pi r\,dr)\,r = \frac{\tau}{R}\int_0^R 2\pi r^3\,dr = \frac{\tau}{R}\,J \]

J = ∫ r² dA = polar moment of inertia of the section (mm⁴).

Put the two results together. This is the torsion equation:

\[ \frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{R} = \frac{G\,\theta}{L} \]

T = torque (N·mm), J = polar moment of inertia (mm⁴), τ = maximum shear stress at radius R (N/mm²), R = outer radius (mm), G = modulus of rigidity (N/mm²), θ = angle of twist (radian), L = length (mm).

Memory trick. Read it as "T-J, tau-R, G-theta-L". The equation looks like the bending equation M/I = σ/y = E/R with M → T, I → J, σ → τ, y → R and E → G.
Common mistake. θ must be in radian in Gθ/L. If the question gives the angle in degrees, multiply by π/180 (1° = 0.01745 rad). Keep T in N·mm when R is in mm.

4. Polar moment of inertia, polar modulus and rigidity

The polar moment of inertia J is the second moment of area of the section about the axis of the shaft (perpendicular to the section). By the perpendicular axis theorem, J = Ixx + Iyy.

\[ J_{\text{solid}} = \frac{\pi}{32}\,D^4 \qquad J_{\text{hollow}} = \frac{\pi}{32}\left(D_o^4 - D_i^4\right) \]

D = diameter of a solid shaft, Do and Di = outer and inner diameters of a hollow shaft (mm).

The polar modulus (torsional section modulus) Zp = J/R. From T/J = τ/R we get T = τ·Zp. So Zp measures the torsional strength of the section: the torque it carries for a given allowable stress.

\[ T = \frac{\pi}{16}\,\tau\,D^3 \;\;\text{(solid)} \qquad T = \frac{\pi}{16}\,\tau\,\frac{D_o^4 - D_i^4}{D_o} \;\;\text{(hollow)} \]

These two "strength" formulas are used in every design numerical.

  • Torsional rigidity (stiffness) = GJ (N·mm²). From T = GJθ/L, it is the torque needed to produce a unit angle of twist in a unit length. A large GJ means a stiff shaft.
  • Torsional stiffness of a shaft of length L: k = T/θ = GJ/L (N·mm/rad).
  • Torsional flexibility = L/(GJ): the angle of twist produced by a unit torque. It is the reciprocal of torsional stiffness.
  • Torsional strength: the maximum torque the shaft can carry without the shear stress exceeding the allowable value: T = τ·J/R.

5. Power transmitted by a shaft

A torque T that turns at N revolutions per minute does work 2πT in each revolution. The work per second is the power:

\[ P = \frac{2\pi N T}{60} \]

P = power (W), N = speed (rpm), T = mean torque (N·m). With P in kW, T = 60 000 P / (2πN) gives T in N·m. Multiply by 1000 to get N·mm.

The torque of an engine or a motor is not constant. Questions often say "the maximum torque is 20 % more than the mean torque". Then Tmax = 1.2 Tmean. Design the shaft for Tmax.

Design for two conditions. A shaft must be both strong and stiff:

  • Strength: τ ≤ allowable shear stress → \( D = \left(\frac{16\,T}{\pi\,\tau}\right)^{1/3} \).
  • Stiffness: θ ≤ allowable twist (e.g. 1° in 1 m) → \( J = \frac{T\,L}{G\,\theta} \), then \( D = \left(\frac{32\,J}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} \).
  • Take the larger of the two diameters; it satisfies both conditions. Round up to the next standard size.

Quick example. A shaft transmits 50 kW at 1000 rpm. Tmean = 60 000 × 50/(2π × 1000) = 477.5 N·m. With τ = 50 N/mm², D = (16 × 477 500/(π × 50))1/3 = 36.5 mm.

6. Solid and hollow shafts

In a solid shaft the material near the centre carries very low stress (Fig. 4.2b). It adds weight but carries little torque. A hollow shaft removes this lightly stressed core. All its material works at a stress near τmax.

Advantages of a hollow shaft:

  • Higher strength-to-weight ratio and higher torsional stiffness for the same weight.
  • Less material and less rotating mass; this is important in aircraft, automobile propeller shafts and ship shafts.
  • The bore can carry oil, cooling water or wiring.

Disadvantages: a hollow shaft has a larger outside diameter for the same torque, and it costs more to make. Small shafts are therefore usually solid.

Real world. A car's propeller shaft, a helicopter rotor shaft and the main shaft of a large turbine are hollow tubes. A bicycle frame tube and a scaffold pipe use the same idea: material far from the centre gives stiffness at low weight.

8. Deflection of beams: the elastic curve

A beam bends under load. The neutral axis, which was straight, becomes a curve. This curve is the elastic curve (deflection curve) of the beam (Fig. 4.4).

  • Deflection y: the vertical displacement of a point on the neutral axis from its original position (mm).
  • Slope θ: the angle of the tangent to the elastic curve with the original axis. For small deflections θ ≈ tan θ = dy/dx (radian).
  • Radius of curvature R: the radius of the circle that fits the elastic curve at the point.

Why calculate deflection? A beam can be strong enough and still be useless if it sags too much. Floors crack plaster, machine shafts misalign gears and bearings, and lathe beds lose accuracy. Design codes therefore limit the deflection to a small fraction of the span.

9. The differential equation EI·d²y/dx² = M

From the bending equation (Unit 3), M/I = E/R, so the curvature is

\[ \frac{1}{R} = \frac{M}{E\,I} \]

For the short length ds in Fig. 4.4, ds = R·dθ. The deflection is small, so ds ≈ dx and θ ≈ dy/dx. Then

\[ \frac{1}{R} = \frac{d\theta}{ds} \approx \frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \]

Equate the two expressions for 1/R. This is the Euler–Bernoulli equation of the elastic curve:

\[ E\,I\,\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = M \]

E = Young's modulus (N/mm²), I = moment of inertia about the neutral axis (mm⁴), M = bending moment at the section x (N·mm). EI is the flexural rigidity of the beam.

Integrate once and twice:

\[ E\,I\,\frac{dy}{dx} = \int M\,dx + C_1 \qquad E\,I\,y = \iint M\,dx\,dx + C_1x + C_2 \]

The first gives the slope, the second the deflection. C₁ and C₂ come from the boundary conditions.

The relation between slope, deflection and radius of curvature (asked as a short question):

  • Deflection = y; slope = dy/dx; curvature 1/R = d²y/dx² = M/EI.
  • Further, shear force F = dM/dx = EI·d³y/dx³ and load intensity w = dF/dx = EI·d⁴y/dx⁴.
Assumptions. The material obeys Hooke's law; E is constant; the beam is initially straight with a uniform section; deflection is small (so 1/R ≈ d²y/dx²); deflection due to shear force is neglected; plane sections remain plane.

10. Sign convention

  • x is measured from the left end (or from the fixed end of a cantilever), positive to the right.
  • y is positive upward. A downward deflection comes out negative; quote its size in the answer, "deflection = 3.08 mm (downward)".
  • Sagging bending moment is positive; it makes the beam concave up, so d²y/dx² > 0. Hogging moment is negative.
  • Slope is positive when the curve rises to the right (anticlockwise rotation).
Common mistake. A cantilever has a hogging moment, so M is negative: for a load W at the free end, M = −W(L − x) with x from the fixed end. If you drop the minus sign, every slope and deflection gets the wrong sign.

11. Double integration method: standard cases

Steps. (1) Write M at a section x. (2) Put it in EI·d²y/dx² = M. (3) Integrate twice. (4) Use boundary conditions to find C₁ and C₂. (5) Put in x to get slope and deflection where needed.

Boundary conditions to remember:

  • Fixed end: y = 0 and dy/dx = 0.
  • Simple support (pin or roller): y = 0.
  • Symmetrical loading on a simply supported beam: dy/dx = 0 at mid-span; the maximum deflection is at mid-span.
  • Maximum deflection is where dy/dx = 0 (or at the free end of a cantilever).

Case 1: Cantilever, point load W at the free end (Fig. 4.6). With x from the fixed end A, M = −W(L − x).

\[ EI\frac{dy}{dx} = -W\left(Lx - \frac{x^2}{2}\right) + C_1, \qquad EI\,y = -W\left(\frac{Lx^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{6}\right) + C_1x + C_2 \]

At x = 0: dy/dx = 0 → C₁ = 0, and y = 0 → C₂ = 0. At the free end x = L:

\[ \theta_B = -\frac{W L^2}{2EI} \qquad y_B = -\frac{W L^3}{3EI} \]

Negative: the free end slopes down and moves down.

Case 2: Cantilever, UDL w over the whole length. M = −w(L − x)²/2. Integrating with the same boundary conditions gives θB = −wL³/(6EI) and yB = −wL⁴/(8EI).

Case 3: Simply supported, central point load W. RA = RB = W/2. For 0 ≤ x ≤ L/2, M = Wx/2. With y = 0 at x = 0 and dy/dx = 0 at x = L/2: C₁ = −WL²/16, C₂ = 0. So θA = −WL²/(16EI) and yC = −WL³/(48EI).

Case 4: Simply supported, UDL w over the whole span. RA = wL/2 and M = wLx/2 − wx²/2. With y = 0 at x = 0 and x = L: C₁ = −wL³/24, C₂ = 0. So θA = −wL³/(24EI) and yC = −5wL⁴/(384EI).

12. Macaulay's method

With several point loads, the bending moment has a different expression in each segment. Plain double integration then needs one equation per segment and two constants per segment: four or six constants. Macaulay's method writes one equation for the whole beam and keeps only two constants.

Take the section X–X in the last segment (Fig. 4.8). Write M with the distances to the loads in square brackets:

\[ EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = R_A\,x \;-\; W_1[x-a] \;-\; W_2[x-b] \]
\[ EI\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{R_A x^2}{2} - \frac{W_1[x-a]^2}{2} - \frac{W_2[x-b]^2}{2} + C_1 \]
\[ EI\,y = \frac{R_A x^3}{6} - \frac{W_1[x-a]^3}{6} - \frac{W_2[x-b]^3}{6} + C_1x + C_2 \]

Rules of Macaulay's method:

  • Always measure x from the same left end, and take the section in the last segment.
  • Integrate a bracket as a whole: ∫[x − a] dx = [x − a]²/2. Do not expand the bracket.
  • When you put in a value of x, ignore every bracket that becomes negative (zero or negative brackets are dropped). This makes one equation valid for every segment.
  • Write C₁ and C₂ only after the first term (in the first segment), so that they apply to the whole beam.
  • For a UDL that stops before the end, extend it to the end and add an equal upward UDL to cancel the extra part.
  • For maximum deflection, guess the segment, set dy/dx = 0, solve for x and check that x lies in that segment.

Worked examples: N7 (one eccentric load) and N8 (two loads, with the maximum deflection between the loads).

How marks are given. Reactions (1–2 marks), the Macaulay equation (2), integration with constants (2), boundary conditions and constants (2), the required slope/deflection with units (2–3). Always state "y negative means downward".

13. Columns and struts: short and long columns

A column is a vertical member that carries an axial compressive load. A strut is a compression member in any other direction (for example in a truss or a link). A short, thick column fails by crushing. A long, slender column fails by buckling: it bends sideways and collapses at a load far below the crushing load.

  • Radius of gyration k = √(I/A). Use the least I (and least k): the column buckles about the axis of least resistance.
  • Slenderness ratio λ = Le/k, where Le is the effective length.
  • Crippling (buckling, critical) load Pcr: the load at which the column just starts to buckle.
  • Crushing load Pc = σc·A, where σc = crushing stress of the material.

Two differences between a short and a long column (a common short question): a short column fails by crushing and a long one by buckling; the strength of a short column depends on σc and A only, while the strength of a long column depends on E, I and its length and end conditions.

14. Euler's theory

Assumptions of Euler's theory:

  1. The column is initially perfectly straight, and the load is exactly axial.
  2. The material is homogeneous, isotropic and obeys Hooke's law (stress below the proportional limit).
  3. The cross-section is uniform along the length.
  4. The column is long and slender; it fails by buckling only (crushing is neglected).
  5. The self-weight of the column is neglected.
  6. The lateral deflection is small, so EI·d²y/dx² = M applies.
  7. The ends are frictionless hinges, or ideally fixed, as stated.

Both ends hinged (Fig. 4.9a). At a section x from A, the deflection is y. The load P has a moment −P·y about the section (it bends the column back toward the axis):

\[ EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -P\,y \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \alpha^2 y = 0, \quad \alpha^2 = \frac{P}{EI} \]

The solution is y = C₁ cos αx + C₂ sin αx. At x = 0, y = 0 → C₁ = 0. At x = L, y = 0 → C₂ sin αL = 0. C₂ ≠ 0 (the column has bent), so sin αL = 0 and αL = π (the least non-zero value). So

\[ P_E = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{L^2} \]

PE = Euler's crippling load (N), I = least moment of inertia (mm⁴), L = length (mm).

One end fixed, other end free (Fig. 4.9b). The free end deflects by a. At section x from the fixed end, M = P(a − y). The equation d²y/dx² + α²y = α²a gives y = a(1 − cos αx). At x = L, y = a, so cos αL = 0 and αL = π/2. So PE = π²EI/(4L²). The full steps are in Q7.

16. Limitations of Euler's formula and Rankine's formula

Limitations of Euler's theory:

  • It is valid only for long columns. As Le/k → 0 the Euler load → ∞, which is impossible: a short column crushes first.
  • Euler's stress must not exceed the crushing (or proportional-limit) stress. Set π²E/λ² = σc: for mild steel (E = 200 GPa, σc = 330 N/mm²) the limit is λ ≈ 77–80. Below this value Euler's formula gives a wrong (too high) load.
  • It ignores initial crookedness, eccentricity of the load and residual stresses, which all exist in a real column. Real loads are lower.
  • It ignores direct compressive stress.

Rankine's (Rankine–Gordon) formula is an empirical formula. It is valid for short, medium and long columns. It combines the crushing load and the Euler load:

\[ \frac{1}{P_R} = \frac{1}{P_c} + \frac{1}{P_E} \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_R = \frac{\sigma_c\,A}{1 + \alpha\left(\dfrac{L_e}{k}\right)^2} \]

σc = crushing stress, A = area, α = Rankine's constant = σc/(π²E). For a short column PE is very large, so PR ≈ Pc. For a long column Pc is large compared with PE, so PR ≈ PE.

A member loaded only by a torque (no bending moment, axial or shear force); every section carries the same torque.
Polar moment of inertia J
Second moment of area about the shaft axis: πD⁴/32 (solid), π(Do⁴ − Di⁴)/32 (hollow).
Polar modulus Zp
J/R; torsional strength T = τ·Zp. For a solid shaft Zp = πD³/16.
Torsional rigidity
GJ: the torque that gives unit twist per unit length. Torsional flexibility is L/(GJ).
Power
P = 2πNT/60 W, with N in rpm and T in N·m.
Flexural rigidity
EI (N·mm²): the resistance of a beam to bending deflection.

The curved shape of the neutral axis of a loaded beam; EI·d²y/dx² = M.

In a solid circular shaft under torsion, the shear stress is

  1. uniform over the section
  2. maximum at the centre
  3. zero at the centre and maximum at the outer surface
  4. maximum at R/2

τ/R = q/r: stress is proportional to the radius, so it is zero on the axis and maximum at the surface.

Torsional flexibility of a shaft is

  1. GJ/L
  2. L/(GJ)
  3. GJ
  4. TL/J

Flexibility is the twist per unit torque, θ/T = L/(GJ), the reciprocal of torsional stiffness GJ/L.

If the diameter of a solid shaft is doubled, the torque it can carry at the same stress becomes

  1. 8 times
  2. 4 times
  3. 2 times
  4. 16 times

T = (π/16)τD³, so T is proportional to D³: 2³ = 8. (The stiffness GJ goes up 2⁴ = 16 times.)

The maximum deflection of a cantilever of length L with a point load W at the free end is

  1. WL³/48EI
  2. WL²/2EI
  3. WL³/8EI
  4. WL³/3EI

Double integration with y = dy/dx = 0 at the fixed end gives yB = WL³/3EI. WL²/2EI is the slope.

The product EI is called

  1. torsional rigidity
  2. flexural rigidity
  3. section modulus
  4. modulus of resilience

EI is the flexural rigidity of a beam; GJ is the torsional rigidity of a shaft.

In Macaulay's method, when x is less than a, the term [x − a] is

  1. taken as negative
  2. taken as (a − x)
  3. taken as zero (dropped)
  4. taken as x

Macaulay brackets that become negative are ignored; this keeps one equation valid for every segment.

The effective length of a column with one end fixed and the other end free is

  1. 2L
  2. L
  3. L/2
  4. L/√2

The buckled shape is a quarter of a sine wave; the equivalent hinged column is 2L long. So Le is greater than L.

Slenderness ratio of a column is the effective length divided by

  1. the least lateral dimension
  2. the least radius of gyration
  3. the maximum radius of gyration
  4. the area

λ = Le/kmin; the column buckles about the axis with the least radius of gyration.

Euler's formula gives unsafe results for

  1. long columns
  2. columns with both ends fixed
  3. hollow columns
  4. short columns

For small Le/k the Euler load exceeds the crushing load; a short column crushes first. Use Rankine's formula there.

Theory questions

Q1. With usual notations and assumptions, derive the torsion equation T/J = τ/R = Gθ/L.

Nov 2024 Q8(a) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q8(a) · Aug-Sep 2023 Q8(a) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q7(a) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q7(a) (hollow shaft)

Assumptions (6), figure of the twisted shaft and the end section, shear strain → τ/R = Gθ/L, ring → T = τJ/R, final equation.

Model answer

Pure torsion. A shaft is in pure torsion when only a torque acts on it, so every section carries the same torque and only shear stress.

Assumptions.

  1. The material is homogeneous, isotropic and elastic; it obeys Hooke's law.
  2. The shaft is straight with a uniform circular section (solid or hollow).
  3. The torque acts in a plane perpendicular to the axis (pure torsion).
  4. Plane cross-sections remain plane and radii remain straight after twisting.
  5. The angle of twist is small and uniform along the length.
  6. Stresses do not exceed the limit of proportionality.

Step 1 – shear strain. Let a shaft of length L and radius R be fixed at one end and twisted by T at the other. A surface line AB moves to AB′; the end radius OB turns to OB′ through θ. The arc BB′ seen from both views:

\[ BB' = R\,\theta = L\,\varphi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \varphi = \frac{R\,\theta}{L} \]

Step 2 – shear stress. By Hooke's law in shear, φ = τ/G, where τ is the shear stress at the surface:

\[ \frac{\tau}{G} = \frac{R\,\theta}{L} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\tau}{R} = \frac{G\,\theta}{L} \quad (1) \]

For a layer at radius r, the strain is rθ/L, so the stress there is q = Grθ/L. Hence q/r = τ/R = Gθ/L: the stress varies linearly with radius.

Step 3 – torque. Take an elemental ring of radius r and thickness dr. Area dA = 2πr dr. Shear stress on it q = (τ/R) r.

\[ dF = q\,dA = \frac{\tau}{R}\,r\,(2\pi r\,dr), \qquad dT = dF\cdot r = \frac{\tau}{R}\,2\pi r^3\,dr \]
\[ T = \frac{\tau}{R}\int_0^R 2\pi r^3\,dr = \frac{\tau}{R}\,J, \qquad J = \int_0^R 2\pi r^3\,dr = \frac{\pi R^4}{2} = \frac{\pi D^4}{32} \]

So T/J = τ/R (2). Combine (1) and (2):

\[ \frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{R} = \frac{G\,\theta}{L} \]

T = torque, J = polar moment of inertia, τ = max shear stress at outer radius R, G = modulus of rigidity, θ = angle of twist (rad), L = length.

Hollow shaft (Feb-Mar 2022). For a hollow shaft of inner radius Ri and outer radius Ro, integrate from Ri to Ro:

\[ T = \frac{\tau_{\max}}{R_o}\int_{R_i}^{R_o} 2\pi r^3\,dr = \frac{\tau_{\max}}{R_o}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\left(R_o^4 - R_i^4\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \tau_{\max} = \frac{2\,T\,R_o}{\pi\left(R_o^4 - R_i^4\right)} = \frac{16\,T\,D_o}{\pi\left(D_o^4 - D_i^4\right)} \]

The maximum stress is at the outer surface; at the inner surface τi = τmax·Ri/Ro.

Q2. Explain: (i) polar moment of inertia, (ii) torsional rigidity, (iii) torsional strength, (iv) torsional flexibility, (v) pure torsion. Why is a hollow shaft preferred to a solid shaft?

Jan 2025 Q8(a) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q1 (torsional moment; polar section modulus) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q1 (torsional stiffness) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q1 (pure torsion; angle of twist; torsional flexibility)

One definition with formula and unit for each term, then the hollow-shaft comparison with numbers.

Model answer
  1. Polar moment of inertia J: the second moment of area of a plane section about an axis perpendicular to its plane (the shaft axis). J = ∫r² dA = Ixx + Iyy. Solid: J = πD⁴/32; hollow: J = π(Do⁴ − Di⁴)/32. Unit mm⁴. It measures the resistance of the section to twisting.
  2. Torsional rigidity: the product GJ (N·mm²). From T = GJθ/L, it is the torque required to produce a twist of one radian in a unit length. The torque per radian of twist for the whole shaft, GJ/L, is the torsional stiffness.
  3. Torsional strength: the maximum torque a shaft can transmit without exceeding the allowable shear stress. T = τ·J/R = τ·Zp, where Zp = J/R is the polar modulus (polar section modulus). For a solid shaft T = (π/16)τD³.
  4. Torsional flexibility: the angle of twist produced by a unit torque, θ/T = L/(GJ) (rad per N·mm). It is the reciprocal of torsional stiffness.
  5. Pure torsion: the state of a member subjected only to equal and opposite torques at its ends, without bending moment, axial force or transverse shear force. Every section has the same torque and only shear stresses. Torsional moment is the twisting moment T = F × r about the axis.

Why a hollow shaft is preferred.

  • In a solid shaft the stress at the centre is zero and the core material is lightly stressed (Fig. Q2.1b). A hollow shaft removes this core, so all its material is near τmax.
  • For the same torque and the same maximum stress, a hollow shaft with Di = 0.7Do weighs only 61 % of the solid shaft (a saving of 39 %).
  • For the same weight, the hollow shaft (Di = 0.7Do) is 2.09 times as strong and 2.9 times as stiff.
  • Less rotating mass means lower inertia; the bore can carry oil or cables.
  • Limitations: larger outside diameter and higher cost of manufacture.

Q3. Derive the Euler–Bernoulli differential equation for the deflection of a beam, EI·d²y/dx² = M. Write the relation between slope, deflection and radius of curvature.

Nov 2024 Q9(b) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q8(a) · Nov-Dec 2025 Q9(a) · Aug-Sep 2023 Q9(a) · Jan 2025 Q9(a) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q1 (flexural rigidity; methods to find deflection)

Figure of the elastic curve, M/I = E/R, ds = R dθ, small slope → 1/R = d²y/dx², assumptions, sign convention.

Model answer

Definitions. When a beam bends, its neutral axis takes the shape of a curve called the elastic curve. The deflection y is the vertical displacement of a point on the neutral axis; the slope θ = dy/dx is the angle of the tangent to the elastic curve.

Derivation. Let P and Q be two points on the elastic curve a small distance ds apart. The normals at P and Q meet at the centre of curvature O; the angle between them is dθ and the radius is R.

\[ ds = R\,d\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{d\theta}{ds} \]

The deflection is small, so ds ≈ dx and tan θ ≈ θ = dy/dx. Hence

\[ \frac{1}{R} = \frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \]

(The exact curvature is \( \frac{1}{R} = \frac{d^2y/dx^2}{\left[1 + (dy/dx)^2\right]^{3/2}} \); for small slopes (dy/dx)² ≈ 0.)

From the bending equation M/I = E/R:

\[ \frac{1}{R} = \frac{M}{E\,I} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{M}{EI} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E\,I\,\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = M \]

EI = flexural rigidity, M = bending moment at the section.

Assumptions. Hooke's law holds; E and I are constant; the beam is initially straight; deflections are small; deflection due to shear is neglected; plane sections remain plane.

Sign convention. x positive to the right, y positive upward; sagging moment positive (concave up, d²y/dx² > 0).

Relations.

\[ \text{deflection} = y, \quad \text{slope} = \frac{dy}{dx}, \quad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{M}{EI}, \quad F = EI\frac{d^3y}{dx^3}, \quad w = EI\frac{d^4y}{dx^4} \]

Methods to find deflection: double integration method, Macaulay's method, moment-area method, conjugate beam method, strain energy (Castigliano) method.

Q4. By the double integration method, derive the slope and deflection at the free end of a cantilever of length L with a point load W at the free end. Also find the maximum slope and deflection of a simply supported beam with a UDL w over the whole span.

Jun-Jul 2024 Q9(a) and Q9(b) · Nov 2024 Q9(a) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q8 · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q8(a) (uniform bending moment)

Figure, M at section x, integrate twice, boundary conditions, final formulas with negative sign meaning downward.

Model answer

(a) Cantilever with W at the free end. Let the cantilever AB be fixed at A and free at B. Take x from A.

Bending moment at section X–X (hogging): M = −W(L − x).

\[ EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -W(L - x) \]
\[ EI\frac{dy}{dx} = -W\left(Lx - \frac{x^2}{2}\right) + C_1 \qquad EI\,y = -W\left(\frac{Lx^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{6}\right) + C_1x + C_2 \]

Boundary conditions at the fixed end x = 0: dy/dx = 0 → C₁ = 0; y = 0 → C₂ = 0.

Slope and deflection equations:

\[ EI\frac{dy}{dx} = -W\left(Lx - \frac{x^2}{2}\right), \qquad EI\,y = -\frac{W}{6}\left(3Lx^2 - x^3\right) \]

At the free end (x = L):

\[ \theta_B = -\frac{WL^2}{2EI} \qquad y_B = -\frac{WL^3}{3EI} \]

Negative signs: the free end slopes downward and deflects downward. These are the maximum values.

(b) Simply supported beam with UDL w over span L. RA = RB = wL/2. At distance x from A:

\[ EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{wL}{2}x - \frac{w x^2}{2} \]
\[ EI\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{wLx^2}{4} - \frac{wx^3}{6} + C_1 \qquad EI\,y = \frac{wLx^3}{12} - \frac{wx^4}{24} + C_1x + C_2 \]

At x = 0, y = 0 → C₂ = 0. By symmetry dy/dx = 0 at x = L/2:

\[ 0 = \frac{wL^3}{16} - \frac{wL^3}{48} + C_1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_1 = -\frac{wL^3}{24} \]

Maximum slope at the supports (x = 0) and maximum deflection at the centre (x = L/2):

\[ \theta_A = -\frac{wL^3}{24EI} \qquad y_C = \frac{1}{EI}\left[\frac{wL^4}{96} - \frac{wL^4}{384} - \frac{wL^4}{48}\right] = -\frac{5\,wL^4}{384\,EI} \]

(c) Beam under a uniform bending moment M (Mar-Apr 2021). If equal couples M act at the ends of a simply supported beam, M is constant, so EI·d²y/dx² = M. Integrating, EI·dy/dx = Mx + C₁ and EI·y = Mx²/2 + C₁x + C₂. With y = 0 at x = 0 and x = L: C₂ = 0, C₁ = −ML/2. So the slope at the ends is ML/(2EI), and the central deflection is ML²/(8EI). The beam bends into a circular arc of radius R = EI/M.

Q5. Explain Macaulay's method for finding the deflection of beams. Derive the general slope and deflection equations for a simply supported beam with two point loads.

Aug-Sep 2023 Q10(a) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q1 (methods to find deflection)

Why plain integration is lengthy, the bracket rules, one worked general equation, constants from the supports.

Model answer

Need. When a beam carries several point loads, the bending-moment expression changes at each load. Ordinary double integration needs a separate equation for each segment and two constants per segment, which must be matched at every load point. Macaulay's method uses one equation for the whole beam with only two constants.

Method. Consider beam AB of span L with loads W₁ at C (distance a from A) and W₂ at D (distance b). Reactions RA = [W₁(L − a) + W₂(L − b)]/L, RB = W₁ + W₂ − RA. Take section X–X in the last segment DB at distance x from A:

\[ EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = R_A\,x \;\Big|\; -\,W_1[x-a] \;\Big|\; -\,W_2[x-b] \]
\[ EI\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{R_Ax^2}{2} + C_1 \;\Big|\; -\,\frac{W_1[x-a]^2}{2} \;\Big|\; -\,\frac{W_2[x-b]^2}{2} \]
\[ EI\,y = \frac{R_Ax^3}{6} + C_1x + C_2 \;\Big|\; -\,\frac{W_1[x-a]^3}{6} \;\Big|\; -\,\frac{W_2[x-b]^3}{6} \]

The vertical lines separate the segments: for a point in AC use only the terms before the first line; in CD the terms before the second line; in DB all terms.

Rules.

  1. x is measured from the left end for all segments.
  2. Brackets are integrated as a whole: ∫[x − a]ⁿ dx = [x − a]ⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1).
  3. When a bracket is negative (x < a), it is taken as zero.
  4. Constants C₁ and C₂ are written with the first segment, so they are the same for all segments.
  5. A UDL that does not reach the end is extended to the end, and an equal and opposite UDL is added on the extended part.
  6. A couple M₀ at distance c is written as M₀[x − c]⁰.

Constants. At x = 0, y = 0: C₂ = 0 (all brackets are negative). At x = L, y = 0:

\[ 0 = \frac{R_AL^3}{6} - \frac{W_1(L-a)^3}{6} - \frac{W_2(L-b)^3}{6} + C_1L \]

This gives C₁. The deflection anywhere follows from the y equation, and the maximum deflection is where dy/dx = 0 (solve in the segment where the slope changes sign). Worked example: N8.

Q6. Write a note on the classification of columns. State the assumptions made in Euler's theory and its limitations. Explain effective length.

Nov 2024 Q10(a) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q10(a) · Jan 2025 Q10(a) · Nov-Dec 2025 Q10(a) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q1 (short and long columns; slenderness ratio; equivalent length) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q1 (slenderness ratio)

Short/medium/long by slenderness ratio with failure mode, 6–7 assumptions, 4 limitations, end-condition table and figure.

Model answer

Column and strut. A column is a vertical member carrying an axial compressive load; a strut is a compression member that may be inclined (truss members, links).

Classification (by slenderness ratio λ = Le/k, k = least radius of gyration = √(Imin/A)):

  1. Short columns: λ < 32 (Le < 8 times the least lateral dimension). They fail by crushing; Pc = σcA.
  2. Medium columns: 32 < λ < 120. They fail by a combination of crushing and buckling; Rankine's formula is used.
  3. Long columns: λ > 120 (Le > 30 times the least lateral dimension). They fail by buckling (lateral bending) at a load much lower than the crushing load; Euler's formula is used.

Assumptions in Euler's theory.

  1. The column is initially perfectly straight and the load is exactly axial.
  2. The material is homogeneous, isotropic and perfectly elastic; it obeys Hooke's law.
  3. The cross-section is uniform throughout the length.
  4. The length is very large compared with the lateral dimensions; the column fails by buckling alone.
  5. The direct compressive stress is negligible compared with the bending stress.
  6. The self-weight of the column is neglected.
  7. The ends are frictionless (hinged ends) or perfectly fixed.

Limitations of Euler's theory.

  • It ignores the direct stress, so it is valid only for long columns. For short columns PE → ∞ as Le/k → 0, which is absurd.
  • The Euler stress π²E/λ² must be less than the crushing stress: for mild steel λ must exceed about 80 (77 for σc = 330 N/mm², E = 200 GPa).
  • Real columns have initial curvature, eccentric loads and imperfect end fixity, so the actual load is less than PE.
  • It gives no factor of safety against crushing; Rankine's empirical formula is used for practical design.

Effective length. The effective (equivalent) length Le of a column is the length of a column with both ends hinged that has the same crippling load. It is the distance between adjacent points of inflection (zero moment) of the buckled shape. PE = π²EI/Le².

Q7. Derive Euler's crippling load for a column (a) when both ends are hinged and (b) when one end is fixed and the other end is free.

Nov 2024 Q10(b) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q10(b) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q9(a) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q9(a) · Nov-Dec 2025 Q10(b) · Jan 2025 Q10(b) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q9(a)

Figure, moment at section x, differential equation, general solution, two end conditions, least non-zero root.

Model answer

(a) Both ends hinged. Column AB of length L carries the crippling load P. At a section x from A, the lateral deflection is y. The moment at the section is M = −P·y (it tends to reduce the deflection).

\[ EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -P\,y \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \frac{P}{EI}\,y = 0 \]

Let α² = P/EI. The solution of d²y/dx² + α²y = 0 is

\[ y = C_1\cos(\alpha x) + C_2\sin(\alpha x) \]

End conditions: at x = 0, y = 0 → C₁ = 0. At x = L, y = 0 → C₂ sin(αL) = 0. C₂ cannot be zero (then there is no buckling), so sin(αL) = 0:

\[ \alpha L = 0,\ \pi,\ 2\pi, \ldots \quad\text{least non-zero: } \alpha L = \pi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sqrt{\frac{P}{EI}}\,L = \pi \]
\[ P_E = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{L^2} \]

I = least moment of inertia. The buckled shape is y = C₂ sin(πx/L), a half sine wave.

(b) One end fixed, other end free. End A is fixed, end B is free. Under the load P, B moves sideways by a. At section x from A (deflection y), the moment of P about the section is

\[ M = P\,(a - y) \quad\Rightarrow\quad EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = P\,(a - y) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \alpha^2 y = \alpha^2 a \]

General solution (complementary function + particular integral):

\[ y = C_1\cos(\alpha x) + C_2\sin(\alpha x) + a \]

At x = 0, y = 0 → C₁ = −a. At x = 0, dy/dx = 0 → αC₂ = 0, so C₂ = 0. Hence y = a(1 − cos αx).

At the free end x = L, y = a:

\[ a = a\left(1 - \cos\alpha L\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \cos\alpha L = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha L = \frac{\pi}{2} \]
\[ \sqrt{\frac{P}{EI}}\,L = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_E = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{4L^2} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(2L)^2} \]

The effective length is 2L; the load is one quarter of that for a hinged column of the same length.

N8. A beam of length 6 m is simply supported at its ends and carries two point loads of 48 kN and 40 kN at 1 m and 3 m respectively from the left support. Find (i) the deflection under each load, (ii) the maximum deflection, (iii) the point at which the maximum deflection occurs. Take E = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm² and I = 85 × 10⁶ mm⁴.

Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q8(b)

  1. EI = 2 × 10⁵ × 85 × 10⁶ = 1.7 × 10¹³ N·mm² = 17 000 kN·m².
  2. Reactions: RA × 6 = 48 × 5 + 40 × 3 → RA = 60 kN; RB = 88 − 60 = 28 kN.
  3. Macaulay: \( EI\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 60x - 48[x - 1] - 40[x - 3] \)
  4. \( EI\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 30x^2 + C_1 - 24[x - 1]^2 - 20[x - 3]^2 \)

  8. Under 40 kN (x = 3): EI·y = 270 − 490 − 8(8) = −284.0 → y = 16.71 mm.

N9. Find Euler's crippling load for a hollow column of 38 mm outer diameter and 2.5 mm thick. The length of the column is 2.5 m and it is hinged at both ends. Take E = 2.05 × 10⁵ N/mm². Also find the crippling load by Rankine's formula using α = 1/7500 and σc = 335 N/mm².

Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q9(b)

Data note. The scan prints "E = 2.05 MPa", which is an obvious error; the value is E = 2.05 × 10⁵ N/mm² (205 GPa).
  1. Inner diameter d = 38 − 2 × 2.5 = 33 mm.
  2. \( I = \dfrac{\pi}{64}\left(38^4 - 33^4\right) = 44\,140\ \text{mm}^4,\qquad A = \dfrac{\pi}{4}\left(38^2 - 33^2\right) = 278.82\ \text{mm}^2 \)
  3. \( k = \sqrt{I/A} = \sqrt{44\,140/278.82} = 12.58\ \text{mm} \) (check: k = √(D² + d²)/4 ✓); both ends hinged → Le = L = 2500 mm.
  4. Euler: \( P_E = \dfrac{\pi^2 EI}{L_e^2} = \dfrac{\pi^2 \times 2.05 \times 10^5 \times 44\,140}{2500^2} = 14\,289\ \text{N} = 14.29\ \text{kN} \)
  5. Slenderness ratio λ = 2500/12.58 = 198.7; λ²/7500 = 5.264.
  6. Rankine: \( P_R = \dfrac{\sigma_c A}{1 + \alpha\lambda^2} = \dfrac{335 \times 278.82}{1 + 5.264} = \dfrac{93\,403}{6.264} = 14\,912\ \text{N} = 14.91\ \text{kN} \)
  7. Rankine is about 4 % higher than Euler here: α = 1/7500 corresponds to E = σc/(π²α) ≈ 255 GPa, a little above the given E. For a long column the two formulas nearly agree.

Answer: PEuler = 14.29 kN; PRankine = 14.91 kN.
