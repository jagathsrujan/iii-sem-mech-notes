Heat Treatment

Heat treatment changes the properties of a metal by heating and cooling it in the solid state. This unit covers the TTT and CCT diagrams, annealing, normalising, hardening, tempering, surface hardening and age hardening. The sketches and the comparison tables carry most of the marks.

Concepts

Exam pattern. One 20-mark question (two parts of 10, choose one). Repeats: TTT, annealing and normalising, hardening and tempering, induction and flame hardening, martempering, hardenability, precipitation hardening.

1. What heat treatment does

Heat treatment is a controlled cycle of heating, soaking and cooling of a solid metal to get the required structure and properties. The cooling rate decides the final structure.

Purposes: relieve stress, soften for machining, refine grain, increase hardness and strength, improve toughness and surface wear resistance. The treatments work because steel changes structure at A₁ (727 °C), A₃ and Acm (Unit 2).

2. TTT diagram (isothermal transformation)

The TTT diagram (Time–Temperature–Transformation, also called the S-curve or C-curve) shows how long austenite takes to transform at a constant temperature. It is drawn for one steel composition. Fig. 4.1 is for eutectoid steel (0.8 % C).

Samples are quenched to salt baths at fixed temperatures and held for different times. The start and end of transformation give two C-curves.

  • Above A₁ austenite is stable. Left of the start curve it is unstable austenite.
  • The nose (about 550 °C, 1 s) is where diffusion is fastest.
  • Ms (about 230 °C) and Mf (about −50 °C) are horizontal lines. Martensite forms by shear, not diffusion.

3. CCT diagram and how it differs

The CCT diagram (Continuous Cooling Transformation) is made by cooling samples at steady rates, as in real quenching.

  • CCT curves lie to the right and below the TTT curves.
  • The critical cooling rate just misses the nose and gives full martensite.
  • Plain 0.8 % C steel has no bainite in CCT. Pearlite stops at about 450 °C.

4. Annealing: recovery, recrystallisation, grain growth

Heating cold-worked metal restores softness in three stages (Fig. 4.3).

StageWhat happensEffect on properties
RecoveryPoint defects vanish. Dislocations rearrange. Grains stay elongated.Internal stress falls. Strength and ductility change little. Electrical conductivity returns.
RecrystallisationNew strain-free equiaxed grains nucleate and grow. The old grains vanish.Strength and hardness fall sharply. Ductility rises sharply.
Grain growthLarge grains grow at the cost of small ones to lower grain-boundary energy.Strength and toughness fall further. Surface becomes rough (orange peel).

The recrystallisation temperature is the lowest temperature at which cold-worked metal fully recrystallises in about 1 h: about 0.3–0.5 Tm (kelvin). More cold work lowers it.

5. Types of annealing

Annealing is heating, soaking and slow cooling (usually in the furnace). It gives a soft, ductile, stress-free structure.

TypeTemperatureCoolingStructure / purpose

6. Normalising

Normalising heats steel to 40–50 °C above A₃ (or Acm), soaks it, and cools it in still air. Air cooling is faster than furnace cooling, so the pearlite is finer. The steel is harder and stronger than annealed steel and more uniform. It refines coarse grain from casting and forging. See the comparison table in Q3.

7. Hardening and quenching

Hardening heats steel to A₃ + 30–50 °C (or A₁ + 30–50 °C for hypereutectoid), soaks it, and quenches it faster than the critical rate. Austenite changes to hard, brittle martensite. Steel needs at least 0.3 % C.

Quench mediumSeverityNote
BrineVery fastPlain carbon steel; cracks likely
WaterFastSimple shapes
OilSlowAlloy steels, complex shapes
AirVery slowHigh-alloy tool steels

Martensite has a larger volume. Quenching too fast gives distortion and cracks.

8. Tempering

Tempering reheats hardened steel to a temperature below A₁, holds it, and cools it in air. It converts brittle martensite to tough tempered martensite.

TypeTemperatureStructureProperties and uses
Low150–250 °CTempered martensiteHard (58–62 HRC), some toughness. Cutting tools, gauges, bearings.
Medium350–450 °CTroostiteTough, elastic. Springs, chisels, hammers.
High500–650 °CSorbiteVery tough, moderate strength (25–35 HRC). Gears, shafts, connecting rods.

Why tempering must follow hardening. Martensite is brittle and full of stress. Tempering relieves stress, stops cracks, gives toughness, converts retained austenite and stabilises size. Hardening plus high tempering is quenching and tempering. Avoid 250–400 °C for some steels (temper embrittlement).

9. Martempering and austempering

Both treatments quench into a hot bath so that the part cools evenly. They reduce distortion and cracking (Fig. 4.6).

  • Martempering: quench into a bath just above Ms (about 250 °C), hold until the temperature is equal through the part, then air cool through Ms. Martensite forms evenly. Temper afterwards.
  • Austempering: quench into a bath at 250–400 °C, hold until all austenite becomes bainite, then air cool. Hard, tough bainite; no tempering.
  • Both suit small sections only.

10. Hardenability and the Jominy test

Hardenability is the ability of steel to form martensite to a depth when quenched. It is not the same as hardness. Hardness is the maximum value; hardenability is the depth.

The Jominy end-quench test measures it. A standard bar (25 mm diameter, 100 mm long) is heated to austenite and placed in a fixture. A jet of water (24 °C) quenches only the bottom end. The cooling rate falls along the bar. Hardness (HRC) is measured on a ground flat at set distances from the quenched end (Fig. 4.7).

A flat curve means high hardenability (alloy steel). A steep curve means low (plain carbon steel).

Factors: more carbon; alloying elements (Mn, Cr, Mo, Ni) shift the nose right; coarse austenite grains; homogeneous austenite. Quench medium and section size change the cooling rate, not the steel.

11. Surface (case) hardening

Some parts, such as gears and shafts, need a hard wear-resistant surface and a tough core. Surface hardening gives both. There are two ways: change the surface chemistry, or heat only the surface.

MethodProcessCase, hardness
Pack carburisingParts in charcoal + BaCO₃, 900–950 °C. Then quench and temper. Low-carbon steel.0.5–2 mm, 58–62 HRC
Gas / liquid carburisingCarbon-rich gas or salt bath. Faster, exact control.0.5–2 mm
CyanidingNaCN bath, 800–870 °C. C + N diffuse in. Then quench.0.1–0.5 mm

Induction hardening: a coil with high-frequency current heats only the skin (skin effect) in seconds. A water spray quenches it (Fig. 4.9). Case 0.5–5 mm, steel 0.4–0.6 % C. Fast and easy to automate.

Flame hardening: an oxy-acetylene flame heats the surface above A₃ and a water spray follows the torch (Fig. 4.10). Case 2–6 mm. Suits large parts and local areas such as gear teeth and rails.

12. Age (precipitation) hardening

Age hardening strengthens an alloy by fine precipitates from a supersaturated solid solution. It needs a sloping solvus. Example: Al–4 % Cu.

  1. Solution treatment. Heat above the solvus (about 500 °C) and soak. All Cu dissolves in α.
  2. Quench. Cool fast in water. Cu has no time to leave. α is supersaturated and soft.
  3. Ageing. Hold at room temperature (natural ageing) or at 130–190 °C (artificial ageing). Cu atoms cluster and precipitate.

Sequence: supersaturated α → GP zones → θ″ → θ′ → θ (CuAl₂). Coherent zones block dislocations, so hardness peaks. Too long or too hot gives coarse θ and over-ageing (Fig. 4.12).

PH steels (for example 17-4 PH: 17 % Cr, 4 % Ni, 4 % Cu) are solution annealed at about 1040 °C, then aged at 480–620 °C. Precipitates give yield strength above 1000 MPa with corrosion resistance. Uses: aircraft parts, valves, shafts.

Key terms

Austenitising
Heating steel into the austenite range so the structure becomes FCC γ.
Martensite
Hard, brittle supersaturated solid solution of carbon in iron (BCT), formed by diffusionless shear on fast quenching.
Bainite
Ferrite and fine cementite formed isothermally between about 550 °C and Ms.
Critical cooling rate
The slowest cooling rate that gives a fully martensitic structure.
Retained austenite
Austenite that stays after quenching because Mf is below room temperature.
Hardenability
Depth to which steel hardens on quenching. Measured by the Jominy test.
Recrystallisation temperature
The lowest temperature at which cold-worked metal forms new strain-free grains in about 1 hour.
Case hardening
Hardening only the surface layer while the core stays tough.
Skin effect
High-frequency current flows in the surface layer only. This is the basis of induction hardening.
Over-ageing
Loss of strength when precipitates grow too coarse during long or hot ageing.

Theory questions

Q1. Draw a neat labelled TTT diagram for eutectoid steel (0.8 % C) and explain it. How is it different from a CCT diagram?

MSE QB Q19 · Aug-Sep 2023 · Jun-Jul 2024 · Nov 2024 · Jan 2025 · Nov-Dec 2025

Draw the two C-curves with A₁, Ms, Mf and the regions. Mark four cooling paths. Then state the difference from CCT.

Model answer

Definition. The TTT (Time–Temperature–Transformation) diagram plots the time for austenite to transform at constant temperature. It is also called the isothermal transformation or S-curve diagram.

Construction.

  1. Cut many small thin samples of 0.8 % C steel.
  2. Austenitise them above 727 °C.
  3. Quench each sample into a salt bath held at a fixed temperature below A₁.
  4. Hold each sample for a different time, then quench in water.
  5. Find the amount of transformed product by microscope or hardness.
  6. Mark the times for start (about 1 %) and end (about 99 %) of transformation. Join them to give the two C-curves.

Explanation.

  • Above A₁ (727 °C) austenite is stable. Left of the start curve it is unstable austenite. Between the curves austenite and product are both present. Right of the finish curve, transformation is complete.
  • Pearlite forms from 727 to about 550 °C. Near A₁ it is coarse and soft (15–25 HRC). Near the nose it is fine and stronger (30–40 HRC).
  • Bainite forms from about 550 °C down to Ms. It is a mixture of ferrite and fine carbide (40–60 HRC), and it is tough.
  • Martensite forms below Ms (about 230 °C) if the cooling is fast enough to miss the nose. It forms by shear, finishes at Mf, is the hardest product (60–67 HRC) and is brittle. Some retained austenite stays.
  • Path ① (water quench): martensite. ② (furnace cool): coarse pearlite. ③ (air cool): fine pearlite. ④ (quench to 350 °C and hold): bainite.

Difference from CCT. TTT is for isothermal holding. CCT is for continuous cooling; its curves lie to the right and lower, and plain 0.8 % C steel shows no bainite. Use TTT for austempering and martempering, and CCT for real quenches.

Limit. The diagram is for one composition and one austenite grain size. Alloy elements move the nose to the right.

Q2. Explain any two types of annealing. Describe recovery, recrystallisation and grain growth.

MSE QB Q30 · 2021 CBCS (Mar-Apr) · Nov 2024

Define annealing; give the three stages with a property graph; then two types with temperature and cooling.

Model answer

Definition. Annealing is heating a metal to a set temperature, soaking, and cooling slowly (furnace cooling). It softens the metal, removes stress, refines the grain and improves machinability and ductility.

Stages of annealing of cold-worked metal.

  • Recovery. At low temperature, dislocations rearrange and point defects vanish. Internal stress falls. Strength and ductility hardly change. Grains remain elongated.
  • Recrystallisation. Above about 0.4 Tm new strain-free equiaxed grains nucleate at the boundaries and grow until they replace all old grains. Strength falls and ductility rises sharply.
  • Grain growth. At higher temperature or longer time, large grains absorb small ones. Strength and toughness fall; the surface becomes rough.

Type 1: Full annealing.

  • Heat to A₃ + 30–50 °C (hypoeutectoid) or A₁ + 30–50 °C (hypereutectoid). Soak 1 h per 25 mm of thickness. Cool in the furnace.
  • Structure: coarse pearlite + ferrite (or pearlite + cementite).
  • Purpose: soften, refine grain, increase ductility, improve machinability, remove stress.

Type 2: Spheroidising annealing.

  • Heat to 650–700 °C (just below A₁) for 15–25 h, or cycle above and below A₁. Cool slowly.
  • Cementite plates change to spheres in a ferrite matrix.
  • Use: high-carbon steel (0.6–1.5 % C). It gives the softest condition for machining and cold forming.

Other types. Process annealing (550–650 °C) removes cold work in low-carbon sheet. Stress-relief annealing (500–650 °C) removes residual stress without structure change.

Definition. Normalising is a heat treatment in which steel is heated 40–50 °C above A₃ (hypoeutectoid) or above Acm (hypereutectoid), soaked, and cooled in still air.

Process.

  2. Soak until the structure is uniform austenite (about 1 h per 25 mm).

Q4. Describe the tempering heat treatment with a neat sketch. Why must hardening always be followed by tempering?

MSE QB Q36 · Jun-Jul 2024

Define hardening and quenching first, give the tempering types on the hardness graph, then explain why.

Model answer

Hardening. Heat steel to A₃ + 30–50 °C (or A₁ + 30–50 °C for hypereutectoid steel), soak, and quench faster than the critical rate in water, oil or brine. Austenite changes to hard martensite (60–67 HRC).

Tempering. Reheat hardened steel to a temperature below A₁ (727 °C), hold it, and cool it in air. Carbon leaves the martensite as fine carbide and the structure becomes tougher.

TypeTemperatureStructureProperties and uses
Low150–250 °CTempered martensiteHard, 58–62 HRC. Cutting tools, gauges
Medium350–450 °CTroostiteTough and elastic. Springs, chisels
High500–650 °CSorbiteVery tough. Gears, shafts, connecting rods

Why hardening must be followed by tempering.

  • Martensite is very brittle. The part can crack under small shock.
  • Quenching leaves large residual stresses. They can cause cracks, even after some hours (season cracking).
  • Some austenite is retained. It changes slowly later, and the size of the part changes.
  • Tempering relieves stress, gives toughness and ductility, and makes the size stable.
  • Tempering gives the exact balance of hardness and toughness that the application needs.

Tempering lowers hardness a little, but the gain in toughness is far more important.

Q5. Explain any two surface hardening methods (carburising, cyaniding, nitriding).

MSE QB Q32 · Nov 2024 · Nov-Dec 2025

Pick pack carburising and nitriding (or cyaniding). Sketch each, give temperature, time, case depth, steel and the heat treatment after.

Model answer

Aim. A hard, wear-resistant surface (case) on a tough core. Parts: gears, cams, shafts.

1. Pack carburising.

  • Steel: low-carbon (0.1–0.25 % C).
  • Parts are packed in a sealed steel box with charcoal and 20–40 % energiser (BaCO₃). The box is heated to 900–950 °C for several hours.
  • Charcoal gives CO. CO releases atomic carbon on the steel surface, and carbon diffuses into austenite. The case reaches 0.8–1.0 % C.
  • Case depth 0.5–2 mm grows with time and temperature.
  • After this, quench (and refine the core if needed) and temper at 150–200 °C. The case is martensite (58–62 HRC); the core stays soft and tough.
  • Gas and liquid carburising use gas or a salt bath. They are faster and more exact.

2. Nitriding.

  • Steel: alloy steel with Cr, Al, Mo or V (nitriding steels), already hardened and tempered.
  • Parts are held in a sealed retort at 500–550 °C in ammonia gas for 10–100 h. NH₃ breaks down and nitrogen forms very hard nitrides (Fe₄N, AlN, CrN).
  • Case depth 0.1–0.7 mm; hardness 900–1200 HV.
  • No quench is needed. Distortion is very small. The case also resists corrosion and fatigue.
  • Limits: slow and costly; thin case.

Cyaniding. The part is dipped in a molten NaCN bath at 800–870 °C for 30–90 min. Carbon and nitrogen diffuse in. Then oil or water quench. The case is thin (0.1–0.5 mm) and quick to make. Cyanide is poisonous.

Q6. Explain the induction hardening and flame hardening methods with neat diagrams.

MSE QB Q39 · MSE QB Q32 · Aug-Sep 2023 · Nov 2024 · Jan 2025 · Nov-Dec 2025

Draw one sketch for each. Give heating source, temperature, quench, case depth, steels, advantages and limits.

Model answer

1. Induction hardening

  • The part is placed in a water-cooled copper coil that carries high-frequency alternating current (10 kHz to 1 MHz).
  • The changing magnetic field induces eddy currents in the part. By the skin effect they flow in the surface, so only the skin heats above A₃ within seconds.
  • A water or polymer spray (quench ring) cools the part at once. The skin becomes martensite. A low temper follows.
  • Case depth 0.5–5 mm: higher frequency gives a thinner case.
  • Steel: medium carbon (0.4–0.6 % C) or low-alloy steel.
  • Advantages: very fast, exact depth, little distortion, clean, easy to automate.
  • Limits: a special coil for each shape; high equipment cost, so suited to mass production.

2. Flame hardening

  • An oxy-acetylene torch heats the surface above A₃ (about 850–900 °C) while it moves along the part.
  • A water spray follows the torch and quenches the heated skin. The skin becomes martensite. A low temper follows.
  • Case depth 2–6 mm.
  • Steel: medium carbon (0.4–0.7 % C), cast iron, alloy steel.
  • Advantages: low cost, portable, suits large parts and local areas (gear teeth, lathe bed ways, rails).
  • Limits: poor temperature control; risk of overheating and cracks; needs a skilled operator.

Q7. Explain (i) hardenability with the Jominy end-quench test and the factors affecting it, and (ii) martempering and austempering.

Nov-Dec 2025 · Syllabus topic

Give the Jominy sketch and curve first. List factors. Then draw martempering and austempering on one TTT diagram.

Model answer

(i) Hardenability. It is the ability of steel to harden to a depth by quenching (to form martensite). Hardness is the highest value reached. Hardenability is the depth of hardening.

Jominy test. A bar 25 mm in diameter and 100 mm long is austenitised and placed in a fixture. A water jet of 12.5 mm bore (24 °C, 65 mm free height) quenches the bottom end only. The cooling rate falls along the bar. Two flat strips are ground on opposite sides. Hardness (HRC) is read every 1.5 mm from the quenched end and plotted.

A flat curve means deep hardening (alloy steel). A steep curve means shallow hardening (plain carbon steel).

Factors.

  • Carbon content: raises hardness and hardenability.
  • Alloying elements (Mn, Cr, Mo, Ni, B): move the TTT nose to the right, so the critical cooling rate is lower.
  • Austenite grain size: coarse grains raise hardenability.
  • Homogeneous austenite: undissolved carbides lower it.
  • Quench medium and section size change the cooling rate, so they change the depth of hardening in practice.

(ii) Martempering and austempering.

  • Martempering: austenitise, quench into a bath just above Ms (about 250 °C), hold until the temperature is equal in the core and skin, then cool in air through Ms. The whole part turns to martensite at the same time, so stress and distortion are low. Temper afterwards.
  • Austempering: austenitise, quench into a bath at 250–400 °C (above Ms), hold until all austenite becomes bainite, then cool in air. The product is tough, hard bainite. No tempering is needed. Distortion and cracks are very rare.
  • Both suit small sections only, since a thick part cannot cool fast enough to miss the nose.

Q8. Explain age (precipitation) hardening of Al–Cu alloys. Write a note on PH steels.

Nov-Dec 2025 · Nov 2024 · Syllabus topic

Draw the Al-rich end of the Al–Cu diagram with the solvus, then the three steps, then the mechanism and PH steels.

Model answer

Definition. Age (precipitation) hardening increases the strength of an alloy by fine precipitates formed from a supersaturated solid solution.

Condition. The solid solubility must fall with falling temperature (a sloping solvus), and the precipitate must be hard.

Steps for Al–4 % Cu.

  1. Solution treatment. Heat to about 500 °C, above the solvus and below the solidus. Soak until all Cu dissolves in single-phase α.
  2. Quenching. Cool fast in water. The Cu is trapped: α is supersaturated, and soft.
  3. Ageing. Hold at room temperature (natural ageing, days) or 130–190 °C (artificial ageing, hours). Precipitates form.

Mechanism. Supersaturated α → GP zones → θ″ → θ′ → θ (CuAl₂). Coherent GP zones and θ″ strain the lattice and block dislocation motion. Hardness peaks when the precipitates are small and close together. Long ageing makes coarse, non-coherent θ, and the alloy over-ages (softens). Higher ageing temperature is faster, but the peak is lower.

PH steels. Precipitation-hardening stainless steels (17-4 PH: 17 % Cr, 4 % Ni, 4 % Cu; 17-7 PH) combine corrosion resistance with high strength.

  • Solution anneal at about 1040 °C and cool to soft martensite (or austenite).
  • Age at 480–620 °C. Fine Cu-rich or Ni–Al precipitates harden the matrix.
  • Yield strength above 1000 MPa; easy to form before ageing; low distortion on ageing.
  • Uses: aircraft parts, valves, pump shafts, nuclear and chemical plant.

Uses of aged Al–Cu alloys. Duralumin and 2024 alloy: aircraft skin, rivets, structural parts.
