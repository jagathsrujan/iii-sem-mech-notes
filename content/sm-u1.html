Simple Stresses and Strains

This unit explains how a bar resists a direct load: the stress inside it, how much it stretches, and the material constants that link the two. Every later unit (beams, shafts, cylinders) uses these ideas, and each exam paper gives one full question (20 marks) to this unit.

Concepts

Each question usually has one 10-mark derivation or explanation (stress–strain curve, tapered bar, relation between elastic constants) and one 10-mark numerical (stepped bar, tapered bar, composite bar or elastic constants). 1. Load and stress

A load is an external force on a member. A rod in a machine, a bolt, a column or a crane rope carries load. The member resists the load with internal forces.

Stress is the internal resisting force per unit area of the cross-section. For a bar with an axial load P on area A:

\[ \sigma = \frac{P}{A} \]

σ = normal stress (N/mm² = MPa), P = axial load (N), A = cross-sectional area (mm²). 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa = 10⁶ N/m².

There are three types of simple (direct) stress. Fig. 1.1 shows them.

  • Tensile stress: two equal forces pull the bar outward. The bar gets longer. Take it as positive (+).
  • Compressive stress: two equal forces push the bar inward. The bar gets shorter. Take it as negative (−).
  • Shear stress (τ): two equal forces act tangential to a plane and try to slide one part over the other. A rivet or a pin in a joint is in shear: \( \tau = P/A \), where A is the area of the shear plane.
Normal and tangential. Tensile and compressive stresses act normal (at 90°) to the plane. They are called normal or direct stresses. Shear stress acts along (parallel to) the plane.

Quick example. A 20 mm rivet joins two plates that pull apart with 30 kN. The rivet has one shear plane of area π/4 × 20² = 314.2 mm². So τ = 30 000 / 314.2 = 95.5 N/mm². If the rivet had two shear planes (double shear), the area would double and τ would be 47.7 N/mm².

2. Strain

Strain is the deformation per unit original length. It has no unit, because it is a length divided by a length.

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\delta L}{L} \]

ε = linear (longitudinal) strain, δL = change in length (mm), L = original length (mm).

  • Tensile strain: increase in length / original length.
  • Compressive strain: decrease in length / original length.
  • Shear strain (φ): the change in a right angle, in radian (Section 7).
  • Volumetric strain (\( \varepsilon_v \)): change in volume / original volume (Section 6).

Some basic terms that the exam asks as short questions:

  • Elasticity: the property of a material to return to its original shape and size when the load is removed. A material is elastic if all its deformation disappears on removal of the load.
  • Plasticity: the property to take a permanent deformation without fracture.
  • Stiffness: the resistance to deformation. For a bar, stiffness = load per unit extension = AE/L (N/mm).
  • Engineering (normal) strain: change in length divided by the original length. True strain uses the current length at each instant.

3. Hooke's law and modulus of elasticity

Hooke's law states: within the elastic limit, stress is directly proportional to strain.

\[ \sigma \propto \varepsilon \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \]

E = modulus of elasticity or Young's modulus (N/mm²). For steel E ≈ 2 × 10⁵ N/mm² (200 GPa), aluminium ≈ 0.7 × 10⁵, copper ≈ 1 × 10⁵, brass ≈ 0.9–1 × 10⁵ N/mm².

A large E means a stiff material: it stretches less under the same stress. Put σ = P/A and ε = δL/L into Hooke's law. You get the most used formula of this unit:

\[ \delta L = \frac{P\,L}{A\,E} \]

Valid when P, A and E are constant along the length L. The product AE is the axial rigidity of the bar.

Quick example. A steel rod 2 m long and 20 mm in diameter carries a pull of 50 kN. Area = 314.2 mm², so σ = 50 000/314.2 = 159.2 N/mm². Strain ε = σ/E = 159.2/(2 × 10⁵) = 7.96 × 10⁻⁴. Extension δL = εL = 7.96 × 10⁻⁴ × 2000 = 1.59 mm.

Real world. A cylinder-head bolt is tightened until it stretches a little; the stretch (PL/AE) sets the clamping force. A lift rope, a crane hook rod and a truss member in a bridge are all designed with this same formula.
Assumptions. All results in this unit assume that the material is homogeneous and isotropic, the load acts along the axis of the bar, the stress stays within the elastic limit, and the stress is uniform over the section. Near the point where a load is applied, or at a sudden step, the real stress is higher (stress concentration); we ignore this.
Units. Keep N and mm everywhere. Then E is in N/mm² and δL comes out in mm. 1 GPa = 10³ N/mm², so 200 GPa = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm².

4. Stress–strain curve of mild steel

We get this curve from a tensile test on a universal testing machine (UTM). The machine pulls a standard specimen slowly until it breaks. It records the load and the extension. Then stress = load / original area, and strain = extension / gauge length.

Learn the points in order (Fig. 1.2):

  • O to A — limit of proportionality. The graph is a straight line. Stress ∝ strain, so Hooke's law holds. The slope of OA is E.
  • A to B — elastic limit. The graph curves a little, but the material is still elastic. If you remove the load at any point up to B, the bar returns to its original length. Beyond B, some strain stays (permanent set).
  • C — upper yield point. The stress at which the material starts to yield.
  • D — lower yield point. The stress falls a little, and the bar then stretches a lot at almost constant stress. This is yielding. Designers use the lower yield stress (about 250 N/mm² for mild steel).
  • D to E — strain hardening. After yielding, the material gets stronger and needs more stress for more strain.
  • E — ultimate stress point. The maximum load divided by the original area. This is the ultimate tensile strength (about 410–470 N/mm² for mild steel).
  • E to F — necking. The area reduces fast at one place (a neck forms). The load falls, so the nominal stress falls.
  • F — breaking point. The specimen breaks. The stress here is the breaking stress.

Use the widget to move along the curve and see each point and its stress.

Engineering stress and true stress. The curve uses the original area, so it shows engineering (nominal) stress. True stress = load / actual area at that instant. True stress keeps rising up to fracture, because the neck area becomes very small.

Ductile and brittle materials. A ductile material (mild steel, copper, aluminium) has a large plastic strain before fracture. It shows necking and a cup-and-cone fracture. Percentage elongation is more than about 5 %. A brittle material (cast iron, concrete, glass) breaks with very little plastic strain. It gives no warning and has a flat fracture.

The tensile test also measures ductility by two numbers. Fit the broken halves together and measure the final gauge length Lf and the smallest diameter at the neck.

\[ \text{Percentage elongation} = \frac{L_f - L_0}{L_0}\times 100 \qquad \text{Percentage reduction in area} = \frac{A_0 - A_f}{A_0}\times 100 \]

L₀, A₀ = original gauge length and area; Af = area at the neck after fracture. Mild steel: about 25 % elongation and 50–60 % reduction in area.

Some metals (aluminium alloys, high-carbon steel) do not show a clear yield point. For them we use the proof stress: draw a line parallel to the elastic line from 0.2 % strain. The stress where it cuts the curve is the 0.2 % proof stress (offset yield strength), Fig. 1.3(b).

5. Working stress and factor of safety

A designer never lets the actual stress reach the failure stress. Loads are uncertain, materials have defects and calculations make assumptions. So we keep the stress at a lower, safe value: the working stress (allowable stress).

\[ \text{Factor of safety (FoS)} = \frac{\text{failure stress}}{\text{working stress}} \]

Failure stress = yield stress for ductile materials, ultimate stress for brittle materials. FoS is always more than 1 (typical 2–4 for static loads on steel).

Example: mild steel with yield stress 250 N/mm² and FoS 2.5 gives a working stress of 250/2.5 = 100 N/mm².

6. Lateral strain, Poisson's ratio and volumetric strain

When you pull a bar, it gets longer and also thinner (Fig. 1.4). When you push it, it gets shorter and fatter.

  • Longitudinal strain = δL/L (along the load).
  • Lateral strain = δd/d or δb/b (at right angles to the load). It always has the opposite sign of the longitudinal strain.

Poisson's ratio (μ or 1/m) is the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain, within the elastic limit:

\[ \mu = \frac{\text{lateral strain}}{\text{longitudinal strain}} \qquad \text{lateral strain} = -\mu\,\varepsilon \]

Steel μ ≈ 0.25–0.33, aluminium ≈ 0.33, rubber ≈ 0.5, cork ≈ 0. The value of μ is between 0 and 0.5.

Quick example. A bar 25 mm in diameter stretches with a longitudinal strain of 6 × 10⁻⁴. With μ = 0.3, the lateral strain is −0.3 × 6 × 10⁻⁴ = −1.8 × 10⁻⁴. The diameter decreases by 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ × 25 = 0.0045 mm.

Volumetric strain is the change in volume per unit original volume, \( \varepsilon_v = \delta V/V \).

Rectangular bar under one axial stress σ: the length strain is ε and both side strains are −με. Volume = L·b·t, so

\[ \varepsilon_v = \frac{\delta V}{V} = \frac{\delta L}{L} + \frac{\delta b}{b} + \frac{\delta t}{t} = \varepsilon(1 - 2\mu) \]

For a circular bar the same result holds: \( \varepsilon_v = \delta L/L + 2\,\delta d/d = \varepsilon(1-2\mu) \).

Cube under three stresses σx, σy, σz (Fig. 1.5). Each stress gives its own strain and also a Poisson effect in the other two directions. Add them (superposition):

\[ \varepsilon_x = \frac{\sigma_x}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_y}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma_z}{E} \]\[ \varepsilon_v = \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \varepsilon_z = \frac{(\sigma_x + \sigma_y + \sigma_z)(1 - 2\mu)}{E} \]

Use + for tensile and − for compressive stresses. εy and εz follow the same pattern.

Bulk modulus (K). Put a body in a fluid under pressure p. The same compressive stress p acts on all faces (hydrostatic stress). Bulk modulus is the ratio of this direct stress to the volumetric strain:

\[ K = \frac{p}{\varepsilon_v} = \frac{p}{\delta V / V} \]

K in N/mm². For steel K ≈ 1.6–1.7 × 10⁵ N/mm².

7. Shear stress, shear strain and modulus of rigidity

Fix the bottom face of a block and push the top face sideways with a force P (Fig. 1.6 b). The shear stress on the top face is τ = P/A. The block changes from a rectangle to a parallelogram. The angle φ by which the right angle changes is the shear strain:

\[ \varphi \approx \tan\varphi = \frac{\delta}{h} \qquad G = \frac{\tau}{\varphi} \]

φ in radian; G = modulus of rigidity (shear modulus), N/mm². For steel G ≈ 0.8 × 10⁵ N/mm².

Complementary shear stress. A shear stress τ on two opposite faces makes a couple. The element would rotate, so the other two faces must carry an equal and opposite couple. Take moments for a block of sides dx, dy and thickness t: τ·(dx·t)·dy = τ′·(dy·t)·dx, so τ′ = τ. Statement: a shear stress on one plane is always accompanied by an equal shear stress on the perpendicular plane, and the pair forms an opposite couple.

8. Elastic constants and their relations

The four elastic constants are E, G, K and μ. Only two of them are independent for an isotropic material. The three relations come from simple geometry. The steps are below; Theory Q4, Q5 and Q6 give the full exam write-up.

(a) E = 2G(1 + μ). Take a square block ABCD of side a in pure shear, with face DC fixed (Fig. 1.7).

  1. The top moves by BB′ = aφ. The diagonal DB stretches by EB′ = BB′ cos 45° = aφ/√2.
  2. Strain of DB = (aφ/√2)/(a√2) = φ/2 = τ/(2G).
  3. Pure shear is the same as tension τ along DB and compression τ along AC. So the strain of DB = τ/E + μτ/E = (τ/E)(1 + μ).
  4. Equate: τ/(2G) = (τ/E)(1 + μ), which gives \( E = 2G(1+\mu) \).

(b) E = 3K(1 − 2μ). Put equal stress σ on all six faces of a cube.

  1. Strain in each direction: \( \varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E}(1 - 2\mu) \).
  2. Volumetric strain = sum of the three strains: \( \varepsilon_v = \frac{3\sigma}{E}(1 - 2\mu) \).
  3. By definition, \( \varepsilon_v = \sigma/K \). Equate the two: \( E = 3K(1 - 2\mu) \).

(c) E = 9KG/(3K + G).

  1. From (a): \( \mu = \frac{E - 2G}{2G} \).
  2. Put this in (b): \( E = 3K\left(\frac{3G - E}{G}\right) \), so \( EG = 9KG - 3KE \).
  3. Collect E: \( E(G + 3K) = 9KG \), so \( E = \frac{9KG}{3K + G} \).

9. Principle of superposition and bars with several loads

Principle of superposition: when a body carries several loads, the total deformation is the algebraic sum of the deformations from each load acting alone. It is valid only within the elastic limit (linear material, small deformation).

For a bar with loads at several sections (Fig. 1.8), use free bodies:

  1. Check equilibrium: the sum of forces to the right = the sum of forces to the left. Find the unknown force P from this.
  2. Cut each segment. Take the part on one side of the cut. The internal force in the segment = algebraic sum of the external forces on that side.
  3. Mark tension (+) and compression (−). Draw the axial force diagram.
  4. Find each δ = NL/(AE) with its sign and add: \( \delta = \sum N_i L_i/(A_i E_i) \).
Common mistake. Do not use the applied load at a section as the force in a segment. Each segment carries the sum of the forces on one side of it. Check your segment force from the other side too: the answer must be the same.

A simple way to find a segment force: stand inside the segment and look to the left. A force at the left end that points away from the segment (to the left) pulls it, so it is tension. A force that points towards the segment pushes it, so it is compression. Add all the forces on the left side with these signs.

10. Bars of varying section

A stepped bar has parts of different areas (Fig. 1.9). The same load P passes through each part, but each part has its own stress and extension. The total extension is the sum:

\[ \delta L = \frac{P}{E}\left(\frac{L_1}{A_1} + \frac{L_2}{A_2} + \frac{L_3}{A_3}\right) \]

If the parts are of different materials, keep E inside: \( \delta L = P\sum L_i/(A_i E_i) \). The maximum stress is in the part with the smallest area.

11. Uniformly tapering circular bar

When the diameter changes linearly from d₁ to d₂ over the length L, the area changes at each section. So we cannot use PL/AE directly. Take a thin strip of length dx at distance x (Fig. 1.10), find its extension and integrate.

  1. Diameter at x: \( d_x = d_1 - kx \), where \( k = (d_1 - d_2)/L \).
  2. Extension of the strip: \( d(\delta) = \dfrac{P\,dx}{E\,(\pi/4)\,d_x^2} = \dfrac{4P\,dx}{\pi E (d_1 - kx)^2} \).
  3. Integrate from 0 to L. The integral of \( (d_1 - kx)^{-2} \) is \( 1/[k(d_1 - kx)] \). Between limits it gives \( \frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{d_2} - \frac{1}{d_1}\right) = \frac{L}{d_1 d_2} \).
\[ \delta L = \frac{4\,P\,L}{\pi\,E\,d_1\,d_2} \]

Check: if d₁ = d₂ = d, it becomes PL/(AE) for a uniform bar. Maximum stress \( 4P/(\pi d_2^2) \) is at the small end.

Change d₁, d₂, L and P in the calculator and compare the extension with a uniform bar of the mean diameter.

Quick example. A steel bar 1 m long tapers from 40 mm to 20 mm and carries 50 kN. δL = 4 × 50 000 × 1000 / (π × 2 × 10⁵ × 40 × 20) = 0.398 mm. A uniform bar of the mean diameter 30 mm gives 0.354 mm, which is 11 % too small.

Mean diameter is wrong. PL/(AE) with the mean diameter (d₁ + d₂)/2 gives a smaller extension than the true value. The formula uses the product d₁d₂, not the square of the mean.

12. Uniformly tapering rectangular bar

A flat plate of constant thickness t has a width that changes linearly from a to b over the length L (Fig. 1.11). Use the same strip method.

  1. Width at x: \( b_x = a - kx \), where \( k = (a - b)/L \). Area of the strip = \( t\,b_x \).
  2. Extension of the strip: \( d(\delta) = \dfrac{P\,dx}{E\,t\,(a - kx)} \).
  3. Integrate from 0 to L: \( \int_0^L \frac{dx}{a - kx} = \frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{a}{b} \).
\[ \delta L = \frac{P\,L}{E\,t\,(a - b)}\,\ln\frac{a}{b} \]

ln = natural logarithm (log to base e). Maximum stress P/(bt) is at the narrow end.

13. Composite bars

A composite bar has two or more bars of different materials that act together and carry one load. Examples: a steel rod inside a copper tube (Fig. 1.12), a reinforced concrete (RCC) column, and rods that share a load through a rigid plate.

The load sharing is statically indeterminate: equilibrium alone gives one equation for two unknowns. So we use two conditions:

  1. Equilibrium: \( P = P_1 + P_2 = \sigma_1 A_1 + \sigma_2 A_2 \).
  2. Compatibility: the bars are joined, so they deform by the same amount: \( \delta_1 = \delta_2 \), that is \( \dfrac{\sigma_1 L_1}{E_1} = \dfrac{\sigma_2 L_2}{E_2} \).
\[ \text{Equal lengths: } \sigma_1 = \frac{E_1}{E_2}\,\sigma_2 = m\,\sigma_2 \qquad \frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{A_1 E_1}{A_2 E_2} \]

m = E₁/E₂ is the modular ratio. With equal lengths the load divides in the ratio of AE. With unequal lengths it divides in the ratio of AE/L.

Method for every composite problem.

  1. Find the area of each material. For a tube, \( A = \frac{\pi}{4}(D^2 - d^2) \).
  2. Write the compatibility equation and express one stress in terms of the other (σ₁ = mσ₂ for equal lengths).
  3. Put it into the equilibrium equation and solve for σ₂, then σ₁.
  4. Find the loads P₁ = σ₁A₁ and P₂ = σ₂A₂. Check that P₁ + P₂ = P.
  5. Find the common deformation δ = σ₁L₁/E₁ and check it with σ₂L₂/E₂.
Common mistake. Do not divide the load in the ratio of areas. The stiffer material always takes more stress. Equal strain, not equal stress, is the rule for bars that are joined.
Real world. In an RCC column, steel bars and concrete shorten together. Steel is about 15 times stiffer, so each mm² of steel carries about 15 times the stress of the concrete. If the steel is too little, the concrete crushes, as in the photo.

14. Extension of a bar under its own weight

A long bar that hangs from one end stretches under its own weight (Fig. 1.13). At distance y from the free end, the force is the weight of the part below: w·A·y. The force is zero at the free end and maximum (W = wAL) at the support.

\[ \delta L = \int_0^L \frac{w A y\,dy}{A E} = \frac{w L^2}{2E} = \frac{W L}{2 A E} \]

w = weight per unit volume (ρg, N/mm³), W = total weight. The extension is half of the value for a load W at the end.

Quick example. A steel wire 10 m long hangs vertically. With ρ = 7850 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s², w = 7.70 × 10⁻⁵ N/mm³. Then δL = 7.70 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 000² / (2 × 2 × 10⁵) = 0.019 mm. The effect is small for short bars, but it matters for long mine-hoist ropes and tall columns.

Model answer

Introduction. A standard mild-steel specimen is loaded in tension in a universal testing machine until it breaks. Load and extension are recorded. Nominal stress σ = P/A₀ is plotted against strain ε = δL/L₀. The curve is shown below.

  1. Limit of proportionality (A). From O to A the curve is a straight line. Stress is proportional to strain and Hooke's law holds. The slope of OA is Young's modulus, E = σ/ε.
  2. Elastic limit (B). Beyond A the curve departs slightly from the straight line, but the material is still elastic up to B. On removal of the load at any stage up to B the specimen regains its original length. Beyond B, a permanent set remains.
  3. Upper yield point (C). The stress at which the material begins to yield. Its value depends on the speed of loading and the shape of the specimen.
  4. Lower yield point (D). The stress drops to D, and the specimen elongates considerably at nearly constant stress (yielding). Strain at the end of yielding is about 10–15 times the elastic strain. The lower yield stress (≈ 250 N/mm²) is taken as the yield strength for design.
  5. Strain hardening (D to E). After yielding, the material offers more resistance, so stress rises with strain. Elongation is uniform along the gauge length.
  6. Ultimate stress point (E). Maximum load / original area = ultimate tensile strength (≈ 410–470 N/mm² for mild steel).
  7. Necking (E to F). The cross-section reduces rapidly at a local zone (neck). The load required falls, so the nominal stress falls.
  8. Breaking point (F). The specimen fractures with a cup-and-cone fracture. Stress at F = breaking stress.

Note. If the actual area at each instant is used (true stress), the curve rises continuously up to fracture. Mild steel is ductile: percentage elongation about 25 %, reduction in area about 50–60 %.

Q2. Derive an expression for the stress and total elongation of a uniformly tapering circular bar of diameter d₁ at one end and d₂ at the other end, length L, under an axial force P.

Nov-Dec 2025 Q1(a) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q1(a) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q2(a)

Sketch with the strip dx, diameter at x, extension of the strip, integration, final result and the check d₁ = d₂.

Model answer

Let P = axial load, L = length, d₁ = diameter at the larger end, d₂ = diameter at the smaller end, E = Young's modulus.

  1. The diameter varies linearly. At distance x from the larger end: \[ d_x = d_1 - \frac{d_1 - d_2}{L}\,x = d_1 - kx, \qquad k = \frac{d_1 - d_2}{L} \]
  2. Area of the section at x: \( A_x = \dfrac{\pi}{4}(d_1 - kx)^2 \).
  3. Stress at the section: \[ \sigma_x = \frac{P}{A_x} = \frac{4P}{\pi (d_1 - kx)^2} \] Maximum stress \( 4P/(\pi d_2^2) \) at the smaller end; minimum \( 4P/(\pi d_1^2) \) at the larger end.
  4. Consider a strip of length dx at x. The area is nearly constant over dx, so its extension is \[ d(\delta L) = \frac{P\,dx}{A_x E} = \frac{4P\,dx}{\pi E (d_1 - kx)^2} \]
  5. Total extension: \[ \delta L = \frac{4P}{\pi E}\int_0^L (d_1 - kx)^{-2}\,dx = \frac{4P}{\pi E}\left[\frac{1}{k(d_1 - kx)}\right]_0^L \]
  6. Substitute limits: at x = L, d₁ − kL = d₂; at x = 0 it is d₁. \[ \delta L = \frac{4P}{\pi E k}\left(\frac{1}{d_2} - \frac{1}{d_1}\right) = \frac{4P}{\pi E}\cdot\frac{L}{d_1 - d_2}\cdot\frac{d_1 - d_2}{d_1 d_2} \]
\[ \delta L = \frac{4\,P\,L}{\pi\,E\,d_1\,d_2} \]

Check. For a uniform bar d₁ = d₂ = d: δL = 4PL/(πEd²) = PL/(AE), which is the known result.

Q3. Derive an expression for the change in length of a bar of uniform thickness t whose width varies uniformly from a to b over a length L (tapering rectangular bar).

Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q3(a)

Same strip method as Q2, but the area is t × bₓ, so the integral gives a natural logarithm.

Model answer

Let P = axial load, L = length, t = thickness (constant), a = width at the larger end, b = width at the smaller end, E = Young's modulus.

  1. Width at distance x from the larger end: \( b_x = a - \dfrac{a - b}{L}x = a - kx \), with \( k = (a - b)/L \).
  2. Area at x: \( A_x = t\,(a - kx) \). Stress at x: \( \sigma_x = P/[t(a - kx)] \).
  3. Extension of a strip of length dx: \[ d(\delta L) = \frac{P\,dx}{E\,t\,(a - kx)} \]
  4. Total extension: \[ \delta L = \frac{P}{Et}\int_0^L \frac{dx}{a - kx} = \frac{P}{Et}\left[-\frac{1}{k}\ln(a - kx)\right]_0^L \]
  5. Limits: at x = L, a − kL = b. \[ \delta L = \frac{P}{Etk}\left[\ln a - \ln b\right] = \frac{P}{Et}\cdot\frac{L}{a - b}\,\ln\frac{a}{b} \]
\[ \delta L = \frac{P\,L}{E\,t\,(a - b)}\,\ln\frac{a}{b} \]

ln is log to base e (= 2.303 log₁₀).

Q4. Derive the relation between Young's modulus E, modulus of rigidity G and Poisson's ratio μ.

Aug-Sep 2023 Q2(a) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q2(a) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q2(a)

Square block in pure shear; strain of diagonal BD found two ways (geometry and stresses); equate. Final E = 2G(1 + μ).

Model answer

Consider a square block ABCD of side a and unit thickness. The face DC is fixed. A shear stress τ acts on faces AB and DC, and the complementary shear stress τ acts on faces AD and BC. The block deforms to A′B′CD; the shear strain is φ.

Step 1: strain of diagonal BD from geometry.

  1. Displacement of B: BB′ = a·φ (φ is small).
  2. Draw BE perpendicular to DB′. Because φ is small, DE ≈ DB, so the increase in length of the diagonal is EB′.
  3. The angle DB′A′ ≈ 45°, so EB′ = BB′ cos 45° = aφ/√2.
  4. Original length DB = a√2. Strain of the diagonal: \[ \varepsilon_{BD} = \frac{EB'}{DB} = \frac{a\varphi/\sqrt{2}}{a\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\varphi}{2} \]
  5. Since G = τ/φ, \[ \varepsilon_{BD} = \frac{\tau}{2G} \qquad (1) \]

Step 2: strain of diagonal BD from the stresses.

  1. Pure shear τ on the faces is equivalent to a direct tensile stress τ along diagonal BD and a direct compressive stress τ along diagonal AC (at 90° to BD).
  2. Strain along BD = strain from the tension + strain from the Poisson effect of the compression on AC: \[ \varepsilon_{BD} = \frac{\tau}{E} + \mu\frac{\tau}{E} = \frac{\tau}{E}(1 + \mu) \qquad (2) \]

Step 3: equate (1) and (2).

\[ \frac{\tau}{2G} = \frac{\tau}{E}(1 + \mu) \]

\[ E = 2G(1 + \mu) \]

Note. Since 0 ≤ μ ≤ 0.5, G lies between E/3 and E/2. For steel, E = 2 × 10⁵ and μ = 0.3 give G = 0.77 × 10⁵ N/mm².

Q5. Derive the relation between modulus of elasticity E and bulk modulus K. Hence show that Poisson's ratio cannot exceed 0.5.

Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q3(a)

Cube under equal stress σ on all six faces; volumetric strain = sum of three linear strains; use K = σ/εv.

Model answer

Consider a cube of side L under equal tensile stress σ on all six faces (σx = σy = σz = σ).

  1. Strain in the x-direction (own stress minus the Poisson effects of the other two): \[ \varepsilon_x = \frac{\sigma}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma}{E} - \mu\frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{\sigma}{E}(1 - 2\mu) \]
  2. By symmetry, εy = εz = εx.
  3. Volume V = L³. So δV = 3L²·δL and the volumetric strain is \[ \varepsilon_v = \frac{\delta V}{V} = 3\frac{\delta L}{L} = 3\varepsilon_x = \frac{3\sigma}{E}(1 - 2\mu) \]
  4. By definition of bulk modulus, \( K = \sigma/\varepsilon_v \), so \( \varepsilon_v = \sigma/K \).
  5. Equate the two expressions of εv: \[ \frac{\sigma}{K} = \frac{3\sigma}{E}(1 - 2\mu) \]
\[ E = 3K(1 - 2\mu) \]

Limit of μ. E and K are positive for every real material. So (1 − 2μ) > 0, that is μ < 0.5. When μ = 0.5, K is infinite: the material is incompressible (rubber is close to this).

General result. For any three stresses, \( \varepsilon_v = (\sigma_x + \sigma_y + \sigma_z)(1 - 2\mu)/E \), and for a bar under one stress, \( \varepsilon_v = \varepsilon(1 - 2\mu) \).

Q6. Derive the relationship between Young's modulus, modulus of rigidity and bulk modulus. Also express Poisson's ratio in terms of K and G.

Jun-Jul 2024 Q2(a)

State (and briefly prove, or quote from Q4 and Q5) the two relations, then eliminate μ. Show the algebra in steps.

Model answer

Known relations (derive them as in Q4 and Q5 if the question asks):

\[ E = 2G(1 + \mu) \quad (1) \qquad\qquad E = 3K(1 - 2\mu) \quad (2) \]

  1. From (1): \( \mu = \dfrac{E}{2G} - 1 = \dfrac{E - 2G}{2G} \).
  2. Substitute into (2): \[ E = 3K\left(1 - \frac{2(E - 2G)}{2G}\right) = 3K\left(\frac{G - E + 2G}{G}\right) = \frac{3K(3G - E)}{G} \]
  3. Multiply by G: \( EG = 9KG - 3KE \).
  4. Collect the E terms: \( E(G + 3K) = 9KG \).
\[ E = \frac{9KG}{3K + G} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{9}{E} = \frac{3}{G} + \frac{1}{K} \]

Poisson's ratio in terms of K and G. Equate (1) and (2): 2G(1 + μ) = 3K(1 − 2μ). So 2G + 2Gμ = 3K − 6Kμ, which gives

\[ \mu = \frac{3K - 2G}{6K + 2G} \]

Check with steel. K = 1.67 × 10⁵ and G = 0.77 × 10⁵ N/mm² give E = 9(1.67)(0.77)/(5.01 + 0.77) × 10⁵ = 2.0 × 10⁵ N/mm² and μ = 0.30.

Q7. Write short notes on: (i) Modulus of elasticity (ii) Modulus of rigidity (iii) Bulk modulus (iv) Poisson's ratio (v) Complementary shear stress (vi) Principle of superposition (vii) Volumetric strain.

Nov 2024 Q1(b) · Jan 2025 Q2(a)

Two marks each: definition, formula with symbols, unit, one typical value or sketch.

Model answer
  1. Modulus of elasticity (E). Within the elastic limit, the ratio of normal (tensile or compressive) stress to the corresponding linear strain: E = σ/ε. Unit N/mm². It measures stiffness in tension or compression. Steel: 2 × 10⁵ N/mm².
  2. Modulus of rigidity (G). Within the elastic limit, the ratio of shear stress to shear strain: G = τ/φ, where φ is the change in the right angle in radian. Unit N/mm². Steel: 0.8 × 10⁵ N/mm².
  3. Bulk modulus (K). When a body is under equal direct stress p on all faces, the ratio of this stress to the volumetric strain: K = p/(δV/V). Unit N/mm². It measures resistance to change in volume. Steel: about 1.67 × 10⁵ N/mm².
  4. Poisson's ratio (μ). Within the elastic limit, the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain: μ = (δd/d)/(δL/L). No unit. Value 0 to 0.5; steel 0.25–0.3.
  5. Complementary shear stress. A shear stress on a plane is always accompanied by a shear stress of equal magnitude on the perpendicular plane. The two pairs form equal and opposite couples, so the element is in equilibrium (τ′ = τ).
  6. Principle of superposition. When a body is under several loads, the resultant deformation (or stress) is the algebraic sum of the deformations caused by each load acting separately. It holds only within the elastic limit, where deformation is proportional to load. Use: δ = Σ NᵢLᵢ/(AᵢEᵢ) for a bar with loads at several sections.
  7. Volumetric strain. The ratio of change in volume to original volume, εv = δV/V. For a bar under axial stress σ: εv = ε(1 − 2μ). For three mutually perpendicular stresses: εv = (σx + σy + σz)(1 − 2μ)/E. Under hydrostatic pressure p: εv = p/K.

Q8. What is a composite bar? Derive expressions for the stresses in the two materials and the load shared by each when a composite bar carries an axial load P. Define modular ratio.

Syllabus topic

Sketch, two conditions (equilibrium and equal deformation), final formulas, and the note that the stiffer material takes more stress.

Model answer

Definition. A composite bar is a bar made of two or more different materials, joined so that they deform together and share one external load. Examples: a steel rod inside a copper tube, an RCC column, a timber post with steel plates.

Let P = total load; A₁, E₁, σ₁, P₁ for material 1 and A₂, E₂, σ₂, P₂ for material 2; both of length L.

  1. Equilibrium: \[ P = P_1 + P_2 = \sigma_1 A_1 + \sigma_2 A_2 \quad (1) \]
  2. Compatibility: both bars shorten by the same amount: \[ \frac{\sigma_1 L}{E_1} = \frac{\sigma_2 L}{E_2} \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_1 = \frac{E_1}{E_2}\sigma_2 = m\,\sigma_2 \quad (2) \]
  3. Substitute (2) in (1): \[ P = \sigma_2\left(\frac{E_1}{E_2}A_1 + A_2\right) \]
\[ \sigma_2 = \frac{P\,E_2}{A_1E_1 + A_2E_2} \qquad \sigma_1 = \frac{P\,E_1}{A_1E_1 + A_2E_2} \]\[ P_1 = \frac{A_1E_1}{A_1E_1 + A_2E_2}\,P \qquad P_2 = \frac{A_2E_2}{A_1E_1 + A_2E_2}\,P \]

Common deformation δ = σ₁L/E₁ = PL/(A₁E₁ + A₂E₂).

Modular ratio. The ratio of the Young's moduli of the two materials, m = E₁/E₂. The stresses are in the ratio m, and the loads are shared in the ratio of the axial rigidities A₁E₁ : A₂E₂.

Unequal lengths. If the bars have lengths L₁ and L₂ (for example rods standing on blocks), compatibility becomes σ₁L₁/E₁ = σ₂L₂/E₂, and the loads divide in the ratio A₁E₁/L₁ : A₂E₂/L₂.

Numericals

Method. (1) Sketch the bar and write all data in N and mm. (2) Check equilibrium and find any unknown force. (3) Find the force in each segment from a free body (+ tension, − compression). (4) Find the area of each part. (5) Stress σ = N/A in each part. (6) Extension δ = NL/(AE) for each part (use 4PL/(πEd₁d₂) for a tapered part), with its sign; add them. (7) For composite bars, write equilibrium and compatibility first. (8) Box the answer with units.
