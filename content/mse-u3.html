1. Classification of engineering materials

Engineers group materials by bonding and structure. The four main classes are metals, ceramics, polymers and composites. Fig. 3.1 shows the tree.

Metals divide into ferrous (iron is the base) and non-ferrous (no iron base). Ferrous metals are cheap and strong. Non-ferrous metals are light, corrosion resistant or good conductors.

Purpose of alloying. A pure metal is usually soft and weak. We add other elements to raise strength and hardness, improve toughness, corrosion and wear resistance, change hardenability, and make casting easier.

4. Steels

Steel is an Fe–C alloy with up to 2.1 % C. Plain carbon steels contain only carbon, with small amounts of Mn and Si. Alloy steels contain deliberate additions of Cr, Ni, Mo, V, W and others.

Steel% CPropertiesUses
Low carbon (mild)0.05–0.30Soft, ductile, weldableSheets, wires, bolts, structures
Medium carbon0.30–0.60Strong, tough; heat treatableShafts, gears, axles, rails
High carbon0.60–1.50Hard, wear resistant, brittleChisels, springs, files, dies
  • Tool steel: high carbon (0.7–1.5 %) with W, Cr, V, Mo. It keeps hardness and sharpness at high temperature. Uses: drills, taps, dies, cutters. High-speed steel (18 W–4 Cr–1 V) cuts at red heat.
  • Molybdenum steel: 0.15–0.5 % Mo (Cr–Mo steel). Mo raises hardenability and creep strength and prevents temper brittleness. Uses: boiler tubes, crankshafts, turbine parts, aircraft frames.
  • Chromium steel: 0.5–2 % Cr. Cr forms hard carbides, so wear resistance and hardenability rise. Uses: ball bearings, gears, dies, axles.
  • Stainless steel: at least 11.5 % Cr. A thin Cr₂O₃ film protects the surface. Types: austenitic (18 Cr–8 Ni, non-magnetic, best corrosion resistance), ferritic (Cr only, magnetic) and martensitic (hardenable, for cutlery and blades). Uses: food equipment, chemical plant, surgical tools.

8. Composites: definition and classification

A composite is a material made of two or more different materials, with a clear interface between them. It has properties that none of its parts has alone. The continuous phase is the matrix. The dispersed phase is the reinforcement.

  • Matrix: holds the fibres, transfers load to them, protects them and gives shape.
  • Reinforcement: carries most of the load and gives strength and stiffness.

Matrix types: PMC (epoxy, polyester, nylon), MMC (Al, Mg, Ti) and CMC (Al₂O₃, SiC, Si₃N₄). Reinforcement types: glass, carbon and aramid fibres, SiC and Al₂O₃ particles or whiskers, and boron fibres.

  • Particle: concrete (cement + gravel), cermet WC–Co tools, carbon black in tyres.
  • Fibre: continuous (aligned, strongest along the fibre) or discontinuous (short, random).
  • Structural: a laminate has stacked plies; a sandwich has strong skins on a light core.

9. Production of FRP composites

Every process does three jobs: place the fibre, wet it with resin, and cure. Q10, Q11 give full answers.

  • Hand lay-up (Fig. 3.5): plies of mat are laid in an open mould, wetted with resin and rolled. It is simple and cheap. It is slow and the fibre content is low.
  • Filament winding (Fig. 3.6): resin-wet roving is wound on a rotating mandrel. It makes pipes, tanks and rocket cases.
  • Bag moulding (Fig. 3.7): a wet lay-up is sealed under a bag. Vacuum or pressure removes air and extra resin.
  • Pultrusion (Fig. 3.8): fibres are pulled through a resin bath and a heated die. It makes rods, beams and tubes of constant section.

10. Production of MMCs

  • Stir casting (Fig. 3.9): preheated particles are stirred into molten alloy. The slurry is cast. It is cheap and suits large parts. Particles may settle or clump.
  • Powder metallurgy (Fig. 3.10): metal and reinforcement powders are blended, pressed in a die and sintered. The distribution of particles is uniform. The method suits small parts.

11. Hybrid composites, advantages and applications

A hybrid composite has two or more kinds of reinforcement in one matrix, for example carbon and glass fibre in epoxy. One fibre covers the weakness of the other.

Q3. Discuss grey cast iron: composition, properties and uses.

MSE QB Q29 · Jan 2025 Q5(a) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS)

Composition with numbers; flake graphite sketch; properties each with a reason; five uses.

Model answer

Grey cast iron is the most widely used cast iron. It has a grey fracture because of the graphite flakes.

Composition (wt %): C 2.5–4.0, Si 1.0–3.0, Mn 0.4–1.0, S up to 0.1, P 0.1–1.0, balance Fe.

Microstructure: graphite flakes in a matrix of pearlite (or ferrite). Si helps graphite to form. Slow cooling also helps.

  • Strength: UTS 100–400 MPa (low). The flakes act as internal notches.
  • Compression: 3–4 times the tensile strength. The flakes close under compression.
  • Ductility: below 1 %. The iron is brittle.
  • Damping: excellent (10–20 times steel). Flake surfaces absorb vibration.
  • Machinability: excellent. Graphite lubricates the tool and breaks the chip.
  • Castability: excellent. The melt is fluid and shrinkage is low.
  • Wear resistance good; weldability poor.

Uses: machine-tool beds and frames (damping), engine blocks and cylinder heads, brake drums, flywheels, gear housings, pipes and manhole covers, pump and valve bodies.

Q8. Define composite material and give a brief classification of composites. Enumerate the advantages, disadvantages and applications of composites.

MSE QB Q24 · MSE QB Q25 · Jun-Jul 2024

Definition, matrix and reinforcement roles, classification sketch, then three lists.

Model answer

Definition. A composite is a material made by combining two or more chemically different materials, with a distinct interface between them, so that the result has properties better than each part alone.

  • Matrix (continuous phase): binds the reinforcement, transfers load, protects it from damage and environment, gives shape.
  • Reinforcement (dispersed phase): carries the load; gives strength and stiffness.

Classification. By matrix: PMC, MMC, CMC. By reinforcement: particle, fibre (continuous or discontinuous), structural (laminate, sandwich).

Advantages:

  • very high strength-to-weight and stiffness-to-weight ratio
  • properties can be tailored by fibre type and direction
  • good fatigue and corrosion resistance
  • can make large, complex parts in one piece
  • low thermal expansion, good damping

Disadvantages:

  • high cost of fibres and processing
  • anisotropic properties
  • low impact strength; delamination
  • hard to repair and to recycle
  • slow production for high-grade parts

Applications: aircraft structures and rocket cases; wind-turbine blades; racing cars and bikes; boat hulls; pressure vessels and pipes; sports goods; artificial limbs; brake discs; bullet-proof armour.

Q10. With a neat sketch explain the production of composites by (a) filament winding and (b) bag moulding.

MSE QB Q41 · Nov-Dec 2025 Q5(b)

Sketch each, give the process steps in order, then advantages and uses.

Model answer

(a) Filament winding.

  1. Continuous fibre rovings are pulled from spools through a resin bath.
  2. The wet roving passes through a guide eye on a traversing carriage.
  3. The mandrel rotates; the carriage moves along it. The winding angle depends on carriage speed to mandrel speed.
  4. Layers are wound until the thickness is correct. The resin is cured in an oven or by rotating in heat.
  5. The mandrel is removed (collapsible or dissolved).

Advantages: high fibre content (60–80 %), fibre in the correct direction, automatic, repeatable, low material waste. Limits: only convex shapes; poor outer finish; mandrel cost. Uses: pipes, tanks, pressure vessels, rocket motor cases, drive shafts.

(b) Bag moulding (vacuum bag).

  1. Apply release agent and gel coat to the open mould.
  2. Lay fibre plies and wet them with resin (a wet lay-up).
  3. Cover with peel ply, bleeder (absorbs extra resin) and breather (spreads vacuum).
  4. Seal a flexible bag over the stack with sealant tape and connect the vacuum port to a pump.
  5. Vacuum presses the bag against the laminate, removes air and extra resin. Cure at room temperature or in an oven (pressure bag or autoclave also used).
  6. Remove the bag and the plies, then demould.

Advantages: higher fibre content and strength than hand lay-up; fewer voids; better surface and thickness control. Limits: more labour and cost than hand lay-up; only one good face. Uses: aircraft parts, boat hulls, racing-car panels.

Q11. Explain the pultrusion technique with a neat sketch and list its advantages. Explain hand lay-up with a neat sketch.

Nov-Dec 2025 Q6(a) · Nov 2024 Q5(b) · Nov 2024 Q6(a)

Two sketches. Pultrusion: fibre, bath, die, pullers. Hand lay-up: mould, gel coat, plies, roller.

Model answer

Pultrusion. A continuous process to make constant-section FRP profiles. The fibre is pulled (not pushed) through the line.

  1. Fibre rovings or mats are pulled from the creel.
  2. They pass through a resin bath (wet-out).
  3. A pre-former sets the fibre position and squeezes out extra resin.
  4. The heated die shapes the section and cures the resin.
  5. Pullers draw out the cured profile; a saw cuts it to length.

Advantages: continuous, fast and automatic; low labour cost; high fibre content and strength along the length; good surface; little waste. Limits: constant section only; high die cost. Uses: rods, beams, tubes, ladder rails, cable trays, window profiles.

Hand lay-up. The oldest and simplest open-mould process.

  1. Clean the mould; apply release agent and a gel coat.
  2. Place a layer of mat or cloth in the mould.
  3. Pour or brush on catalysed resin to wet the fibre.
  4. Roll the layer to remove air and make it compact.
  5. Repeat until the thickness is right. Cure at room temperature, then demould and trim.

Advantages: low tooling cost; any size or shape; simple. Limits: slow, skilled labour, fibre content only 30–40 %, only one smooth face, uneven quality. Uses: boat hulls, tanks, roofing sheets.

Q12. Briefly explain metal matrix composites and ceramic matrix composites. Explain the production of MMCs (stir casting, powder metallurgy) with a neat sketch.

MSE QB Q42 · Aug-Sep 2023 Q5(b) · Jan 2025 Q5(b) · Nov 2024 Q6(b)

Define MMC and CMC with matrix, reinforcement, merits, uses; then two sketched routes.

Model answer

MMC. A metal (Al, Mg, Ti, Cu) matrix with ceramic reinforcement (SiC, Al₂O₃, C fibres or particles). Merits: higher strength and stiffness, wear resistance, high service temperature, low thermal expansion, good conductivity. Demerits: costly, heavier than PMC, poor wetting, reaction at the interface. Uses: pistons, cylinder liners, brake discs, heat sinks, aerospace parts.

CMC. A ceramic matrix (Al₂O₃, SiC, Si₃N₄) with ceramic fibres or whiskers. The fibres stop crack growth, so the usually brittle ceramic becomes tough. Merits: very high temperature use, light, oxidation and wear resistance. Demerits: costly, difficult to make. Uses: gas-turbine parts, rocket nozzles, brake discs, heat shields.

(a) Stir casting.

  1. Melt the matrix alloy in a crucible.
  2. Preheat the reinforcement particles (500–800 °C) to remove moisture and improve wetting.
  3. Stir the melt to make a vortex and add the particles slowly into it.
  4. Keep stirring to spread the particles evenly.
  5. Pour into a preheated mould and let it solidify.

Merit: simple, cheap, large parts. Demerit: settling and clumping of particles, porosity.

(b) Powder metallurgy.

  1. Blend metal powder and reinforcement.
  2. Compact in a die under high pressure to a green compact.
  3. Sinter below the melting point of the matrix.
  4. Finish (sizing, extrusion or forging) if needed.

Merit: uniform distribution; high reinforcement content. Demerit: costly; limited size.

Q13. What are hybrid composites? List their applications.

MSE QB Q45

Definition, why hybridise, types, advantages, applications table.

Model answer

Definition. A hybrid composite has two or more different kinds of reinforcement (or matrix) in one composite, so that the strength of one makes up for the weakness of the other.

Why: carbon fibre is stiff but brittle and costly; glass is cheap but less stiff; aramid is tough but weak in compression. A hybrid gives a balance of stiffness, toughness and cost.

Types: inter-ply (different plies), intra-ply (mixed in one ply), sandwich, selective reinforcement.

Advantages: balanced properties, lower cost than all-carbon, better impact tolerance, tailored thermal expansion. Limits: complex design and manufacture, delamination.
