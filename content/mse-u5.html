Solid Solutions and Phase Diagrams

A phase diagram is the map of an alloy: it shows which phases exist at each temperature and composition. This unit goes from solid solutions to the iron–carbon diagram, then to the TTT and CCT diagrams that link structure to heat treatment. The Fe–C diagram is the most important sketch in the course.

  • Solid solutions: types, rules governing the formation of solid solutions
  • Phase diagrams: basic terms, phase rule, cooling curves, construction of phase diagrams, interpretation of equilibrium diagrams, types of phase diagrams, lever rule
  • Iron–carbon equilibrium diagram: phases in the Fe–C system, invariant reactions, critical temperatures, microstructure of slowly cooled steels, effect of alloying elements on the Fe–C diagram, ferrite and austenite stabilisers
  • TTT diagram for hypo- and hyper-eutectoid steels, effect of alloying elements, CCT diagram

Concepts

Exam pattern. One 20-mark question, two 10-mark parts. Typical parts: Fe–C diagram, TTT diagram, types of solid solution, phase rule, lever rule, cooling curve. Practise drawing the Fe–C diagram.

1. Solid solutions

A solid solution is a solid in which the solute atoms dissolve in the crystal lattice of the solvent. It is one phase with one crystal structure, like sugar in water. The element in larger amount is the solvent; the other is the solute.

  • Substitutional: solute atoms replace solvent atoms on lattice sites. The atoms are of similar size. Examples: Cu–Ni, Cu–Zn.
  • Interstitial: small solute atoms (H, C, N, B) sit in the gaps between large solvent atoms. Example: carbon in iron (ferrite and austenite).
  • Disordered or ordered (Fig. 5.2): in a disordered solution solute atoms sit at random sites. In an ordered solution (superlattice) they take fixed sites, for example Cu₃Au below about 390 °C.

Solute atoms distort the lattice and block dislocations, so a solid solution is stronger than the pure metal (solid-solution strengthening).

2. Hume-Rothery rules (QB Q35)

These rules decide if two metals form a substitutional solution with wide solubility.

  1. Size factor: atomic radii differ by less than about 15 %.
  2. Crystal structure: solute and solvent have the same crystal structure.
  3. Electronegativity: similar for both metals. A large difference gives a compound instead.
  4. Relative valency: a metal of lower valency dissolves more easily in one of higher valency.
\[ \text{size difference} = \frac{\lvert r_{solute} - r_{solvent}\rvert}{r_{solvent}} \times 100 \;<\; 15\,\% \]

Cu and Ni meet all four rules (both FCC, radii 0.128 and 0.125 nm), so they mix in all proportions. An interstitial solute must be much smaller than the solvent atom (radius ratio about 0.6 or less).

3. Basic terms (QB Q33)

  • System: the part of the universe chosen for study, such as an Fe–C alloy.
  • Component: an element or stable compound that makes the system (Fe and Fe₃C in the Fe–C diagram).
  • Phase: a homogeneous, physically distinct part with its own structure and composition, separated by a boundary.
  • Equilibrium: the state of minimum free energy, which does not change with time.
  • Melting point: the temperature at which a solid becomes liquid on heating. Freezing point: the temperature at which a liquid becomes solid on cooling. They are equal for a pure metal; an alloy melts and freezes over a range.

4. Gibbs phase rule

F is the number of variables we may change without changing the number of phases. At constant pressure (usual for metals):

\[ F = C - P + 1 \]

F = degrees of freedom, C = number of components, P = number of phases present. (With pressure also variable: F = C − P + 2.)

StateCPF
Pure metal, all liquid111
Pure metal, freezing (L + S)120 (fixed temperature)
Binary alloy, one phase212
Binary alloy, two phases221
Eutectic, eutectoid, peritectic230
Result. F cannot be negative, so a binary alloy has at most 3 phases in equilibrium, and only on a horizontal line.

5. Cooling curves (QB Q17)

A cooling curve plots temperature against time as a molten metal cools slowly. A change of slope or a flat arrest shows a phase change, because latent heat is released.

  • Pure metal: the temperature stays flat at the freezing point Tm while the metal freezes (heat lost equals latent heat released, F = 0).
  • Solid-solution alloy: no flat arrest. The slope changes at the liquidus Tl (freezing starts) and at the solidus Ts (freezing ends). F = 1 between them.
  • Eutectic alloy: one flat arrest, like a pure metal.

6. Construction of a phase diagram

  1. Prepare alloys of known composition (Cu–Ni: 0, 25, 50, 75, 100 % Ni).
  2. Melt each and cool it very slowly. Record temperature against time.
  3. Mark the break points and arrests on each cooling curve.
  4. Plot each point at its temperature (y) and alloy composition (x).
  5. Join the upper points (liquidus) and the lower points (solidus).

7. Interpreting a diagram: tie line and lever rule

Mark the alloy composition C0 and temperature. In a two-phase field draw a horizontal tie line through the point. Its ends on the phase boundaries give the compositions of the two phases. The lever rule gives their amounts.

\[ W_L = \frac{C_\alpha - C_0}{C_\alpha - C_L}, \qquad W_\alpha = \frac{C_0 - C_L}{C_\alpha - C_L}, \qquad W_L + W_\alpha = 1 \]

Each phase fraction is the length of the opposite arm divided by the full tie line.

Memory trick. Use the arm on the other side of the alloy point.

Example 1 (Cu–Ni, Fig. 5.5). An alloy of 35 % Ni at 1250 °C has CL = 32 % and Cα = 42 %. WL = (42 − 35)/(42 − 32) = 0.70 and Wα = (35 − 32)/(42 − 32) = 0.30. So 70 % liquid and 30 % solid α.

Example 2 (0.4 % C steel just below 727 °C). Ferrite 0.022 % C, pearlite 0.8 % C. Wα = (0.8 − 0.4)/(0.8 − 0.022) = 0.514; pearlite = 0.486.

Example 3 (1.2 % C steel, just below 727 °C). Proeutectoid cementite = (1.2 − 0.8)/(6.67 − 0.8) = 6.8 %.

10. Microstructure of slowly cooled steels

  • Hypo-eutectoid (below 0.8 % C): ferrite forms on austenite grain boundaries below A₃. The rest becomes pearlite at 727 °C. Result: ferrite + pearlite.
  • Eutectoid (0.8 % C): all austenite becomes pearlite at 727 °C.
  • Hyper-eutectoid (0.8–2.11 % C): cementite forms on grain boundaries below Acm. The rest becomes pearlite. Result: pearlite + cementite network (brittle).

Cast irons. Hypo-eutectic: primary austenite + ledeburite. Eutectic (4.3 % C): all ledeburite. Hyper-eutectic: primary cementite + ledeburite.

11. Effect of alloying elements; ferrite and austenite stabilisers

Alloying elements dissolve in ferrite and austenite or form carbides. They change the critical temperatures and the eutectoid composition.

GroupElementsEffect on Fe–C diagram
Austenite stabilisersNi, Mn, Co, Cu (and C, N)Widen the γ field. Lower A₃ and A₁. Enough Ni gives austenite at room temperature (18-8 stainless).
Ferrite stabilisersCr, Mo, W, V, Si, Al, TiClose the γ loop. Raise A₁ and A₃. High Cr gives fully ferritic steel.

12. TTT diagram and CCT diagram

The TTT (time–temperature–transformation) diagram shows how fast austenite transforms at constant (isothermal) temperature. The left C-curve is the start and the right curve the finish. Above the nose (about 550 °C) the product is pearlite; below it, bainite; below Ms, martensite.

Hypo- and hyper-eutectoid steels (Fig. 5.12). The diagram is similar, with three differences:

  • An extra curve shows where proeutectoid ferrite (hypo) or cementite (hyper) starts. It begins at A₃ or Acm and joins the pearlite curve at the nose.
  • The noses move left: the austenite is less stable than in eutectoid steel.
  • Ms falls as carbon rises (higher for 0.4 % C, lower for 1.2 % C).

Alloying elements (except Co) move the TTT curves to the right, which raises hardenability, and lower Ms.

CCT diagram. Real parts cool continuously. The CCT curve lies at longer times and lower temperatures than the TTT curve. Slow cooling gives pearlite; cooling faster than the critical rate gives martensite (see Unit 4).

Theory questions

Q1. Define solid solution. Describe the classification of solid solutions with neat sketches.

MSE QB Q40 · Aug-Sep 2023 · Jan 2023 (CBCS) · Apr 2022 (CBCS) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS)

Definition; substitutional, interstitial (sketch); ordered, disordered (sketch); examples; effect on strength.

Model answer

Solid solution. A solid solution is a solid phase in which the atoms of the solute are dissolved in the crystal lattice of the solvent. The crystal structure of the solvent is kept and the alloy is one phase (homogeneous).

Classification.

  1. Substitutional solid solution. Solute atoms replace solvent atoms at lattice sites. It needs similar atomic sizes (Hume-Rothery rules). Examples: Cu–Ni (complete), Cu–Zn up to about 39 % Zn, Ag–Au.
  2. Interstitial solid solution. Small solute atoms (H, C, N, B, O) sit in the gaps. Solvent atoms are much larger. Example: carbon in γ-iron (austenite, max 2.11 % C) and in α-iron (ferrite, max 0.022 % C). Solubility is always limited.

Ordered and disordered. In a disordered (random) solution the solute atoms occupy sites at random. In an ordered solution (superlattice) the solute atoms occupy fixed sites; it forms on slow cooling below a critical temperature. Examples: Cu₃Au, CuZn (β′ brass).

By solubility. Complete (unlimited) solid solutions exist when all rules are met (Cu–Ni). Limited (partial) solid solutions exist otherwise (Cu–Zn, Fe–C).

Properties. The solute atoms distort the lattice and obstruct dislocation motion. So a solid solution has higher strength and hardness, but lower ductility and electrical conductivity, than the pure solvent (solid-solution strengthening).

Q4. Explain with a cooling curve the process of solidification of a pure metal. Distinguish homogeneous and heterogeneous nucleation.

MSE QB Q17 · Nov 2024 · Jun-Jul 2024 · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS)

Neat cooling curve with Tm, the four stages with the reason for the flat arrest, then the nucleation table.

Model answer

A pure metal freezes at one fixed temperature, the freezing point Tm. A cooling curve records temperature against time during slow cooling.

  1. Liquid cools. Heat is lost to the mould; the temperature falls steadily. Only liquid is present (F = 1).
  2. Nucleation. At or a little below Tm (undercooling), the first tiny solid nuclei form.
  3. Growth (arrest). Nuclei grow into crystals, usually as dendrites. Latent heat of fusion is released and equals the heat lost, so the temperature stays constant. L and S are both present, so F = 1 − 2 + 1 = 0. The length of the arrest depends on the mass and the cooling rate.
  4. Solid cools. When the last liquid has frozen, the solid cools down to room temperature.

The crystals meet and form grains; their boundaries are the grain boundaries. Fast cooling gives fine grains; slow cooling gives coarse grains.

PointHomogeneous nucleationHeterogeneous nucleation
WhereInside the pure liquid, by chance clusters of atomsOn surfaces: mould wall, impurities, added inoculants
UndercoolingLarge (about 0.2 Tm)Small (a few degrees)
Energy barrierHighLow, because the surface helps
OccurrenceRare; only in very clean liquidCommon in practice
UseLaboratory onlyGrain refinement by inoculants (Ti, B in Al)

Q5. State the Gibbs phase rule and explain it with an example. Explain the lever rule with an example.

Aug-Sep 2023 · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) · Nov 2024

Statement and formula; table of examples; tie line and lever formula; one worked Cu–Ni example and one Fe–C example.

Model answer

Phase rule. For a system in equilibrium, the number of degrees of freedom is the number of independent variables (temperature, pressure, composition) that can change without changing the number of phases.

\[ F = C - P + 2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = C - P + 1 \ \text{(pressure constant)} \]

C = components, P = phases, F = degrees of freedom.

Example: Cu–Ni alloy (C = 2) cooling at constant pressure.

Region of Cu–Ni diagramPF = 2 − P + 1Meaning
Liquid only12Temperature and composition can both change
L + α (freezing)21If T is fixed, the compositions of L and α are fixed
Solid α only12Temperature and composition can both change

For a pure metal (C = 1) at its freezing point, P = 2 and F = 0: the temperature is fixed (flat arrest). At an invariant reaction of a binary alloy, P = 3 and F = 0.

Lever rule. In a two-phase field, the tie line is drawn at the alloy temperature. The phase compositions are read at its ends. The fraction of each phase is the opposite arm divided by the whole tie line.

\[ W_L = \frac{C_\alpha - C_0}{C_\alpha - C_L}, \qquad W_\alpha = \frac{C_0 - C_L}{C_\alpha - C_L} \]

Example 1: Cu–Ni, 35 % Ni at 1250 °C. CL = 32 %, Cα = 42 %. WL = (42 − 35)/(42 − 32) = 0.70. Wα = (35 − 32)/(42 − 32) = 0.30.

Example 2: Fe–C, 3 % C just below 1148 °C. The tie line goes from 2.11 % (austenite) to 4.3 % (liquid, which becomes ledeburite). Primary austenite = (4.3 − 3)/(4.3 − 2.11) = 0.594. Liquid (to ledeburite) = (3 − 2.11)/(4.3 − 2.11) = 0.406.

Example 3: pearlite itself. Ferrite = (6.67 − 0.8)/(6.67 − 0.022) = 88.3 %; cementite = (0.8 − 0.022)/(6.67 − 0.022) = 11.7 %.

Q7. Draw the TTT diagram for hypo- and hyper-eutectoid steels and compare it with a eutectoid steel. Explain the effect of alloying elements on the Fe–C diagram and on the TTT diagram. Write briefly on the CCT diagram.

Nov 2024 · Jun-Jul 2024 · Jan 2025 · Aug-Sep 2023 · Jan 2023 (CBCS) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) · Syllabus topic

Steps to construct; eutectoid TTT; extra proeutectoid curve for hypo/hyper; austenite and ferrite stabilisers; CCT shifted right.

Model answer

TTT diagram. It plots the time (log scale) for the isothermal transformation of austenite at different temperatures. It is also called the C-curve or S-curve.

Steps to construct.

  1. Cut many thin specimens of the steel and heat them above A₃ (or A₁) to make austenite.
  2. Quench the specimens quickly into a salt bath at one fixed temperature below A₁.
  3. Hold each specimen for a different time. Then quench in water.
  4. Examine each specimen (microscope, hardness) to find the percentage of austenite transformed.
  5. Mark the times of 1 % (start) and 99 % (finish) transformation on a temperature–log time graph.
  6. Repeat at other temperatures and join the start points and the finish points.

Eutectoid steel (0.8 % C). Above A₁ the steel is austenite. Between 727 and about 550 °C austenite changes to pearlite (coarse at the top, fine near the nose). Between the nose and Ms the product is bainite (upper and lower). Below Ms (about 220 °C) austenite changes to martensite at once, without diffusion.

Effect of alloying elements on the Fe–C diagram.

  • Austenite stabilisers (Ni, Mn, Co, Cu; also C and N) open the γ field. A₃ falls and A₄ rises. With about 8 % Ni and 18 % Cr the steel stays austenitic at room temperature.
  • Ferrite stabilisers (Cr, Mo, W, V, Si, Al, Ti) close the γ field into a loop. A₃ rises and A₄ falls. High Cr steel (above about 13 %) is ferritic at all temperatures.
  • All elements move the eutectoid point to lower carbon content. Ni and Mn lower the eutectoid temperature; Cr, Mo, W, V, Si raise it.

Effect on the TTT diagram. Almost all alloying elements (except Co) shift the C-curves to the right. The critical cooling rate falls, so hardenability rises and martensite forms in thick sections. They also lower Ms. Carbide formers (Cr, Mo, W, V) separate the pearlite nose from the bainite nose and give a "bay" between them.

CCT diagram. A continuous-cooling transformation diagram shows the transformation during continuous cooling, which is the real condition in heat treatment. Compared with TTT, the curves are displaced to longer times and slightly lower temperatures. Furnace cooling gives coarse pearlite, air cooling gives fine pearlite, and a cooling rate above the critical rate gives martensite. Plain carbon steel shows no bainite on continuous cooling (see Unit 4).
