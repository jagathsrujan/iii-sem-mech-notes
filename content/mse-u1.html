This unit explains how atoms are arranged inside a metal, what goes wrong in that arrangement (defects) and how atoms move through a solid (diffusion). 1. Crystalline and amorphous solids; atomic bonding

Atomic bonding is the attractive force that holds atoms together to form a molecule or a solid. The bond forms when atoms share, give or take outer (valence) electrons and so reach a lower, more stable energy. The bond type decides the melting point, hardness, conductivity and ductility of the material.

The four bond types are ionic (electron transfer, hard and brittle, NaCl), covalent (shared electron pairs, very hard, diamond), metallic (shared electron cloud, conductive and ductile, Cu, Fe) and secondary (weak van der Waals or hydrogen bonds, polymers, ice).

A crystalline solid has its atoms arranged in a regular pattern that repeats in all three directions (long-range order). An amorphous solid has atoms in a random arrangement with no long-range order (only short-range order). Most metals are crystalline. Glass and many polymers are amorphous.

2. Space lattice, unit cell and Bravais lattices

Think of atoms as points in space. A space lattice is an infinite, regular three-dimensional array of points in which every point has identical surroundings. A lattice in two dimensions is the same idea on a plane. Each point is a lattice point. In a real crystal an atom (or a group of atoms, the basis) sits at each lattice point.

The unit cell is the smallest block of the lattice that repeats in all three directions to build the whole crystal. Its size and shape are given by six lattice parameters: the edge lengths a, b, c and the angles α, β, γ between the edges (Fig. 1.2).

Bravais showed that only 14 different space lattices are possible. They fall into 7 crystal systems, which differ in their lattice parameters. Each system may have several lattice types: P (primitive, points only at corners), I (body-centred), F (face-centred) and C (base-centred).

3. Unit cells of cubic structures and HCP

Most engineering metals crystallise in one of four structures. Fig. 1.3 shows SC, BCC and FCC (all cubic). Fig. 1.4 shows HCP (hexagonal).

Atoms per unit cell (n). An atom at a corner is shared by 8 cells, so it counts 1/8. A face-centre atom is shared by 2 cells (1/2). A body-centre atom belongs fully to the cell (1). In HCP, a corner atom is shared by 6 cells (1/6).

  • SC: 8 × 1/8 = 1 atom.
  • BCC: 8 × 1/8 + 1 = 2 atoms.
  • FCC: 8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2 = 4 atoms.
  • HCP: 12 × 1/6 + 2 × 1/2 + 3 = 6 atoms.

Coordination number (CN) is the number of nearest-neighbour atoms that touch one atom. Atomic packing factor (APF) is the fraction of the cell volume filled by atoms.

\[ \text{APF} = \frac{\text{volume of atoms in the unit cell}}{\text{volume of the unit cell}} = \frac{n \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{V_{\text{cell}}} \]

n = atoms per cell, r = atomic radius, Vcell = a³ for a cubic cell. Atoms are treated as hard spheres that touch along the closest-packed direction.

4. Stacking of layers of atoms

Close-packed structures are built by stacking layers in which each atom touches six neighbours in a plane. Call the first layer A. The next layer sits in the hollows (holes) of layer A. There are two sets of hollows, called B and C. The way you stack decides the structure.

  • HCP stacks ABABAB…. The third layer lies exactly above the first.
  • FCC stacks ABCABC…. The fourth layer lies above the first. The close-packed layers are the (111) planes of the cube.
Common mistake. Do not write ABAB for FCC. FCC is ABCABC (three-layer repeat). A fault in this sequence (for example ABCABABC) is a stacking fault, a surface defect.

5. Atomic radius, coordination number and APF

For each structure, find the closest-packed direction where atoms touch. Write the length of that direction in terms of r, equate it to the cell geometry and get a in terms of r. Then use n, a and r in the APF formula. The geometry figures are in theory Q4 and Q5. The results:

\[ \text{SC: } \frac{\pi}{6}=0.52 \qquad \text{BCC: } \frac{\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{8}=0.68 \qquad \text{FCC and HCP: } \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}}=0.74 \]

Full derivations are in theory Q4 (FCC, BCC) and Q5 (HCP).

Density of a crystal follows from the cell: mass of atoms in the cell divided by cell volume. Use it to find r from a measured density.

\[ \rho = \frac{n\,A}{V_{\text{cell}}\,N_A} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad V_{\text{cell}} = a^{3} = \frac{n\,A}{\rho\,N_A} \]

ρ = density (kg/m³), n = atoms per cell, A = atomic mass (kg/mol), NA = 6.023 × 10²³ atoms/mol. Keep SI units: A in kg/mol, a in m.

Common mistake. A is given in g/mol. Convert to kg/mol (divide by 1000) when ρ is in kg/m³. Then convert r from metres to nm (× 10⁹).

6. Crystal imperfections

A real crystal is never perfect. Any departure from the perfect, repeating arrangement of atoms is a crystal imperfection (defect). Defects are classified by their dimension.

ClassDimensionTypes
Point defects0-D (one or two atom sites)Vacancy, interstitial, substitutional atom, Frenkel, Schottky
Line defects1-DEdge dislocation, screw dislocation (and mixed)
Surface (planar) defects2-DGrain boundary, tilt boundary, twin boundary, stacking fault, external surface
Volume (bulk) defects3-DVoids (pores), cracks, inclusions, precipitates

Point defects. A vacancy is a missing atom. An interstitial is an extra small atom squeezed between host atoms (for example C in Fe). A substitutional atom is a foreign atom that replaces a host atom (for example Zn in Cu). In ionic crystals, a Schottky defect is a pair of vacancies (one cation and one anion). A Frenkel defect is a cation that leaves its site and sits in an interstitial position, so a vacancy and an interstitial form as a pair. Vacancy concentration rises with temperature: \( n_v = N\,e^{-Q_v/kT} \).

Line defects: dislocations. A dislocation is a line of misfit atoms. Its size and direction are given by the Burgers vector b: the extra step needed to close a loop (Burgers circuit) drawn around the defect.

  • Edge dislocation: an extra half-plane of atoms is inserted. The dislocation line is the lower edge of this half-plane. b is perpendicular to the line. Symbol ⊥.
  • Screw dislocation: part of the crystal is sheared by one lattice spacing, so the atom planes form a spiral ramp. b is parallel to the line.

Surface (planar) defects.

  • Grain boundary: the thin region (a few atoms wide) where two grains of different orientation meet. Atoms there are in a disordered, high-energy state.
  • Tilt boundary: a low-angle grain boundary (θ below about 10°) made of a vertical row of edge dislocations. Spacing D = b/θ.
  • Twin boundary: a plane across which atoms on one side are the mirror image of atoms on the other side. Twins form by shear (deformation twins) or by annealing (annealing twins).
  • Stacking fault: an error in the stacking order, for example ABCABABC instead of ABCABCABC.

How imperfections help. Not all defects are bad. Engineers use them:

  • Strength: alloying atoms (substitutional or interstitial) block dislocations. This is solid-solution strengthening (brass, steel).
  • Work hardening: cold working creates many dislocations that tangle and make the metal harder.
  • Grain-size control: grain boundaries block dislocations, so fine grains give higher yield strength (Hall–Petch: \( \sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k\,d^{-1/2} \)).

7. Atomic diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of atoms through a solid from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration. It happens because atoms vibrate and, at high temperature, some jump to a neighbouring site. Diffusion needs energy (the activation energy Q) to push an atom past its neighbours.

Mechanisms of diffusion.

  • Vacancy (substitutional) mechanism: an atom jumps into a neighbouring vacancy. The vacancy moves the other way. Needs vacancies, so it is slow. Example: Cu in Ni.
  • Interstitial mechanism: a small atom (C, N, H, O) jumps from one interstitial site to the next without needing a vacancy. It is fast. Example: C in Fe (carburising).

Fick's first law (steady state). The flux J (amount crossing unit area in unit time) is proportional to the concentration gradient. The minus sign shows that atoms flow from high to low concentration.

\[ J = -D\,\frac{dC}{dx} \]

J = flux (kg/m²·s or atoms/m²·s), D = diffusion coefficient (m²/s), dC/dx = concentration gradient (kg/m⁴).

Fick's second law (non-steady state). The concentration at a point changes with time. For constant D:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^{2} C}{\partial x^{2}} \]

Rate of change of C at a point = D × curvature of the concentration profile.

For a semi-infinite solid with constant surface concentration Cs (carburising), the solution is:

\[ \frac{C_x - C_0}{C_s - C_0} = 1 - \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) \]

C₀ = initial concentration, Cs = surface concentration, Cx = concentration at depth x after time t.

The diffusion coefficient depends strongly on temperature (Arrhenius law). Plot ln D against 1/T to get a straight line.

\[ D = D_0\,e^{-Q/RT} \]

D₀ = frequency factor (m²/s), Q = activation energy (J/mol), R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = absolute temperature (K).

Factors affecting diffusion.

  • Temperature: D rises exponentially with T. This is the strongest factor.
  • Diffusing species and host: small atoms (C, N, H) diffuse far faster than large substitutional atoms.
  • Crystal structure: diffusion is faster in open structures (BCC, APF 0.68) than in close-packed ones (FCC, APF 0.74).
  • Type of diffusion path: surface diffusion is fastest, then grain boundary, then dislocation (pipe) diffusion, then lattice (volume) diffusion.
  • Imperfections: more vacancies, dislocations and grain boundaries mean faster diffusion.

Applications. Case carburising and nitriding, homogenising, sintering, diffusion bonding, galvanising (see theory Q12).

Q4. Define atomic packing factor. Calculate the APF of an FCC and a BCC unit cell.

MSE QB Q5 · Assignment A4 · Jun-Jul 2024 Q1(a) · Nov-Dec 2025 Q2(b)

Definition, then one sketch and one derivation for each cell. FCC: touch along the face diagonal. BCC: along the body diagonal.

Model answer

APF is the fraction of the volume of the unit cell that is occupied by atoms (taken as hard spheres).

\[ \text{APF} = \frac{n \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{a^{3}} \]

(a) FCC. n = 4. Atoms touch along the face diagonal.

  1. Face diagonal = \( \sqrt{2}\,a = 4r \), so \( a = 2\sqrt{2}\,r \).
  2. Volume of atoms = \( 4 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3} = \frac{16}{3}\pi r^{3} \).
  3. Volume of cell = \( a^{3} = (2\sqrt{2}\,r)^{3} = 16\sqrt{2}\,r^{3} \).
  4. \( \text{APF} = \dfrac{16\pi r^{3}/3}{16\sqrt{2}\,r^{3}} = \dfrac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = 0.74 \). So 74 % of the volume is filled.

(b) BCC. n = 2. Atoms touch along the body diagonal.

  1. Body diagonal = \( \sqrt{3}\,a = 4r \), so \( a = 4r/\sqrt{3} \).
  2. Volume of atoms = \( 2 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3} = \frac{8}{3}\pi r^{3} \).
  3. Volume of cell = \( a^{3} = \dfrac{64\,r^{3}}{3\sqrt{3}} \).
  4. \( \text{APF} = \dfrac{8\pi r^{3}/3}{64\,r^{3}/(3\sqrt{3})} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{8} = 0.68 \).

For SC: n = 1, a = 2r, APF = \( \frac{4\pi r^{3}/3}{8r^{3}} = \pi/6 = 0.52 \).

Q6. Enumerate the types of crystal imperfections. Explain, with sketches, grain boundary, tilt boundary and twin boundary defects.

MSE QB Q3 · Assignment A5 · Jun-Jul 2024 Q1(b) · Aug-Sep 2023

List the four classes with examples, then three labelled sketches with two lines each.

Model answer

A crystal imperfection is any departure from the perfect periodic arrangement of atoms. Classes:

  • Point (0-D): vacancy, interstitial, substitutional, Frenkel, Schottky.
  • Line (1-D): edge, screw, mixed dislocations.
  • Surface (2-D): grain boundary, tilt boundary, twin boundary, stacking fault.
  • Volume (3-D): voids, cracks, inclusions, precipitates.

1. Grain boundary. A real metal is polycrystalline. It has many small crystals (grains) with the same lattice but different orientation. The thin disordered region (a few atoms wide) between two grains is the grain boundary. Atoms there have higher energy, so boundaries etch first, block dislocations (raise strength) and are fast diffusion paths.

2. Tilt boundary (low-angle boundary). Two neighbouring grains are tilted by a small angle θ (below about 10°). The boundary is a vertical row of edge dislocations with spacing D = b/θ, where b is the Burgers vector.

3. Twin boundary. A plane across which the lattice on one side is the mirror image of the lattice on the other side. Atoms on the plane belong to both. Deformation twins form by shear. Annealing twins form during recrystallisation (common in FCC Cu, brass).

Stacking fault. An error in the stacking order, for example ABCABABC in FCC.

Q7. What are imperfections? Explain how imperfections are helpful in engineering materials.

MSE QB Q6 · Aug-Sep 2023

Define, then list 5–6 uses with one example each. Mention that real strength is 1/100 to 1/1000 of ideal.

Model answer

Imperfections are departures from the perfect, regular arrangement of atoms in a crystal. They are of four kinds: point, line, surface and volume defects. They are always present and often controlled on purpose.

  1. Strengthening by alloying (solid solution). Foreign atoms distort the lattice and block dislocations: brass (Zn in Cu), steel (C in Fe).
  2. Work (strain) hardening. Cold rolling or drawing multiplies dislocations; they tangle and the metal gets harder.
  3. Grain-boundary strengthening. Boundaries stop dislocations. Fine grains give higher yield stress (Hall–Petch, \( \sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k\,d^{-1/2} \)).
  4. Precipitation hardening. Fine particles (volume defects) obstruct slip: Al–Cu alloys (duralumin).
  5. Easy plastic deformation. Dislocation slip lets metals be rolled, forged and drawn at a stress far below the theoretical strength.
  6. Diffusion and heat treatment. Vacancies let atoms move: carburising, annealing, sintering.
  7. Electrical and optical properties. Doping silicon with P or B gives n-type and p-type semiconductors; defects colour gemstones (ruby, Cr³⁺ in Al₂O₃).

Harmful side: voids, cracks and inclusions are stress raisers that lower fatigue life and toughness. Good practice keeps these small and few.

Q9. Sketch and explain the edge dislocation. Differentiate between edge and screw dislocation.

MSE QB Q7 · Assignment A2 · Assignment A6 · Jun-Jul 2024 Q2(a) · Jan 2023 (CBCS)

Edge sketch with extra half-plane, line and Burgers circuit; screw sketch; comparison table of 6–8 rows.

Model answer

Edge dislocation. An extra half-plane of atoms is inserted in the upper part of the crystal. The lower edge of this half-plane is the dislocation line (symbol ⊥). Atoms above the line are squeezed (compression) and atoms below are stretched (tension). To find the Burgers vector b, draw a loop of equal steps round the defect. In a perfect crystal it closes. Round the dislocation it fails by b. For an edge dislocation b is perpendicular to the line. It moves (glides) in the direction of b under shear stress τ, and can also climb by vacancy diffusion.

Screw dislocation. One part of the crystal is displaced by b relative to the other, so the atom planes wind round the line like a spiral ramp (screw). b is parallel to the line.

Q10. Illustrate the phenomenon and mechanisms of diffusion.

MSE QB Q9 · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q3(a) · Apr 2022 (CBCS)

Define diffusion, why it happens, then the two mechanisms with sketches; end with where it is used.

Model answer

Phenomenon. Diffusion is the transport of atoms inside a solid, from high concentration to low concentration, by thermally activated jumps. Example: carbon spreading into the surface of steel in a carburising furnace. Atoms vibrate about their sites; sometimes an atom gets enough energy (activation energy Q) to jump. The rate increases quickly with temperature (\( D = D_0 e^{-Q/RT} \)).

Mechanisms.

  1. Vacancy (substitutional) diffusion. A host or substitutional atom jumps into a neighbouring empty site. The vacancy moves the opposite way. Needs vacancies, so Q is high and the rate is lower. Example: Cu–Ni, self-diffusion.
  2. Interstitial diffusion. A small atom (C, N, H, O) moves from one interstitial site to the next. No vacancy needed; Q is low; it is 10⁶ to 10⁹ times faster. Example: C in α- or γ-iron.

Other paths (grain boundary, dislocation and surface diffusion) are faster still because the structure there is open.

Q11. State and explain Fick's first and second laws of diffusion. Discuss the factors affecting diffusion.

Assignment A3 · Nov-Dec 2025 Q2(a) · Jan 2025 Q1(b) · Aug-Sep 2023 · Nov 2024 Q2(a) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q1(d)

First law with figure; second law (state, derive in 3 lines); erf solution; factors list with reason. Add Arrhenius.

Model answer

Fick's first law. In steady state the diffusion flux is proportional to the concentration gradient:

\[ J = -D\,\frac{dC}{dx} \]

J in kg/m²·s, D in m²/s, dC/dx in kg/m⁴. The minus sign: flow is towards lower concentration.

Fick's second law. In non-steady state, C changes with time. Take a slab of thickness dx and unit area. Flux in at x is J, flux out at x + dx is \( J + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x}dx \). Accumulation = in − out:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t}\,dx = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(D\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right) = D\frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial x^{2}} \]

D constant. The rate of change of C at a point is D times the curvature of the profile.

For a semi-infinite solid with constant surface concentration Cs and initial C₀:

\[ \frac{C_x - C_0}{C_s - C_0} = 1 - \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) \]

Factors affecting diffusion (with \( D = D_0 e^{-Q/RT} \)):

  1. Temperature — D rises exponentially with T.
  2. Diffusing atom and host — small atoms diffuse faster (interstitial > substitutional).
  3. Crystal structure — faster in open BCC than in close-packed FCC.
  4. Defects and path — surface > grain boundary > dislocation > lattice diffusion.
  5. Bond strength / melting point — higher melting point gives larger Q and smaller D.
  6. Concentration gradient — increases flux, not D.
  7. Time — penetration depth ∝ √(Dt).

Q12. Discuss the applications of diffusion in mechanical engineering.

Assignment A8

Give 6–8 applications; say what diffuses into what, and the result. Case hardening first.

Model answer
  1. Case hardening (carburising). Low-carbon steel gears, cams and shafts are heated at about 900–950 °C in a carbon-rich atmosphere. Carbon diffuses into the surface (Fick's second law) to a depth of 0.5–2 mm. After quenching the surface is hard and wear-resistant, and the core stays tough.
  2. Nitriding and carbonitriding. N (and C) diffuse into steel to form hard nitrides at 500–550 °C. There is little distortion.
  3. Homogenising (annealing) of castings. Diffusion removes the segregation of elements within grains.
  4. Sintering in powder metallurgy. Atoms diffuse across particle contacts and bond the powder into a solid part.
  5. Diffusion bonding and brazing. Atoms diffuse across the joint interface and make a strong joint without melting.
  6. Galvanising and aluminising. Zn or Al diffuses into the steel surface and gives corrosion resistance.
  7. Precipitation (age) hardening. Solute atoms diffuse to form fine precipitates in Al alloys.
  8. Creep. At high temperature, diffusion lets grains slide and voids grow in turbine blades.
  9. Semiconductor doping. Dopant atoms diffuse into silicon wafers.

N1. Copper has an FCC structure and an atomic radius of 0.128 nm. Find the lattice constant, the cell volume, the volume of atoms in the cell and the APF.

Syllabus topic (APF of FCC)

  1. FCC: \( a = 2\sqrt{2}\,r = 2\sqrt{2}\times 0.128 = 0.3620 \) nm.
  2. Cell volume \( V = a^{3} = (0.3620)^{3} = 4.745\times10^{-2} \) nm³.
  3. Atoms per cell n = 4. Volume of one atom \( = \frac{4}{3}\pi(0.128)^{3} = 8.785\times10^{-3} \) nm³.
  4. Volume of atoms \( = 4\times 8.785\times10^{-3} = 3.514\times10^{-2} \) nm³.

N4. Carbon diffuses in steady state through FCC iron. The carbon concentration is 1.2 kg/m³ at 5 mm and 0.8 kg/m³ at 10 mm below the surface. D = 3 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s. Find the diffusion flux.

Syllabus topic (Fick's first law)

  1. Concentration gradient \( \dfrac{dC}{dx} = \dfrac{C_2 - C_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \dfrac{0.8 - 1.2}{(10-5)\times10^{-3}} = -80 \) kg/m⁴.
  2. Fick's first law: \( J = -D\,\dfrac{dC}{dx} \).
  3. \( J = -(3\times10^{-11})(-80) = 2.4\times10^{-9} \) kg/m²·s.
  4. The sign is positive: carbon flows towards the greater depth (low concentration).

Answer: J = 2.4 × 10⁻⁹ kg/m²·s, directed into the steel

N5. A gear of 1020 steel (0.2 % C) is carburised at 927 °C. Estimate the time to raise the carbon to 0.3 % at 0.3 mm below the surface. Surface carbon is 1.1 %. D = 1.28 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s. Take erf(1.1) = 0.8802 and erf(1.2) = 0.9103.

Jan 2023 (CBCS) Q2

Data note. The scan of the original paper lost part of the erf table. The two values used here are the standard erf values. Exact erf inversion gives z = 1.127 and t = 1385 s, the same to 3 figures of practice.
  1. \( C_0 = 0.2,\ C_s = 1.1,\ C_x = 0.3,\ x = 0.3\times10^{-3} \) m.

  3. Interpolate: \( z = 1.1 + 0.1\times\dfrac{0.8889 - 0.8802}{0.9103 - 0.8802} = 1.129 \).

  5. \( t = \dfrac{(1.329\times10^{-4})^{2}}{1.28\times10^{-11}} = \dfrac{1.765\times10^{-8}}{1.28\times10^{-11}} \approx 1.38\times10^{3} \) s.
