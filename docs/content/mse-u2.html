1. Tensile test and the engineering stress–strain curve

The tensile test pulls a standard specimen in a universal testing machine until it breaks. The machine records load P and extension ΔL. Fig. 2.1 shows the specimen.

σ = engineering stress (N/mm² = MPa), A₀ = original area, e = engineering strain (no unit), L₀ = original gauge length. Both use the original dimensions.

Fig. 2.2 is the curve of a ductile material (mild steel). Its key points:

  • A, proportional limit: up to A stress ∝ strain (Hooke's law). The slope OA is Young's modulus E.
  • Elastic limit: the largest stress after which the material returns fully to its shape on unloading. It lies very near A.
  • B, upper yield point, and C, lower yield point: the material begins to deform plastically with no rise in stress. Mild steel shows this drop and plateau; the lower yield stress is quoted as σy.
  • C to E, strain hardening: dislocations multiply and block each other, so more stress is needed to keep straining.
  • E, ultimate tensile strength (UTS, σu): the highest engineering stress. Beyond E the neck forms.
  • E to F, necking and fracture: the area at the neck shrinks fast, so the load, and the engineering stress, fall until the bar breaks at F.

Many materials (aluminium, high-strength steel) show no sharp yield point. For these, draw a line parallel to the elastic slope, starting at 0.2 % strain (e = 0.002). The stress where this line cuts the curve is the 0.2 % proof stress (offset yield strength), Fig. 2.3(b).

Brittle materials (cast iron, glass, ceramics) show no yield point and no necking. They break suddenly at less than 1 % strain and absorb little energy. Ductile materials stretch visibly first, so they give warning (Fig. 2.3a).

2. True stress and true strain

The engineering curve falls after UTS only because it divides by the original area A₀. The real area keeps shrinking. True stress uses the instantaneous area A. True strain adds up each small increment dL/L.

σT = true stress, εT = true strain, A = instantaneous area.

Volume is constant during plastic flow (A·L = A₀·L₀). From this, up to the start of necking:

Fig. 2.4 compares the two curves. The true curve keeps rising up to fracture, because the metal keeps strain hardening. In the plastic range the true curve follows (K = strength coefficient, n = strain-hardening exponent).

Common mistake. The relations σT = σ(1+e) and εT = ln(1+e) are valid only up to the start of necking. After necking the strain is not uniform; use the measured neck area instead: σT = P/A and εT = ln(A₀/A).

3. Mechanical properties

These properties come from the stress–strain curve (Fig. 2.5). The 8 properties of QB Q44 are defined in the flashcards at the end of this part.

  • Stiffness is the resistance to elastic deformation. Measure: Young's modulus E = slope of the elastic line. Steel (E ≈ 200 GPa) is about 3 times stiffer than aluminium (E ≈ 70 GPa).
  • Resilience is the energy a material stores per unit volume up to the elastic limit: (area under the elastic part).
  • Toughness is the energy per unit volume absorbed up to fracture: the whole area under the curve. It combines strength and ductility.
  • Ductility is the plastic strain at fracture. It is measured as % elongation = (Lf − L₀)/L₀ × 100 and % reduction in area = (A₀ − Af)/A₀ × 100.

4. Hardness testing

Hardness is the resistance of a material to localised plastic deformation, such as indentation or scratching. In a hardness test a hard indenter is pressed into the surface under a set load, and the size or depth of the impression is converted to a number. For steels, UTS (MPa) ≈ 3.45 × BHN.

Brinell test (BHN)

A hardened steel (or tungsten carbide) ball of diameter D (10 mm) is pressed with load P (3000 kgf for steel, 500 kgf for soft metals) for 10–30 s. The diameter d of the impression is measured with a microscope.

P in kgf, D and d in mm; BHN is in kgf/mm². The denominator is the curved area of the spherical impression.

Merits: averages over coarse grains; simple. Limits: not for very hard (> 450 BHN) or thin parts.

Vickers test (VHN or HV)

A square-based diamond pyramid with 136° between opposite faces is pressed with a load of 1–100 kgf. The two diagonals d₁, d₂ of the square impression are measured and averaged.

P in kgf, d in mm. Result = load ÷ slant surface area of the pyramid impression.

Merits: one scale for all materials; accurate; works on thin sheet. Limits: slow; needs a polished surface.

Rockwell test (HR)

The Rockwell tester measures the depth of the impression and shows the number on a dial, so it needs no microscope. First a minor load P₀ (10 kgf) seats the indenter and sets the zero. Then a major load P₁ is added, then removed, with P₀ still on. The permanent increase in depth h gives the hardness: HR = N − h/s (N = 100 for a diamond cone and 130 for a ball; s = 0.002 mm).

Exam pattern. A hardness question asks for the principle, the indenter, the diagram, the formula and 3–4 merits. State units: BHN and VHN are in kgf/mm².

5. Plastic deformation: slip and twinning

Plastic deformation is permanent. In crystals it happens in two ways: slip (the main one) and twinning (Fig. 2.9).

Slip is the sliding of one part of a crystal over another along a slip plane in a slip direction. The slip plane is the plane of densest atom packing; the slip direction is the closest-packed direction. A plane with a direction in it is a slip system. FCC has 12 slip systems (ductile), HCP only 3. Slip happens by dislocation motion, one row of atoms at a time, at a stress far below the theoretical shear strength. Slip starts when the resolved shear stress reaches the critical value: (Schmid's law).

Twinning is a shear that makes the deformed region a mirror image of the parent across a twin plane. Each atomic plane moves a fraction of a spacing, in proportion to its distance from the twin plane.

Slip moves atoms by whole spacings and gives large strain. Twinning moves them by fractions of a spacing, gives a mirror-image lattice and small strain, and helps mainly in BCC and HCP metals at low temperature or under impact.

6. Fracture

Fracture is the separation of a body into two or more pieces under stress. It has two steps: crack formation and crack propagation. Fractures are ductile or brittle (Fig. 2.10).

A ductile fracture is slow, dull and fibrous and gives warning. A brittle fracture is sudden, bright and flat, at low temperature, high strain rate or a notch (full comparison in Q1).

Cup-and-cone fracture

This is the usual ductile fracture of a round bar in tension. It has five stages (Fig. 2.11): (a) necking, (b) microvoids form at the centre of the neck, (c) the voids join into a flat central crack, (d) the crack spreads outward until the remaining ring shears at 45° (shear lips), (e) the bar separates as a cup and a cone.

Crack modes (Type I, II and III)

A crack can be loaded in three ways (Fig. 2.12). Type (Mode) I opening: the tensile stress is normal to the crack plane; it is the most common and the most dangerous. Type (Mode) II sliding: in-plane shear, perpendicular to the crack front. Type (Mode) III tearing: shear parallel to the crack front.

Griffith's criterion and fracture toughness

Real solids fail far below their theoretical strength because of tiny cracks. Griffith said a crack grows when the elastic strain energy released is at least equal to the surface energy of the two new faces.

For a through-thickness central crack of length 2a in a thin plate (plane stress): strain energy released = πσ²a²t/E, surface energy needed = 4aγt (two surfaces of area 2a·t, γ per unit area each). Setting d/da of (4aγt − πσ²a²t/E) to zero gives:

E = Young's modulus, γ = surface energy (J/m²), a = half-length of the crack. It fits perfectly brittle materials (glass); for metals use 2(γ + γp), where γp is plastic work at the tip.

A longer crack means a lower fracture stress. In engineering this is written with the stress intensity factor K = Yσ√(πa) (Y = geometry factor, about 1 to 1.1). The crack runs when K reaches the material's fracture toughness KIC (Mode I, plane strain, unit MPa√m):

Typical KIC: Al alloy 25–40, steel 50–150, glass 0.7 MPa√m. High KIC = crack-tolerant.

7. Fatigue

Fatigue is the failure of a material under repeated (cyclic) stress that is well below its static yield strength. It causes most failures of shafts, springs and aircraft parts. There is no warning.

Mechanism (three stages): (1) crack initiation at a surface flaw, notch, scratch, keyway or inclusion, where slip bands pile up; (2) crack propagation: the crack grows a little with each cycle and leaves smooth beach marks (and microscopic striations); (3) final fracture: the remaining area is too small to carry the load and breaks suddenly (rough zone), Fig. 2.14(b).

Stress cycle terms: σmax, σmin, mean stress σm = (σmax + σmin)/2, stress range Δσ = σmax − σmin, amplitude σa = Δσ/2 and ratio R = σmin/σmax. Fully reversed bending has σm = 0 and R = −1.

The S–N curve (Fig. 2.15) plots stress amplitude S against the number of cycles to failure N (log scale). Steels and titanium show a horizontal part: below the endurance limit (fatigue limit) Se the life is infinite (about 10⁷ cycles is taken). Aluminium, copper and magnesium alloys show no such limit; their fatigue strength is the stress for a set life such as 10⁸ cycles. For steels Se ≈ 0.5 × UTS.

Fatigue testing uses the R. R. Moore rotating-beam machine (Fig. 2.16). Many identical polished specimens are tested at different stresses; each stress and its life N give one point on the S–N curve.

Factors that lower fatigue life: stress concentration (notches, sharp corners, holes), rough surface finish, large size, tensile mean stress, corrosive environment (corrosion fatigue), high temperature, and surface decarburisation. Shot peening and polishing help.

8. Creep

Creep is the slow, permanent deformation of a material under a constant load (stress) over a long time at high temperature, usually above 0.4 Tm (Tm = melting temperature in kelvin). The stress may be below the yield strength. Examples: turbine blades, boiler and steam pipes, furnace parts, lamp filaments, and lead pipes sagging at room temperature.

A creep curve (Fig. 2.17) plots strain against time at constant stress and temperature. After the instantaneous elastic strain ε₀ there are three stages:

  • I Primary (transient) creep: the creep rate falls with time because strain hardening makes further flow harder.
  • II Secondary (steady-state) creep: the rate is constant and at its minimum. Strain hardening and recovery (softening) balance. This is the longest stage.
  • III Tertiary creep: the rate rises quickly as the area falls (necking), voids form at grain boundaries and the part ruptures.
Why stage II matters. It lasts most of the life, so it fixes the service life: time to rupture ≈ allowable strain ÷ steady-state creep rate. Designers pick stress and temperature to keep the minimum creep rate low.

Creep properties: minimum (steady-state) creep rate, creep strength (stress for a given creep strain in a given time), rupture life and stress-rupture strength. A higher temperature or stress makes creep faster. Creep-resistant: nickel superalloys, stainless and Cr–Mo steels, ceramics.

Stress relaxation (Fig. 2.18) is the fall of stress with time in a part held at constant total strain, for example a tightened bolt or a press-fit at high temperature. Elastic strain converts slowly into creep strain. For a simple (Maxwell) model σ = σ₀e−t/τ, where τ is the relaxation time (the time for σ to fall to 37 % of σ₀).

9. Alloys, steels and solidification (brief)

An alloy has two or more elements, at least one a metal. Alloying raises strength and hardness (solute atoms block dislocations) and improves corrosion resistance. A solute is substitutional (Cu in Ni) or interstitial (C in Fe).

Steel is iron with up to about 2 % carbon (cast iron 2–4.5 %; eutectoid steel 0.8 % C). Low-carbon steel (up to 0.25 % C) is soft and ductile, medium (0.25–0.6 %) is balanced, high (0.6–1.4 %) is hard. Alloy steels add Cr, Ni, Mo, V, Mn, W or Si; stainless steel has over 11.5 % Cr.

Solidification has two steps: nucleation (stable nuclei form after slight undercooling; homogeneous in the pure melt, heterogeneous on walls or impurities) and growth (grains grow until they meet at grain boundaries). A pure metal freezes at one temperature, so the cooling curve (Fig. 2.19) has a flat plateau at Tm while latent heat leaves. Fast cooling gives fine grains.

Q1. Define fracture. List the various types of fracture in materials and explain.

MSE QB Q10 · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q4(b) · Apr 2022 (CBCS) Q7(a)

Definition, ductile and brittle types with sketches (Fig. 2.10), points of difference.

Model answer

Definition. Fracture is the separation of a solid body into two or more pieces under an applied stress. It happens in two steps: crack formation, then crack propagation.

Types. By the amount of plastic deformation before failure, fracture is of two main types (a third, fatigue fracture, comes from cyclic loading).

  1. Ductile fracture. Large plastic strain; a neck forms first. A round bar breaks in a cup-and-cone shape (highly ductile metals such as gold or lead neck down to a point). Crack growth is slow and stable, the surface is dull and fibrous, and 45° shear lips form at the edge. It gives warning and absorbs much energy.
  2. Brittle fracture. Almost no plastic strain, no neck. The crack runs very fast across the section, along cleavage planes (transgranular) or grain boundaries (intergranular). The surface is flat, bright and granular, often with chevron marks. It is sudden, with no warning, and the energy is low. It is favoured by low temperature, high strain rate, notches and BCC or HCP metals.
  3. Fatigue fracture. Cyclic stress below yield starts a surface crack that grows with each cycle (beach marks) until sudden final fracture. See Q10.

MSE QB Q11 · Nov-Dec 2025 Q3(a)

Model answer

Test. A standard specimen (gauge length L₀, area A₀) is pulled in a universal testing machine. For each load P the engineering stress σ = P/A₀ and strain e = ΔL/L₀ are plotted.

Sketch: Fig. 2.2 (axes: strain on x, stress on y; label O, A, B, C, E, F, σy, σu).

Q6. Explain briefly, with suitable sketches, plastic deformation by slip and twinning. Compare them.

MSE QB Q27 · Jun-Jul 2024 Q3(b) · Jan 2025 Q4(b) · Apr 2022 (CBCS) · Jan 2023 (CBCS)

Sketch Fig. 2.9; define slip plane, direction, system; twin plane; comparison table.

Model answer

Slip. One part of a crystal slides over the next along a slip plane (densest packing) in a slip direction (closest-packed). Atoms move through whole lattice spacings, so the lattice above and below stays the same and steps (slip lines) appear on the surface. It happens by dislocation motion at stress τ = σ cos φ cos λ equal to the critical resolved shear stress. FCC has 12 slip systems (very ductile), BCC 48, HCP 3.

Twinning. A shear along a twin plane turns the lattice into a mirror image across it. Each plane moves by a fraction of a spacing, proportional to its distance from the twin plane. It needs higher stress than slip; it occurs in BCC and HCP metals at low temperature or under impact, and as annealing twins in FCC.

Q7. With a neat diagram, explain briefly the stages in cup-and-cone fracture. Explain ductile fracture with its stages.

MSE QB Q14 · Nov-Dec 2025 Q4(a) · Nov 2024 Q4(a) · Jan 2025 Q3(b) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q4(a) · Aug-Sep 2023

Five-stage figure (Fig. 2.11) and the final cup and cone.

Model answer

Ductile fracture is fracture after large plastic deformation; the usual form in a round tensile bar is cup-and-cone. Stages (sketch Fig. 2.11):

  1. Necking. At UTS, the deformation localises; the neck reduces the area and a triaxial tensile stress develops at its centre.
  2. Microvoid formation. Voids (cavities) nucleate at the neck centre at inclusions and second-phase particles.
  3. Void coalescence. The voids grow and join into a flat, disc-shaped central crack, perpendicular to the load.
  4. Crack spreading and shear lips. The crack spreads outward until the remaining ring of metal is thin; near the surface it turns and runs at about 45° (maximum shear stress), forming shear lips.
  5. Final separation. The bar parts into a cup (concave) and a cone (convex). The central surface is fibrous and dull; the rim is smooth.

Stress–strain features of ductile fracture: yield, strain hardening, UTS, then a falling curve (Fig. 2.2). Energy absorbed is large and there is visible warning.

Q8. With a neat diagram, discuss Type I, Type II and Type III fracture.

MSE QB Q12

Three crack-opening modes (Fig. 2.12); define by displacement direction and give the use.

Model answer

A crack in a loaded body can move in three independent ways. Any real load is a mix of these modes. Sketch Fig. 2.12 (block with a slit and force arrows).

  1. Type (Mode) I, opening. Tensile stress acts normal to the crack plane; the two faces move apart. This is the commonest and most dangerous mode; KIC is defined for it.
  2. Type (Mode) II, sliding (in-plane shear). Shear stress acts in the crack plane, perpendicular to the crack front; the faces slide over each other in the direction the crack grows.
  3. Type (Mode) III, tearing (out-of-plane shear). Shear stress acts in the crack plane, parallel to the crack front; the faces slide in the "scissors" direction.

The stress intensity factor of each mode is KI, KII, KIII; for Mode I, KI = Yσ√(πa), and fracture occurs when KI = KIC.

Q9. State Griffith's criterion for brittle fracture and derive σf. What is fracture toughness?

Syllabus topic · Jan 2023 (CBCS)

Energy balance for a plate with crack 2a (Fig. 2.13), result σf = √(2Eγ/πa), KIC.

Model answer

Statement. A crack in a brittle solid propagates when the decrease in elastic strain energy from crack growth is at least equal to the increase in surface energy of the new crack faces.

Derivation for a thin plate (thickness t), central crack of length 2a, stress σ:

  1. Strain energy released: Ue = −πσ²a²t/E.
  2. Surface energy: Us = 2 × (2a·t) × γ = 4aγt.
  3. Total U = 4aγt − πσ²a²t/E (sketch Fig. 2.13b: it peaks at the critical length).
  4. Put dU/da = 0: 4γt = 2πσ²at/E, so σ² = 2Eγ/πa.

A crack longer than ac = 2Eγ/πσ² grows without limit. In metals, 2γ is replaced by 2(γ + γp) because plastic work γp at the tip is large.

Fracture toughness KIC is the critical value of the Mode I stress intensity factor K = Yσ√(πa) at which a crack propagates in plane strain; unit MPa√m. It is the material property that replaces yield strength in damage-tolerant design: σc = KIC/(Y√(πa)).

Q10. Define fatigue. Explain the R. R. Moore fatigue testing method with the S–N diagram. Define endurance limit. Explain the mechanism of fatigue failure and the factors affecting fatigue life.

MSE QB Q15 · Nov-Dec 2025 Q4(b) · Nov 2024 Q4(b) · Aug-Sep 2023 · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q5(a) · Apr 2022 (CBCS) Q7(b)

Definition, machine sketch (Fig. 2.16), S–N curve (Fig. 2.15), endurance limit, factors.

Model answer

Fatigue is the failure of a material under repeated or fluctuating stress, at a stress much lower than the static tensile or yield strength. Stages: crack initiation at a surface flaw, crack growth (beach marks), sudden final fracture.

R. R. Moore test. Sketch Fig. 2.16. A polished, waisted round specimen is held at both ends in a spindle driven by a motor. Weights hang from two bearings near the centre, so the specimen is in pure bending (constant moment) between them. As it rotates, each surface fibre goes from tension to compression and back once per revolution: a fully reversed cycle (σm = 0, R = −1). The bending stress is σ = 32M/πd³ at the waist. A counter records the revolutions N until the specimen breaks.

Procedure and S–N curve. Test about 8–10 identical specimens, each at a lower stress, starting near 2/3 of UTS. Plot the stress amplitude S against log N (Fig. 2.15). Steel gives a curve that becomes horizontal near 10⁶–10⁷ cycles: the stress there is the endurance limit Se, the highest amplitude that can be applied for an infinite number of cycles without failure. For steels Se ≈ 0.5 × UTS. Non-ferrous metals have no limit; the fatigue strength at 10⁸ cycles is quoted.

Mechanism. (1) Crack initiation: repeated slip makes slip bands, extrusions and intrusions at the surface; notches and inclusions start cracks earlier. (2) Crack propagation: each cycle adds a striation; the smooth, beach-marked area grows from the origin (Fig. 2.14b). (3) Final fracture: the remaining section breaks suddenly (rough zone).

Q11. Define creep. Explain the three stages of creep with a neat graph. Why is the second stage very important?

MSE QB Q13 · MSE QB Q18 · Jan 2025 Q4(a) · Jun-Jul 2024 Q4(b) · Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS) Q5(b) · Jan 2023 (CBCS)

Definition with examples, curve (Fig. 2.17), the three stages, importance of stage II, factors, stress relaxation.

Model answer

Creep is the slow, time-dependent permanent deformation of a material under a constant stress, usually at a temperature above about 0.4 Tm. Examples: turbine blades, boiler and steam pipes, furnace parts, lamp filaments.

Curve (Fig. 2.17): strain on the y-axis, time on the x-axis, at constant stress and temperature. After the instantaneous strain ε₀:

  1. Primary creep: decreasing rate (strain hardening is faster than recovery).
  2. Secondary creep: constant, minimum rate (hardening balances recovery); a straight line.
  3. Tertiary creep: accelerating rate; necking, grain-boundary voids and cracks; ends in rupture.

Why the second stage is important.

  • It covers most of the life of a component.
  • Its slope, the minimum creep rate, is a constant material property at given σ and T; strain at any time = ε₀ + (dε/dt)·t.
  • So designers use it to predict the life and set the allowable stress and temperature (for example, 1 % strain in 10⁵ h).
  • Tertiary creep is too rapid and risky to be allowed in service.

Factors: higher stress, higher temperature, finer grains, and low melting point increase the creep rate. Resistant materials: nickel superalloys, Cr–Mo steels, ceramics. Stress relaxation is the related fall of stress at constant strain (Fig. 2.18).

Q13. Explain with a cooling curve the solidification of a pure metal. Distinguish homogeneous and heterogeneous nucleation.

Syllabus topic · MSE QB Q17 · Jun-Jul 2024 Q9(b)

Cooling curve (Fig. 2.19), nucleation then growth, grain size.

Model answer

Solidification is the change of a metal from liquid to crystalline solid. Sketch Fig. 2.19: temperature against time.

  1. Liquid cooling: the melt loses superheat and its temperature falls steadily.
  2. Nucleation: the melt must be undercooled slightly (ΔT) below Tm before stable nuclei form. Latent heat then makes the temperature rise back to Tm.
  3. Growth at constant temperature: the plateau at Tm; solid and liquid exist together while latent heat leaves. One temperature, because a pure metal has a single melting point (zero freezing range).
  4. End of solidification: all liquid is gone; the solid grains meet at grain boundaries and then cool down.
PointHomogeneous nucleationHeterogeneous nucleation
WhereInside the pure meltOn mould walls, impurities, inoculants
Undercooling neededLarge (0.2 Tm)Small (0.01–0.1 Tm)

Fast cooling gives many nuclei and fine grains with better strength; slow cooling gives few, coarse grains.

Numericals

Method. (1) Write the formula for the quantity asked. (2) Convert all data to N, mm, N/mm² (or kgf, mm for hardness; J/m², Pa, m for Griffith). (3) Substitute, compute, and state the unit. N1. A mild steel bar of 16 mm diameter is tested in tension. It yields at a load of 92 kN, reaches a maximum load of 182 kN and breaks at a load of 69 kN. The diameter at the fracture neck is 10 mm. Find (i) yield stress, (ii) ultimate stress, (iii) engineering stress at fracture, (iv) true stress and true strain at fracture. Also find the true stress and true strain at UTS if the engineering strain there is 0.20.

Based on Mar-Apr 2021 (CBCS)

  1. Original area A₀ = π/4 × 16² = 201.06 mm².
  2. Yield stress σy = 92 000 / 201.06 = 457.6 N/mm².
  3. Ultimate stress σu = 182 000 / 201.06 = 905.2 N/mm².
  4. Engineering stress at fracture = 69 000 / 201.06 = 343.2 N/mm².
  5. Neck area Af = π/4 × 10² = 78.54 mm². True stress at fracture σT = 69 000 / 78.54 = 878.5 N/mm².
  6. True strain at fracture εT = ln(A₀/Af) = ln(201.06/78.54) = ln 2.56 = 0.940. (After necking use the area form.)
  7. At UTS (before necking): σT = σu(1 + e) = 905.2 × 1.20 = 1086 N/mm²; εT = ln(1.20) = 0.182.

Answer: σy = 457.6, σu = 905.2, fracture (engineering) = 343.2 N/mm²; true fracture stress = 878.5 N/mm², true fracture strain = 0.940; at UTS σT = 1086 N/mm², εT = 0.182

N3. A glass plate has E = 70 GPa and surface energy γ = 0.3 J/m². It contains an internal crack of length 2a = 2 µm. (a) Find the fracture stress by Griffith's criterion. (b) Find the critical half-length of a crack that would make the plate fail at 50 MPa.

Syllabus topic · Based on Jan 2023 (CBCS)

  1. a = 1 µm = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m. σf = √(2Eγ/πa).
  2. 2Eγ = 2 × 70 × 10⁹ × 0.3 = 4.2 × 10¹⁰ Pa·J/m².
  3. σf = √(4.2 × 10¹⁰ / (π × 10⁻⁶)) = √(1.337 × 10¹⁶) = 1.156 × 10⁸ Pa = 115.6 MPa.
  4. (b) ac = 2Eγ / (πσ²) = 4.2 × 10¹⁰ / (π × (50 × 10⁶)²) = 4.2 × 10¹⁰ / 7.854 × 10¹⁵ = 5.35 × 10⁻⁶ m = 5.35 µm.
  5. So a crack of total length 2ac = 10.7 µm fails the plate at 50 MPa; longer cracks fail it at lower stress.

Answer: σf = 115.6 MPa; ac = 5.35 µm (crack length 10.7 µm)
