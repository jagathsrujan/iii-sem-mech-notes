First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law is the law of conservation of energy. This unit shows how to write an energy balance for a closed system (a piston and cylinder) and for an open system (a turbine, nozzle, compressor or boiler). Every thermal numerical in the exam starts with one of these two balances.

Concepts

Exam pattern. Unit II of the paper has two 20-mark questions (choose one). Each has a 10-mark theory part and a 10-mark numerical. The theory part is almost always one of these: (1) state the first law, explain PMM-I and the limitations; (2) prove that energy is a property, with Joule's experiment; (3) derive the SFEE and apply it to a nozzle, turbine, compressor or throttle. The numerical is a closed-system process (Q, W, ΔU) or an SFEE problem with given enthalpies.

1. Energy, heat, work and the sign convention

The total stored energy of a system is E = U + KE + PE. U is the internal energy (molecular energy), KE = mV²/2 and PE = mgz. For a stationary system KE and PE do not change, so ΔE = ΔU.

Heat and work are energy in transit. They cross the boundary; the system does not store them. They depend on the path, so they are path functions and we write δQ and δW. Energy E is a point function (a property) and we write dE.

  • Heat Q added to the system is positive.
  • Work W done by the system is positive.
  • Heat rejected and work done on the system are negative.
Sign convention. Some books use W as work done on the system. This unit uses the engineering convention: Q − W = ΔE. State the convention at the top of every answer.

2. First law for a cycle; Joule's experiment

Joule placed a known mass of water in an insulated vessel. A falling weight turned a paddle wheel in the water (Fig. 2.1). The work done on the water was W = mgh. The water temperature rose. Joule then cooled the water back to its first state: it rejected heat Q. Over the full cycle he found that W and Q were always in a constant ratio, whatever the method.

First law for a cycle: when a closed system completes a cycle, the net heat transfer equals the net work transfer.

The circle on the integral means one complete cycle. In older units J = 4.186 kJ/kcal (mechanical equivalent of heat).

Same temperature rise by any adiabatic work. Joule got the same temperature rise with paddle work, with electrical work and with friction. So the adiabatic work depends only on the end states. This leads to the idea that energy is a property.

3. First law for a change of state; energy is a property

Take a system that goes from state 1 to state 2 by path A. Return it by path B. Then return it again by path C (Fig. 2.2). Apply ∮δQ = ∮δW to the two cycles and subtract. You get Q − W the same on B and C. So Q − W depends only on the end states, not on the path. It is the change of a property. We call that property energy E. Q1 gives the full proof.

Differential form: δQ − δW = dE. For a cycle ΔE = 0, which gives ∮δQ = ∮δW. For an isolated system Q = W = 0, so ΔE = 0: the energy of an isolated system is constant.

Memory trick. "Heat in minus work out equals the energy gained": Q − W = ΔE. If a gas expands it does positive work and loses energy unless heat comes in.

4. Internal energy, enthalpy and specific heats

Internal energy U is the energy of the molecules: their kinetic energy (translation, rotation, vibration) and their potential energy (forces between molecules). It is a property. For an ideal gas U depends on temperature only (Joule's law).

Enthalpy is defined as H = U + pV, or h = u + pv per kg. It is a property because U, p and V are properties. Its use: for a constant-pressure closed process, Q = ΔU + pΔV = ΔH. In an open system, h is the energy that one kg of fluid carries, including its flow work pv.

Valid for an ideal gas, for any process. cp = specific heat at constant pressure, cv = at constant volume (kJ/kg·K). For air R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, cp = 1.005, cv = 0.718, γ = 1.4.

6. PMM-I and limitations of the first law

A perpetual motion machine of the first kind (PMM-I) is a machine that delivers work continuously without any energy input, or delivers more energy than it receives. It creates energy. This breaks the first law, so it cannot exist.

The first law is a balance sheet. It checks that energy is conserved, but it does not tell whether a process can really happen. The limitations are:

  • It gives no direction to a process. A hot body in contact with a cold body could gain heat from it and the first law would still hold. In practice heat flows only from hot to cold.
  • It gives no limit on the conversion of heat to work. It allows 100 % conversion, but a heat engine must reject some heat.
  • It does not say how much a process is reversible, or how good (useful) the energy is. Work is high-grade energy; heat is low-grade energy.
  • It cannot say whether a process is possible. Two equal-energy states can be impossible to join.
Where the limit comes from. The second law (Unit 3) fills these gaps: it gives direction, and it limits the efficiency of a heat engine (Kelvin–Planck, Clausius).

7. Steady flow and the SFEE

An open system (control volume) has mass crossing its boundary. A turbine and a nozzle are open systems. The flow is steady when:

  • mass flow rate in = mass flow rate out, ṁ = constant (continuity: ṁ = AV/v);
  • the properties at the inlet, at the outlet and inside do not change with time;
  • heat and work rates are constant;
  • the inlet and outlet have uniform properties (one-dimensional flow).

To push 1 kg of fluid into the control volume, the fluid behind it does flow work p₁v₁. At the exit, the fluid does p₂v₂ on the fluid ahead. Flow work + internal energy = enthalpy. This is why enthalpy appears in the SFEE.

q = heat added per kg, ws = shaft work done per kg (kJ/kg); h in kJ/kg, V in m/s, z in m. For the rate form multiply by ṁ: Q̇ − Ẇ = ṁ[Δh + ΔV²/2 + gΔz].

Units. V²/2 is in J/kg and gz is in J/kg. Divide by 1000 to get kJ/kg before you add to h. This is the commonest numerical mistake in this unit.

Each device is a control volume. Strike out the terms that are zero, and the SFEE reduces to a short equation. Table 2.2 gives all of them.

Table 2.2 — SFEE reduced for common devices.

Nozzle and diffuser. A nozzle is a duct of changing area that raises the velocity of a fluid and lowers its pressure. A diffuser does the opposite. The fluid has no time to exchange heat, so the flow is adiabatic. The enthalpy drop becomes kinetic energy. If V₁ is small, V₂ = √(2(h₁ − h₂)) = 44.72 √Δh (Δh in kJ/kg, V₂ in m/s).

Turbine. Steam or gas expands through blades and turns the shaft. The heat loss from the casing is small. The inlet and exit velocity are of the order of 50–100 m/s, so ΔKE is often small, but check if the problem gives it. The work output equals the fall in enthalpy.

Compressor and pump. Work is put in to raise the pressure. The gas is often cooled in a water jacket, so q is negative. Fig. 2.10 shows a rotary and a reciprocating compressor. The reciprocating one has pulsating flow, but with a receiver tank the average flow is steady, so the same SFEE applies.

Throttling. A valve, an orifice or a porous plug reduces the pressure with no work and no heat transfer. The velocity may rise at the restriction, but the pipe on both sides is of the same size, so ΔKE is small. The result is h₁ = h₂. The process is irreversible. A throttling calorimeter uses this to find the dryness of wet steam.

Heat exchanger, boiler, condenser. No shaft work. Heat passes from the hot stream to the cold stream inside the device, so with no loss to outside, heat lost by hot = heat gained by cold. A boiler adds heat to water, a condenser removes heat from steam, and in each case q = Δh.

Real world. A power plant uses all of these together: a boiler (q in), a turbine (w out), a condenser (q out) and a pump (w in). The same devices appear in a refrigerator (compressor, condenser, throttle valve, evaporator).
Method for any SFEE problem. (1) Draw the control volume. (2) Write the full equation. (3) Cross out the zero terms. (4) Put every energy term in kJ/kg. (5) Solve; check the sign of q and w.

Q2. State the first law of thermodynamics. Explain the perpetual motion machine of the first kind (PMM-I). List the limitations of the first law with an example.

Jan 2025 · Nov 2024 · Jan 2023 (CBCS) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) · Apr 2022 (CBCS)

Statement and equation (3 marks), PMM-I with sketch (3), four limitations with a hot-body example (4).

Model answer

Statement. Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only change from one form to another. For a closed system: the net heat supplied equals the net work done plus the increase in stored energy, . For a cycle, .

PMM-I. A perpetual motion machine of the first kind is a machine that produces work continuously without receiving any energy, or one that delivers more energy than it receives.

  • For a cycle, the first law says W = Q. If Q = 0, then W = 0. So a machine with W > 0 and no energy input is impossible.
  • The first law is a law of nature, drawn from experiment. No one has ever built a PMM-I. A PMM-I would make energy from nothing.
  • A heat engine therefore needs a heat input QH and gives W = QH − QL.

Limitations of the first law.

  1. No direction. It does not say which way a process goes. Example: a cup of hot tea cools in the room by losing Q to the air. The reverse (the tea gets hotter by taking Q from cooler air) also conserves energy, but it never happens.
  2. No limit on heat-to-work conversion. It allows 100 % of the heat to become work. In fact every heat engine must reject heat to a sink (Kelvin–Planck).
  3. No difference between heat and work. They are equivalent in the first law (1 kJ = 1 kJ). In fact work is high-grade energy (it can all become heat) and heat is low-grade.
  4. It cannot say if a process is possible. Friction turns work into heat, but heat cannot turn back into the same work by the reverse path; the first law allows both.

These limits are removed by the second law.
