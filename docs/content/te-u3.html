2. Thermal reservoir and heat engine

A thermal reservoir is a body so large that it gives or takes heat without a change in its temperature. The source (high-temperature reservoir, HTR) supplies heat. The sink (low-temperature reservoir, LTR) receives heat. The sea, a river, the atmosphere and a large furnace are reservoirs.

A heat engine is a device that works in a cycle, takes heat from a source, gives net work , and rejects heat to a sink. Steam power plants, petrol engines and diesel engines are heat engines. Fig. 3.1 shows the model.

4. Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements

The second law has two classic statements. Both say what is impossible.

Kelvin-Planck statement. It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and produces no effect other than the extraction of heat from a single reservoir and the performance of an equal amount of work.

So every heat engine needs a sink. is impossible.

Clausius statement. It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and produces no effect other than the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter body.

So a refrigerator needs a work input. Heat does not flow from cold to hot on its own.

A device that breaks the Kelvin-Planck statement is a perpetual motion machine of the second kind (PMM2). It obeys the first law but breaks the second. A PMM1 would create energy and break the first law.

5. Equivalence of the two statements

The two statements are equivalent. If a device breaks one statement, you can build a device that breaks the other. This proves that both say the same thing. The full proof is Theory Q1. The idea is below.

  • K-P violation gives Clausius violation. A "100 % engine" E drives a reversible refrigerator R. The combined device moves heat from cold to hot with no net work.
  • Clausius violation gives K-P violation. A device C moves from cold to hot with no work. An ordinary engine E takes from the hot reservoir and rejects to the cold one. The combined device takes from one reservoir and gives the same amount of work.
Exam method. (1) Assume a device that breaks statement A. (2) Couple it to a normal device. (3) Make the heat at one reservoir cancel. (4) Show that the combination breaks statement B. Write all Q and W values on the sketch.

6. Reversible and irreversible processes

A reversible process can be reversed so that both the system and the surroundings return exactly to their first states, with no net change anywhere. All other processes are irreversible. A reversible process is an ideal limit. It is slow (quasi-static), and it has no friction and no finite driving difference.

The causes of irreversibility are the factors that stop a process from being reversible. Fig. 3.4 shows three of them.

  • Friction (solid friction, viscosity of fluids): work becomes internal energy that cannot be changed back to the same work.
  • Heat transfer through a finite temperature difference: heat flows only from hot to cold. To reverse it you need a refrigerator and work.
  • Unrestrained (free) expansion: a gas expands into a vacuum with no work. Compression back needs work.
  • Mixing of two different gases or fluids, and chemical reactions such as combustion.
  • Electrical resistance (Joule heating), magnetic hysteresis and inelastic deformation.
  • Throttling through a valve or porous plug (a sudden pressure drop).
Internal and external. An internally reversible process has no irreversibility inside the system. A totally reversible process also has none in the surroundings. A Carnot engine is totally reversible.

Reversible processes are the benchmark. A reversible engine gives the maximum work. A reversible refrigerator needs the minimum work. The gap between a real machine and the reversible machine is the irreversibility.

8. Carnot theorem and the thermodynamic temperature scale

Carnot theorem. No heat engine working between two given reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine working between the same two reservoirs.

Corollary. All reversible engines working between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, whatever the working fluid.

Proof idea (full proof in Theory Q4): suppose an irreversible engine I is more efficient than a reversible engine R. Run R backwards as a heat pump, driven by I. The combined device takes heat from only one reservoir and gives net work. This breaks the Kelvin-Planck statement. So I cannot be more efficient. For two reversible engines, each one tests the other, so their efficiencies are equal.

Because all reversible engines give the same , the ratio depends only on the two reservoir temperatures. Lord Kelvin used this to define a temperature scale that does not depend on any substance: the thermodynamic (Kelvin) temperature scale.

The triple point of water is fixed at 273.16 K. If , then , which breaks Kelvin-Planck. So absolute zero cannot be reached.

Use the theorem as a test for an engine claim. The efficiency of any engine is less than or equal to the Carnot efficiency. If a claim is higher, the claim is impossible. If it equals it, the engine is reversible. If it is lower, the engine is irreversible.

Model answer

Limitations of the first law. The first law () does not tell: (1) the direction of a process, (2) that heat cannot be converted fully into work in a cycle (quality of energy), (3) why an engine needs a sink ( is allowed by the first law), (4) the difference between reversible and irreversible processes. The second law gives these.

Kelvin-Planck statement (heat engine). It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and produces no effect other than the extraction of heat from a single reservoir and the performance of an equal amount of work. (So a heat engine needs a sink; .)

Part A: violation of Kelvin-Planck gives violation of Clausius.

  1. Let E be a device that breaks Kelvin-Planck. It takes from the HTR and gives . It rejects no heat.
  2. Use the work to drive a reversible refrigerator R. R takes from the LTR and rejects to the HTR.
  3. HTR: loses to E, gains from R. Net heat to the HTR is .
  4. LTR: loses .
  5. Work: all the work of E goes into R. The combined device exchanges no work with the surroundings.
  6. The only net effect is the transfer of from the LTR to the HTR with no work. This breaks the Clausius statement.

Part B: violation of Clausius gives violation of Kelvin-Planck.

  1. Let C be a device that breaks Clausius. It moves from the LTR to the HTR with no work.
  2. Let E be an ordinary engine. It takes from the HTR, gives , and rejects to the LTR. Then .
  3. LTR: receives from E and loses to C. Net heat is zero.
  4. HTR: loses to E and gains from C. Net heat taken from the HTR is .
  5. The combined device takes heat from one reservoir (the HTR) and gives the same amount of work, with no other effect. This breaks the Kelvin-Planck statement.

Q2. Define a reversible process. Define irreversibility and discuss at least three factors that make a process irreversible.

Nov-Dec 2025 Q6(b) · Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS)

One definition of each, then at least three causes, each with a one-line reason and a small sketch.

Model answer

Reversible process. A process that can be reversed so that both the system and the surroundings return exactly to their first states, with no net change in the system or in the surroundings. It is quasi-static (a series of equilibrium states) and has no dissipation.

Irreversible process. A process that is not reversible. If it is reversed, the system can return to its first state, but the surroundings stay changed. Irreversibility is the property of such a process. Its measure is the lost work .

Test. Run the process backwards. If both system and surroundings return to the first state without outside work or heat, it is reversible.

Factors that cause irreversibility.

  1. Friction (solid friction, viscosity). Work becomes heat in the body and the surroundings. To get the work back you need extra work. The surroundings change for ever.
  2. Heat transfer through a finite temperature difference. Heat flows only from hot to cold. To return it you need a refrigerator and work. Reversible heat transfer needs .
  3. Unrestrained expansion. A gas expands into a vacuum and does no work. To compress it back you need work, and heat flows out.
  4. Mixing of different gases or fluids. Work is needed to separate them again.
  5. Electrical resistance, magnetic hysteresis, inelastic deformation, chemical reaction (combustion) and throttling. Each dissipates useful energy into heat.
