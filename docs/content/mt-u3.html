1. Welding and its classification

Welding is a permanent joining process. It joins two or more parts by heat, or by pressure, or by both, with or without filler metal. The joint is as strong as the parent metal if it is made well.

We classify welding by the way the joint forms. Fig. 3.1 shows the tree.

  • Fusion welding: the edges of the parent metal melt and fuse, usually with filler metal. No pressure is applied. Examples: gas, arc.
  • Pressure (solid-state) welding: the metal is heated until plastic and then pressed together. Examples: resistance, forge.
  • Brazing and soldering join with a filler that melts while the parent metal stays solid. They are not true welding.

3. Gas welding (oxy-acetylene)

In gas welding a flame of fuel gas burnt in oxygen melts the edges of the joint. A filler rod adds metal. Oxy-acetylene gives the hottest flame (about 3200 °C), so it is the most common.

4. Resistance welding

In resistance welding a large current passes through the joint. The contact resistance makes heat. The parts are then pressed together. No flux, no filler and no gas are needed.

H = heat generated (J), I = current (A), R = resistance of the joint (Ω), t = time of current flow (s).

Current is large (thousands of amperes) and voltage is low (2 to 10 V). A step-down transformer gives this. The electrodes are copper alloy and water cooled. Fig. 3.5 shows spot welding.

Fig. 3.6 shows the other types.

  • Spot: a single spot joins overlapped sheets. Used for car bodies and appliances.
  • Seam: roller electrodes make a row of overlapping spots. Gives a leak-tight joint for tanks and cans.
  • Projection: embossed projections on one sheet concentrate the current. Many spots at once; used for nuts and brackets.
  • Butt (upset or flash): two bars in line are clamped, heated and pressed end to end. Used for rods, chains and rails.

7. Chip formation, shear zone and shear angle

The tool pushes into the work. The metal ahead of the edge is compressed. It shears along a plane and slides up the rake face as a chip. Fig. 3.9 shows this.

  • The shear plane is the plane of maximum shear. The shear angle φ is its angle to the cutting direction.
  • The shear zone is the thin band where the plastic deformation really happens. It is thicker near the tool tip.
  • The chip is thicker and shorter than the layer removed: .
  • A large shear angle means a thin chip, less deformation, low force, low heat and good finish. Raise it with a larger rake angle and low friction.

r = chip thickness ratio (always less than 1), t₁ = uncut chip thickness (= feed in turning), t₂ = chip thickness, α = rake angle.

12. Tool wear and tool life

Tool wear is the gradual loss of tool material (Fig. 3.14).

  • Flank wear: a flat land VB on the flank face from rubbing on the work. Standard failure measure: VB = 0.3 mm.
  • Crater wear: a scoop on the rake face from the hot chip sliding. Depth KT.
  • Notch wear: a groove at the depth-of-cut line, caused by work-hardened skin and the hard scale of the work.

Tool life T is the cutting time until the tool reaches the wear limit. Taylor's equation links it with cutting speed (Fig. 3.15):

V = cutting speed (m/min), T = tool life (min), n = exponent (HSS 0.08–0.2, carbide 0.2–0.4, ceramic 0.4–0.6), C = constant (speed for T = 1 min).

Speed has the strongest effect on tool life, then feed, then depth of cut.

Theory questions

Q1. Classify welding processes and state the principle of fusion and pressure welding.

Syllabus topic

Tree of processes (Fig. 3.1) with one line on each group.

Model answer

Definition. Welding is a permanent joining process in which two or more parts are joined by heat, pressure or both, with or without filler metal.

Classification (Fig. 3.1):

  • Fusion welding: parent metal melts and fuses. Gas (oxy-acetylene), arc (SMAW, TIG, MIG, SAW), thermit, electron beam and laser.
  • Pressure (solid-state) welding: metal is heated to the plastic state and pressed. Resistance (spot, seam, projection, butt), forge, friction, ultrasonic.
  • Brazing and soldering: filler melts above or below 450 °C; parent metal does not melt.

Comparison.

FusionPressure
Metal melts; filler usedMetal only plastic; usually no filler
Heat from flame or arcHeat from resistance, friction or furnace
Larger heat-affected zoneNarrow heat-affected zone
Slow; skilled welderFast; suits mass production

Q2. With a neat sketch explain the following welding joints: (a) butt joint (b) lap joint (c) edge joint (d) tee joint.

MT QB Q19

Four sketches in a row (Fig. 3.2), weld shaded, one use each. Add the corner joint for completeness.

Model answer

A welded joint is the way two parts meet at the weld. The five basic types are shown in Fig. 3.2.

  • (a) Butt joint: two plates lie edge to edge in the same plane. The edges are square for thin plates (up to 5 mm) or bevelled (single V, double V, U) for thick plates so the weld penetrates the full depth. Strong in tension and fatigue. Used for pressure vessels, pipes, ship plates.
  • (b) Lap joint: one plate overlaps the other. Fillet welds are made along the edges, on one or both sides. No edge preparation. Good in shear, poor in fatigue because of the offset load path. Used for sheet metal and tanks.
  • (c) Edge joint: two parallel plates are put edge to edge so their edges are flush. The weld is made along the edges. Low strength; for thin sheet and light covers that carry no heavy load.
  • (d) Tee joint: one plate stands at 90° on the face of the other. Fillet welds on one or both sides. Used for frames, stiffeners and brackets.
  • Corner joint: two plates meet at their edges at 90° to make a corner. Used for boxes and frames.

Preparation (bevel angle, root gap) depends on plate thickness. Always sketch the weld metal as a shaded triangle or V.

Q4. With a neat sketch explain the resistance welding process.

MT QB Q17 · Jun-Jul 2025 Q8(a) (4th sem MP-I, spot welding)

Principle H = I²Rt, spot welding sketch (Fig. 3.5), the cycle, types (Fig. 3.6), advantages and limits.

Model answer

Principle. Two parts are held between electrodes. A large current passes through the joint. The resistance of the contact heats the metal, and the force of the electrodes presses the plastic metal into a weld. .

Fig. 3.5 shows spot welding: a step-down transformer gives low voltage and very high current (thousands of amperes) to two water-cooled copper electrodes.

  1. Clean overlapped sheets are placed between the electrodes.
  2. The electrodes squeeze the sheets (force F).
  3. The current flows for a short time (0.02–1 s). A molten nugget forms at the interface.
  4. The current stops. The force holds the nugget until it freezes (forging).
  5. The electrodes lift off.

Types (Fig. 3.6): spot, seam (roller electrodes, leak-tight), projection (embossed projections) and butt (upset or flash).

Advantages: fast, easy to automate, no filler or flux, little distortion, clean. Limitations: costly equipment, lap joints for sheets only (spot, seam), thickness limit, high power demand.

Uses: car bodies, appliance panels, fuel tanks, wire mesh, rail ends.

Q5. Explain briefly orthogonal and oblique cutting with a neat sketch.

MT QB Q24

Plan-view sketch (Fig. 3.8) and a table of differences.

Model answer

Cutting is classified by the angle of the cutting edge to the direction of the cutting velocity V (Fig. 3.8).

Orthogonal cutting. The edge is perpendicular to V. The chip flows normal to the edge. Only two forces act (Fc and Ft), so cutting is a two-dimensional problem. The edge is longer than the width of cut. All basic theory (Merchant circle) uses it. Example: parting off, tube turning.

Oblique cutting. The edge is inclined at the angle i to the normal of V. The chip flows sideways at the chip-flow angle η (Stabler: η ≈ i). Three forces act (Fc, Ft, Fr): a three-dimensional problem. The longer edge spreads heat and gives longer tool life and a better finish because the chip flows away from the work. Example: turning with a side cutting edge angle, milling, drilling.

Q6. Illustrate the formation of chip. Discuss the types of chips with neat sketches.

MT QB Q25

Fig. 3.9 (shear plane, shear zone, t₁, t₂) then three chip types (Fig. 3.10). Add the meaning of shear angle.

Model answer

Formation. The wedge-shaped tool enters the work (Fig. 3.9). The metal ahead of the edge is compressed until its shear stress equals the shear strength. It then shears plastically along the shear plane at the shear angle φ and slides up the rake face as a chip.

  • The deformation is in a thin shear zone, not on a line. Nearly all machining heat is made here, with some from friction on the rake face.
  • The chip is thicker and shorter than the layer cut: , .
  • Significance of φ: a large φ gives a thin chip, small shear area, low force, low power and less heat. A small φ gives a thick chip and high force. φ increases with larger rake and lower friction ().

Types of chip (Fig. 3.10):

  • Continuous: ductile metal, high speed, small feed, large rake angle, low friction. Smooth ribbon, good finish, low wear. Disposal is difficult, so a chip breaker is used.
  • Discontinuous: brittle metals (cast iron, bronze), or ductile metal at low speed, large feed, small rake. Short broken pieces, force varies, poorer finish.
  • Continuous with built-up edge (BUE): ductile metal at low speed with high friction. Layers of work metal weld to the tool tip, grow and break off. Gives a rough surface and crater wear. Cure: raise speed, increase rake, reduce feed, use cutting fluid.

Q9. Define cutting speed, feed and depth of cut.

MT QB Q28

Definition, symbol, unit, formula and a turning sketch (Fig. 3.7).

Model answer

These are the three cutting parameters (Fig. 3.7).

  • Cutting speed V: the relative surface speed between tool and work in the main cutting direction. m/min (D in mm, N in rpm). It is the main motion. It has the largest effect on tool temperature and tool life.
  • Feed f: the advance of the tool per revolution of the work (mm/rev) in turning and drilling, or per stroke in shaping. It is the secondary motion. It sets chip thickness and surface finish.
  • Depth of cut d: the thickness of the layer removed in one pass, measured perpendicular to the feed direction (mm). In turning .

Material removal rate mm³/min. For long tool life: use large depth of cut first, then feed, and speed last. Roughing uses high d and f at low V. Finishing uses low d and f at high V.

Tool life is the total cutting time until the tool reaches a set failure limit, for example flank wear VB = 0.3 mm, complete failure, poor finish or sharp rise in force. Tool wear types (Fig. 3.14): flank wear (rubbing on the work), crater wear (hot chip on the rake face) and notch wear at the depth-of-cut line. The wear curve has three stages: initial, steady and rapid failure.

Factors:

Q13. How do you classify cutting tools? Explain with a neat sketch.

MT QB Q32

Fig. 3.12 tree, then one line for each group.

Model answer

Cutting tools are classified by the number of cutting edges (Fig. 3.12).

  • Single point tools have one cutting edge. By construction: solid (forged) tools, brazed-tip tools, and tools with clamped throw-away inserts. By operation: turning, facing, boring, parting, threading, shaping and planing tools. Used on lathe, shaper and planer.
  • Multi point tools have many cutting edges. Toothed: drill, reamer, milling cutter, broach, hob, tap, saw, file. Abrasive: grinding wheel, honing stick, abrasive belt.

Multi point tools cut faster and share the load, but cost more and are harder to sharpen. Single point tools are simple, cheap and easy to regrind.

Q14. Describe tool wear and the three zones of the wear curve.

Syllabus topic (tool wear)

Fig. 3.14: flank, crater and notch wear and the three-stage curve.

Model answer

Tool wear is the gradual loss of tool material at the cutting edge by abrasion, adhesion, diffusion and heat (Fig. 3.14).

  • Flank wear: wear land VB on the flank from rubbing on the machined surface. Gives poor finish and size error. Measured by VB; limit 0.3 mm.
  • Crater wear: a scoop on the rake face where the hot chip slides. Depth KT. Weakens the edge.
  • Notch wear: a groove at the depth-of-cut line from work-hardened skin and scale.

Wear curve: stage I is initial fast wear of the sharp edge; stage II is steady slow wear (the useful life); stage III is rapid wear and failure. Regrind or index the tool at the end of stage II. Tool life is the time to reach the VB limit.
