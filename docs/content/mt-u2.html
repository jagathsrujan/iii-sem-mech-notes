Metal Forming Processes

Forming shapes solid metal by plastic deformation, without removing chips. This unit covers rolling, forging, extrusion and drawing, and also piercing and blanking. You must know each process, its sketch, its defects and its uses.

1. Nature of plastic deformation

When we load a metal, it first deforms elastically. Atoms move a little and return when the load is removed. Above the yield stress the metal deforms plastically. The change of shape stays after unloading. Fig. 2.1 shows the idea.

Plastic deformation happens by slip. Planes of atoms slide over each other along slip planes in slip directions. The planes move by whole atomic spacings, so the crystal stays a crystal. Slip moves line defects called dislocations. This needs far less stress than shearing a whole plane at once.

  • FCC metals (Cu, Al, Au) have many slip systems and are very ductile. HCP metals (Zn, Mg) have few and crack easily when cold.
  • Volume stays constant during plastic flow. If length grows, area falls.
  • In a polycrystal, grains are pulled into long shapes along the flow direction.

Constancy of volume: A = cross-section area, L = length (0 = before, 1 = after)

2. Hot working and cold working

Recrystallisation temperature (Tr) is the lowest temperature at which a heavily cold-worked metal forms new strain-free grains completely in about one hour. It is about 0.3 to 0.5 of the melting point on the absolute scale. Fig. 2.2 shows the effect.

  • Hot working is plastic deformation above Tr.
  • Cold working is plastic deformation below Tr.
  • Lead and tin recrystallise at room temperature, so rolling them at 20 °C is hot working.

Importance of temperature (QB Q15)

  • Higher temperature lowers the yield strength, so the machine needs less force.
  • Ductility rises, so a large reduction is possible without cracks.
  • Above Tr recrystallisation removes strain hardening; the metal stays soft.
  • The finishing temperature controls the final grain size. A low finishing temperature gives fine grains.
  • Too high a temperature causes grain growth, burning and heavy scale. Too low a temperature causes cracks.

3. Rolling: principle

Rolling reduces the thickness of metal by passing it between two rotating rolls. The roll gap is smaller than the entry thickness. The metal gets thinner and longer; it spreads a little in width. Fig. 2.3 shows the strip in the roll bite.

  • Draft = h₀ − h₁, the drop in thickness in one pass.
  • Angle of bite α is the angle at the roll centre between the line of centres and the line to the first contact point.
  • Friction pulls the strip into the rolls. If α is too large, the strip slips and will not enter.
  • The neutral point is where roll speed equals strip speed. Before it the roll is faster; after it the strip is faster.

μ = coefficient of friction, R = roll radius (mm), Δh = draft (mm). Maximum draft rises with bigger rolls and rougher rolls.

Hot rolling turns an ingot into a bloom (large square), a billet (smaller square) or a slab (wide, flat). Cold rolling gives thin, bright, accurate sheet and strip.

5. Rolling of bars and shapes

Bars and structural shapes are rolled in grooved rolls. Each pair of grooves is a pass. The hot billet goes through a sequence of passes. Each pass changes the section a little (Fig. 2.5).

  • Bars: square billet → box or diamond pass → square → oval → round. The bar is turned 90° between passes so that it spreads evenly and does not form fins.
  • Shapes (I-beams, channels, angles, rails): a rectangular bloom passes through roughing, intermediate and finishing grooves. Universal mills use horizontal and vertical rolls together.
  • The reduction should be even over all parts of the section. If not, the thin parts cool first, internal stress builds and defects form.

7. Forging: principle and operations

Forging shapes a metal by compressive force from a hammer or press. The metal is usually hot and plastic. It flows to the shape of the dies or tools. Forged parts have a refined grain, closed porosity and grain flow that follows the shape, so they are strong and tough.

  • Open-die forging: flat or simple tools; the work is free to flow sideways. Used for large and simple parts.
  • Closed-die (impression) forging: the metal fills a cavity in two dies. Excess leaves as flash. Used for mass production.

Fig. 2.7 shows the basic operations.

  • Upsetting: length falls and cross-section grows (heads of bolts, rivets).
  • Drawing out (cogging): thickness falls and length grows.
  • Fullering: a round-edged tool makes grooves and moves metal away from a section.
  • Edging: concave dies gather metal into a shape for the next step.
  • Piercing / punching: a punch makes a hole or a recess; punching through leaves a slug.
  • Bending: the bar is bent to an angle or a curve.

8. Smith forging

Smith forging is hand forging by a blacksmith. The smith heats the metal in a forge, holds it with tongs on an anvil and shapes it with hand hammers and a sledge hammer. It suits small batches and repairs; accuracy and output are low. Fig. 2.8 shows the set-up.

9. Drop forging

Drop forging is closed-die forging with a drop hammer. A heavy ram with the upper die is lifted and then falls on the hot work in the lower die. The energy is E = m g h (plus steam or air pressure in a power hammer). Several blows fill the cavity. Fig. 2.9 shows the hammer and the dies.

  • The dies may have blocking and finishing impressions. Flash is trimmed off later.
  • The gutter is a recess that takes the flash.

10. Press forging

Press forging uses a slow squeezing force from a hydraulic or mechanical press. The ram moves at low speed, so the metal flows deep into the section. Fig. 2.10 shows a hydraulic press.

11. Forging defects

Fig. 2.11 shows four common defects.

  • Mismatch: dies not aligned.
  • Lap / cold shut: metal folds over and is pressed flat.
  • Unfilled section: low temperature or too little metal.
  • Surface cracks: work too cold.
  • Scale pits, decarburisation: oxide hammered in; carbon lost.

12. Extrusion: principle and hot extrusion

Extrusion forces a billet through a die opening to make a long product with a constant cross-section. The metal is in compression, so even brittle metals can be extruded. Fig. 2.12 shows the two hot processes.

Extrusion ratio: A₀ = billet area, A₁ = product area. Typical R is 10 to 100.

  • Aluminium is extruded at 350–500 °C, copper at 750–950 °C and steel at 1100–1250 °C.
  • Products: rods, tubes, window frames and profiles.

13. Cold (impact) extrusion and hydrostatic extrusion

Cold (impact) extrusion. A slug of soft metal (Al, Pb, Sn, Zn, Cu) is placed in a die at room temperature. A fast punch hits it. The metal flows backward around the punch to make a cup or tube, or forward through the die. Fig. 2.13 shows both ways.

  • Use: collapsible tubes, battery cans, cartridge cases.
  • Good finish, close tolerance, no scrap.

Hydrostatic extrusion. A fluid at high pressure (up to about 1500 MPa) surrounds the billet. The fluid pushes the billet through the die (Fig. 2.14). There is almost no friction at the container wall.

  • Advantages: large reduction, good for brittle metals.
  • Limits: high cost, seals, tapered billet nose.

14. Wire, rod and tube drawing

Drawing pulls a bar, wire or tube through a tapered die to reduce its size. The pull is tensile, but the metal is squeezed by the die wall. It is cold work, so it gives accuracy, a bright finish and higher strength.

Area reduction per pass r (usually 20 to 30 %). A₀, A₁ = area before and after the die.

  • Wire drawing (Fig. 2.15): a rod of 5 to 10 mm is pointed, passed through the die and pulled by a drawing block (capstan). Dies are tungsten carbide or diamond. Soap is the lubricant.
  • Rod and bar drawing (Fig. 2.16): thicker straight stock is pulled on a draw bench by a carriage with tongs.
  • Tube drawing (Fig. 2.17): reduces the diameter and wall of a tube. Sinking has no inside support. A fixed plug, a floating plug or a moving mandrel controls the inside size and wall.

15. Piercing and blanking

Both are shearing operations on a press with a punch and a die (Fig. 2.18).

  • Blanking: the piece cut out is the product (the blank). The strip with holes is scrap. Die opening = blank size; the punch is smaller by the clearance.
  • Piercing: a punch makes a hole in the sheet. The cut-out slug is scrap. Punch size = hole size; the die opening is larger.
  • Clearance is about 5 to 10 % of the thickness on each side.

F = punch force (N), L = cut length (mm), t = thickness (mm), τs = shear strength (N/mm²)

Rotary piercing (Mannesmann) makes seamless tube. Two skewed rolls turn a hot billet and push it over a plug (Fig. 2.19).

Common mistake. Do not mix the two. In blanking the cut piece is the product. In piercing the hole is the product.

Theory questions

Q1. Explain the importance of temperature in metal forming and write the difference between hot working and cold working.

MT QB Q15

Define plastic deformation and Tr, give the effects of temperature in points, then a 10-row table.

Model answer

Plastic deformation. Beyond the yield stress, atomic planes slip over each other by whole atomic spacings along slip planes. The shape change stays after unloading (Fig. 2.1). Volume stays constant.

Recrystallisation temperature (Tr). The lowest temperature at which a cold-worked metal recrystallises fully in about 1 hour. It is about 0.3 to 0.5 Tm (absolute). Fig. 2.2 shows strength falling and ductility rising near Tr.

Importance of temperature in metal forming:

  1. As the temperature rises, the yield strength falls. The forming force and power fall.
  2. Ductility rises, so large deformation is possible without cracks.
  3. Above Tr, recrystallisation removes strain hardening. The metal stays soft during the whole process.
  4. Porosity and cavities in the cast structure close; coarse grains break into fine equiaxed grains.
  5. The finishing temperature decides the grain size. A low finishing temperature gives fine grains and better strength.
  6. Too high a temperature causes grain growth, burning, hot shortness and heavy oxide scale.
  7. Too low a temperature (below Tr) causes strain hardening, high force and cracks.

Q2. Explain the principle of (a) forging (b) extrusion.

MT QB Q18

Give a definition, the working principle in points and a sketch for each process.

Model answer

(a) Principle of forging. Forging is the shaping of metal by compressive force from a hammer or a press. The metal is usually hot, so it is plastic.

  • The hot metal is placed between two dies or between flat tools.
  • A hammer blow or a press stroke compresses it. The metal flows plastically to fill the shape of the dies. The volume stays constant.
  • In open-die forging (Fig. 2.7, Fig. 2.8) the work flows freely. In closed-die forging (Fig. 2.9, Fig. 2.10) it fills a cavity and the excess leaves as flash.
  • Grain flow follows the part shape and the porosity closes. The part is strong and tough.

(b) Principle of extrusion. Extrusion forces a billet through a die opening with a ram. The product has the shape of the die opening and a constant cross-section (Fig. 2.12).

  • The billet is heated and placed in a container. The ram pushes it against the die.
  • The metal is under strong compression, so it flows through the opening without cracks.
  • In direct extrusion the product moves with the ram. In indirect extrusion the die is on a hollow ram and the product leaves through the ram.
  • Extrusion ratio R = A₀/A₁.
  • Cold, impact and hydrostatic extrusion (Fig. 2.13, Fig. 2.14) use the same principle at lower temperature or with fluid pressure.

Uses. Forging: crankshafts, connecting rods, hand tools. Extrusion: rods, tubes, window frames, aluminium profiles.

Q3. Describe rolling mills and the rolling of bars and shapes.

MT QB Q20

Principle with Fig. 2.3, six mill types (Fig. 2.4), then pass design for bars and shapes (Fig. 2.5).

Model answer

Rolling. Rolling reduces the thickness of a metal by passing it between two rotating rolls with a gap smaller than the entry thickness (Fig. 2.3). Friction pulls the work in. Draft = h₀ − h₁. The strip is gripped only if tan α ≤ μ, and the largest draft is μ²R.

Rolling mills (stand arrangements, Fig. 2.4).

  1. Two-high: two rolls turning in one direction. The work is returned over the top. It is simple and used for small reductions.
  2. Two-high reversing: the roll direction is reversed after each pass. Used for blooming, slabbing and plate. It needs a powerful reversible drive.
  3. Three-high: three rolls; the work goes forward through the bottom pair and back through the top pair. No reversal is needed, but lifting tables are needed.
  4. Four-high: two small work rolls and two large backup rolls. Small work rolls need less force. The backup rolls stop bending. Used for hot and cold sheet.
  5. Cluster (Sendzimir): a very small work roll supported by many backup rolls. Used for thin and hard strip such as stainless steel.
  6. Tandem: several stands in line. The work passes through all of them in one go and the speed rises along the line. High output.

Rolling of bars and shapes.

  • The rolls have grooves cut around them. A pair of grooves forms a pass (Fig. 2.5).
  • For round bars: billet → box pass → diamond / square → oval → round. The bar is turned 90° between passes to control the spread and avoid fins.
  • For shapes such as I-beams, channels, angles and rails: bloom → roughing passes (dog-bone) → intermediate → finishing pass. Edging rolls and universal mills with vertical rolls shape the flanges.
  • The reduction must be balanced across the section so that all parts cool and elongate together. Otherwise residual stress, cracks and twist occur.

Q4. Explain the following rolling defects with a neat sketch: (a) wavy edges (b) zipper cracks (c) edge cracks (d) centre splits (e) alligatoring (f) folds.

MT QB Q21

Refer to Fig. 2.6, then give cause and remedy for each defect in points.

Model answer

Fig. 2.6 shows the six defects as sketches. Rolling defects arise from uneven deformation, wrong temperature or poor ingot quality.

  1. Wavy edges. The rolls bend under load, so the strip is thicker at the centre. The edges are thinned more and become longer than the centre, so they ripple. Remedy: crowned or bent rolls, backup rolls (four-high mill), smaller draft.
  2. Zipper cracks. Short cracks along the centre line, like a zip. The centre has too little ductility for the tension it feels (low temperature or too much draft). Remedy: higher rolling temperature, lower draft.
  3. Edge cracks. Cracks start at the edges. The edges cool first, are less ductile and are stretched by spread. Remedy: keep the edges hot, use edging passes, reduce the draft.
  4. Centre split. A crack in the mid-thickness plane. In thick sections with a light draft the surface is worked but the core gets tensile stress. Remedy: heavier draft per pass, uniform heating through the thickness.
  5. Alligatoring. The front end of a slab or plate splits along the mid-plane into two parts like the jaws of an alligator. The surface and the core flow unevenly, or the ingot has poor structure. Remedy: lower draft, even heating, sound ingots.
  6. Folds. A fin is squeezed out by too much draft or a wrong pass shape. It is folded and rolled into the surface in the next pass and shows a lap. Remedy: correct pass design, lower draft, square edges.

Q7. Explain hot extrusion (direct and indirect), cold (impact) extrusion and hydrostatic extrusion with neat sketches.

Syllabus topic

Fig. 2.12 for hot extrusion with a comparison table, Fig. 2.13 and Fig. 2.14 for the others.

Model answer

Hot extrusion (Fig. 2.12). A heated billet is pushed through a die. Typical temperatures: aluminium 350–500 °C, copper 750–950 °C, steel 1100–1250 °C.

  • Direct: the ram pushes the billet toward the die; the product moves with the ram. A dummy block protects the ram. High friction at the container wall raises the force.
  • Indirect: the die is on a hollow ram that moves into a closed container. The billet does not move, so friction is small and force is about 25 to 30 % lower.

Cold (impact) extrusion (Fig. 2.13). A slug of soft metal is struck at room temperature by a fast punch. In backward extrusion the metal flows up around the punch and forms a cup. In forward extrusion it flows through a die opening. Uses: collapsible tubes, battery cans, cartridge cases. Advantages: good finish and strength, close tolerance, no scrap.

Hydrostatic extrusion (Fig. 2.14). A fluid at high pressure surrounds the billet and pushes its tapered nose through the die. There is no friction at the container wall.

Q8. With a neat sketch explain (a) wire drawing (b) rod drawing (c) tube drawing.

MT QB Q23

Fig. 2.15, Fig. 2.16 and Fig. 2.17; point out the die, the pull, and the support for the tube.

Model answer

Drawing. Drawing pulls stock through a tapered die to reduce its size. The pull is tensile, but the die wall squeezes the metal. Area reduction r = (A₀ − A₁)/A₀, usually 20–30 % per pass.

(a) Wire drawing (Fig. 2.15).

  • A rod of 5 to 10 mm is cleaned and pointed. The point is passed through the die and gripped.
  • A rotating drawing block (capstan) pulls the wire. The diameter falls from d₀ to d₁.
  • The die has an approach cone, a bearing land and a back relief. Material: tungsten carbide or diamond.
  • Lubricant (soap) reduces friction and wear. Wire is annealed between passes. Several dies in series are used.
  • Uses: electric wire, springs, ropes, nails.

(b) Rod drawing (Fig. 2.16).

  • Straight rods or bars are drawn on a draw bench.
  • The die is fixed in a holder. The pointed end of the rod passes through the die and is gripped by tongs on a carriage.
  • A chain or hydraulic cylinder pulls the carriage along the bench. The rod is drawn in a single length.
  • Gives bright bars with accurate size and good finish, and raises strength by strain hardening.

(c) Tube drawing (Fig. 2.17). The die reduces the outer diameter. The inside is controlled in four ways:

  1. Sinking: no inside support. Diameter falls; the wall thickens a little.
  2. Fixed plug: a plug on a rod is held at the die. The inside size and wall are controlled.
  3. Floating plug: a plug without a rod takes its position at the die. Long tubes can be coiled.
  4. Moving mandrel: a long mandrel runs inside and moves with the tube. Gives the best inside finish and a thin wall.

Q9. Illustrate the following metal forming processes with a neat sketch: (a) piercing (b) blanking.

MT QB Q14

Fig. 2.18 for both; state which piece is the product; add the clearance rule and force formula.

Model answer

Both are shearing operations on a press. A punch moves down through the sheet into a die opening. The metal fails by shear along the cutting edges (Fig. 2.18).

(a) Blanking. The punch cuts a piece out of the strip. The cut-out piece is the blank and is the product. The remaining strip is scrap.

  • The strip lies on the die. The stripper holds it down.
  • The die opening is made to the blank size. The punch is smaller by the clearance.

(b) Piercing. The punch makes a hole in the sheet. The cut-out slug is waste and the sheet with holes is the product.

  • The punch is made to the hole size. The die opening is larger by the clearance.
  • Clearance is about 5 to 10 % of the sheet thickness on each side. Too little clearance raises force and wear. Too much clearance gives burrs.
  • Cutting force F = L t τs, where L = cut length, t = thickness and τs = shear strength.
  • For seamless tube, rotary piercing (Fig. 2.19) uses skewed rolls and a plug to pierce a hot billet.

Uses. Washers, coins, brackets and sheet parts with holes.
