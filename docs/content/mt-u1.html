Casting Processes and Melting Furnaces

Casting makes complex metal parts by pouring molten metal into a mould. This unit covers the metals that are cast, the furnaces that melt them (cupola, electric arc, resistance, induction), and the special casting methods: CO₂ and shell moulding, die casting, centrifugal, squeeze and continuous casting.

Concepts

1. Manufacturing processes

Manufacturing is the conversion of raw material into a finished product of the required shape, size and properties, by a planned sequence of processes. Each process adds value to the material.

We group the processes by what they do to the material. Fig. 1.1 shows the five groups.

  • Casting: pour molten metal into a mould cavity and let it solidify.
  • Forming: change the shape of solid metal by plastic deformation (rolling, forging, extrusion, drawing).
  • Machining: remove excess material as chips to get the final shape.
  • Joining: join separate parts (welding, brazing, soldering, riveting).
  • Finishing and heat treatment: improve surface and properties without a large change of shape.

2. Metals cast in the foundry and choice of alloy

Almost every metal can be cast. We split cast metals into ferrous (iron base) and non-ferrous (no iron base). Fig. 1.2 shows the tree.

Factors that decide the casting alloy (QB question):

  • Required properties: strength, hardness, ductility, toughness, fatigue and wear resistance.
  • Castability: fluidity, shrinkage, hot-tearing tendency, gas absorption, melting range.
  • Service condition: temperature, corrosion, pressure tightness, vibration damping.
  • Melting point and furnace: high melting alloys need costlier furnaces and moulds.
  • Process and shape: section thickness, size and complexity suit some alloys and processes only.
  • Machining and heat treatment: machinability, weldability and response to heat treatment.
  • Cost and quantity: metal price, melting loss, availability and the number of parts.

3. Casting and melting furnaces

The furnace is chosen by the metal, the melting rate, the quality needed and the cost. Fig. 1.3 to 1.7 show the furnaces you must know.

3.1 Cupola furnace (cast iron)

The cupola is a vertical shaft furnace that melts cast iron in large amounts. It is the oldest and cheapest foundry furnace. Metal, coke and flux enter at the top. Air enters through tuyeres near the bottom.

Charge (from the charging door): pig iron, cast-iron scrap and steel scrap; coke as fuel (about 1 part per 8 to 10 parts of metal by mass); flux (limestone, about 25 % of coke mass) to form a slag that removes ash and impurities.

Zones (from the top): preheating zone, melting zone, reducing zone, combustion zone, well.

ZoneWhat happens
Preheating (stack)Hot gas dries and warms the charge to about 1000 °C.
MeltingIron melts at about 1200 °C and drips down through the coke.
Reducing (protective)CO₂ + C → 2CO (heat absorbed). The CO gas protects the iron from oxidation.
Combustion (oxidising)Just above the tuyeres. C + O₂ → CO₂ gives intense heat, about 1500 to 1800 °C.
Well (hearth)Molten metal collects here under a layer of slag.

Working. Light a coke bed with wood. Add the charge in layers. Start the air blast. The metal melts, runs into the well, and is tapped into ladles. Slag is removed from the slag hole. At the end of the heat, the drop-bottom doors open and the remaining charge falls out.

Merits: simple, cheap, continuous operation, high melting rate. Demerits: only for cast iron, poor composition control, silicon and manganese burn off, sulphur pick-up from coke.

3.2 Electric arc furnaces

Electric arc furnaces melt steel and alloys. The arc gives temperatures up to about 3000 °C. There are two types.

Direct arc furnace (Fig. 1.4). Three graphite electrodes come down through the roof. The arc strikes between each electrode and the charge, so the current passes through the metal. It is used for steel in large heats (up to 200 t).

Indirect arc furnace (Fig. 1.5). It has a horizontal barrel with two electrodes entering from the ends. The arc burns between the electrodes above the metal. Heat reaches the charge by radiation. The barrel rocks to stir the bath. It is used for small heats of non-ferrous alloys and cast iron.

3.3 Resistance furnace

Current passes through a heating element of high resistance. The element gives heat . The heat passes to the charge by radiation and convection. Elements are nichrome (up to 1100 °C), silicon carbide (up to about 1500 °C) or molybdenum disilicide. The furnace melts low-melting metals (Al, Zn, Mg) and holds metal at a set temperature. Fig. 1.6 shows the layout.

Merits: clean, accurate temperature control, no fumes. Demerits: slow, high power cost, limited temperature, element life is short.

3.4 Coreless induction furnace

A water-cooled copper coil surrounds a refractory crucible. Alternating current in the coil makes a changing magnetic field. This induces eddy currents in the metal, and the metal heats itself. The same field stirs the melt. There is no iron core, so it is “coreless”. Fig. 1.7 shows it.

Merits: fast, clean, good stirring, close composition control. Demerits: no refining of the metal (charge must be clean), high capital cost. Used for steel, cast iron and copper and aluminium alloys.

3.5 Crucible furnace

A pot (crucible) of clay–graphite or silicon carbide holds the metal. Fuel (coke, oil or gas) burns round the pot. The pot may be in a pit, tilt, or lift out. It melts small batches (up to a few hundred kg) of brass, bronze, aluminium and zinc. It is simple and cheap but slow, and the crucible life is short.

4. CO₂ moulding and shell moulding

CO₂ moulding uses sand mixed with 3 to 5 % sodium silicate (water glass). The sand is rammed round the pattern. CO₂ gas (1 to 2 bar, 10 to 60 s) passes through vent holes. The reaction makes silica gel, which binds the sand into a hard mould. Fig. 1.8 shows the steps.

Merits: fast (no baking), accurate, good surface, high mould strength. Demerits: poor collapsibility, sand is hard to reuse, the sand mix has a short life, and over-gassing weakens the mould.

Shell moulding (Croning process) makes a thin hard shell (5 to 10 mm) of sand and thermosetting resin on a heated metal pattern. Fig. 1.9 shows the six steps.

Merits: smooth surface, accuracy about ±0.25 mm, high production rate, low sand use. Demerits: costly metal pattern, resin is expensive, size limit. Used for gear housings, cylinder heads, connecting rods and camshafts.

5. Die casting

Die casting forces molten metal under high pressure into a reusable steel mould called a die. The die has two halves: a fixed half and a moving half. The metal freezes quickly, and ejector pins push the casting out. Dies are used for non-ferrous alloys (Zn, Al, Mg, Cu, Sn, Pb). There are two machine types.

Hot chamber machine (Fig. 1.10). The injection cylinder and plunger sit inside the molten metal in the furnace pot. The plunger forces metal through a gooseneck and nozzle into the die. Pressure is 7 to 35 MPa. It is fast (up to a few hundred shots per hour). It suits low-melting alloys (Zn, Sn, Pb, some Mg). Aluminium cannot be used: it attacks the iron parts.

Cold chamber machine (Fig. 1.11). The furnace is separate. A ladle pours metal into a horizontal shot sleeve. A hydraulic plunger forces the metal into the die at 20 to 150 MPa. It suits Al, Cu and Mg alloys of high melting point. It is slower than the hot chamber.

Low pressure die casting (Fig. 1.12). A sealed furnace sits below the die. Air at 0.2 to 1 bar presses on the melt. The metal rises up a riser tube and fills the die from below with little turbulence. Used for aluminium wheels and cylinder heads.

Gravity vs pressure die casting. In gravity die casting (permanent mould casting) the metal enters the die by its own weight. In pressure die casting a machine injects it at high pressure.

Gravity die casting is slower and cheaper. It gives thicker walls and less porosity, and sand cores can be used. Pressure die casting is very fast and gives thin walls and fine detail. Q10 gives the full table.

6. Centrifugal casting

Centrifugal force pushes molten metal against the mould wall. There are three methods.

True centrifugal casting (Fig. 1.13). A cylindrical mould spins about its own horizontal (or vertical) axis. Metal is poured in and forms a hollow cylinder. There is no core. The bore is round and its size depends on the amount of metal poured. Used for pipes, tubes, cylinder liners and bushes.

N = speed (rpm), r = inside radius of the casting (m), g = 9.81 m/s², G = gravity factor (about 60 to 80 for horizontal pipes)

Merits: no core, no gating or riser, dense fine grain, impurities move to the bore and are machined off. Demerits: only round hollow shapes, poor bore accuracy, segregation of heavy elements, costly machine.

Semi-centrifugal casting (Fig. 1.14). The mould spins about a vertical axis. The metal enters by a central sprue. The centre of the casting is solid (or cored). The central sprue acts as a riser and is cut off later. Used for wheels, pulleys, gear blanks and flywheels.

Centrifuging (Fig. 1.15). Many small mould cavities are placed round a central sprue, away from the axis. The mould spins and the force fills each cavity under pressure. Used for small, thin parts, jewellery and dental castings.

7. Squeeze casting and continuous casting

Squeeze casting (liquid metal forging). A measured dose of molten metal goes into the lower die. The upper die (punch) closes and applies 50 to 150 MPa. The metal freezes under this pressure. Fig. 1.16 shows the steps.

Merits: almost no porosity, fine grain, high strength, near-net shape, good surface, can be heat treated. Demerits: slower, costly die, accurate dosing of metal needed, simple shapes only. Used for aluminium pistons, wheels and suspension parts.

Continuous casting (Fig. 1.17). Molten steel goes from the ladle into a tundish, then into an open-ended, water-cooled copper mould. A solid skin forms. Rolls pull the strand out and spray water cools it until the core is solid. The strand is straightened and cut by a torch. The product is a slab, bloom or billet for rolling.

Merits: yield 95 to 98 % (ingots about 80 to 85 %), no ingot moulds, uniform quality, low energy and labour. Demerits: high capital cost, fixed section shapes, needs a steady supply of metal, centre-line segregation.

Theory questions

Q1. Define manufacturing process. Classify the manufacturing processes.

MT QB Q6 · Jun-Jul 2025 Q1(b)

Definition, then a block diagram with the five groups and examples.

Model answer

Definition. A manufacturing process is a planned operation or series of operations that changes the shape, size, surface or properties of raw material to make a useful product. Each step adds value to the material.

Classification. Fig. 1.1 shows the block diagram. We classify the processes by their action on the material:

  • Casting processes: molten metal is poured into a mould and solidifies, for example sand, die, centrifugal, investment and continuous casting.
  • Forming (deformation) processes: solid metal is shaped by plastic flow, for example rolling, forging, extrusion, drawing and sheet-metal work.
  • Material removal (machining) processes: extra metal is removed as chips, for example turning, drilling, milling, shaping and grinding.
  • Joining processes: parts are joined permanently or temporarily, for example welding, brazing, soldering, riveting and adhesive bonding.
  • Finishing and property-changing processes: heat treatment (annealing, hardening), plating, painting and polishing.

Another common split is primary shaping (casting, forming, powder metallurgy) and secondary processes (machining, joining, finishing).

Q2. What are the metals cast in a foundry? Classify them and mention the factors that determine the selection of a casting alloy.

MT QB Q12 · Syllabus topic

Classification tree (Fig. 1.2), then 7 to 9 factors with one line each.

Model answer

Metals cast. The foundry casts ferrous metals (cast iron, cast steel) and non-ferrous metals (aluminium, copper, magnesium, zinc, nickel, titanium and their alloys). Fig. 1.2 shows the classification.

  • Ferrous: grey iron, white iron, malleable iron, SG iron, plain carbon steel and alloy steels.
  • Non-ferrous: Al alloys, Cu alloys (brass, bronze), Mg alloys, Zn alloys, Ni and Ti alloys.

Factors for selecting a casting alloy.

  1. Mechanical properties: strength, hardness, ductility, toughness, fatigue and wear resistance needed in service.
  2. Castability: fluidity, shrinkage and hot-tearing tendency, gas absorption, freezing range.
  3. Service condition: operating temperature, corrosion, pressure tightness, damping.
  4. Melting temperature: it fixes the furnace, mould material and energy use.
  5. Size, shape and wall thickness: thin and complex parts need very fluid alloys.
  6. Casting process: some alloys suit only certain processes (Zn in hot chamber die casting).
  7. Machinability, weldability and heat treatment: the part may need these later.
  8. Cost and availability: metal price, melting loss, scrap and production quantity.
  9. Weight and density: light alloys for aircraft and automobiles.

Q3. With a neat sketch, explain the cupola furnace.

MT QB Q7

Sketch Fig. 1.3 with zones, then charge, working, merits, demerits.

Model answer

Definition. The cupola is a vertical shaft furnace used to melt cast iron. Coke burns in a blast of air and melts the metal by direct contact.

Construction (Fig. 1.3). A cylindrical steel shell is lined with firebrick. It stands on columns and has drop-bottom doors. A wind box round the lower shell supplies air to the tuyeres. The charging door is near the top, and a spark arrester caps the stack. The molten metal leaves through the tap hole, and the slag leaves through the slag hole.

Charge. Pig iron, cast-iron scrap, steel scrap, coke (1 part to 8 or 10 parts of metal) and flux (limestone, about 25 % of the coke mass).

Zones (top to bottom):

  • Preheating zone: hot gas warms the charge to about 1000 °C.
  • Melting zone: iron melts at about 1200 °C and drips down.
  • Reducing zone: CO₂ + C → 2CO; the CO protects the metal from oxidation.
  • Combustion zone: C + O₂ → CO₂; the temperature is highest (1500 to 1800 °C).
  • Well: molten metal and slag collect; the slag floats on the metal.

Working.

  1. Dry the lining, put a sand bed on the drop-bottom doors, and light a bed of coke with wood.
  2. Add alternate layers of metal, coke and flux through the charging door.
  3. Start the air blast. The metal melts and runs down into the well.
  4. Tap the metal into ladles at intervals. Remove slag through the slag hole.
  5. At the end, open the drop-bottom doors and let the charge fall out.

Merits: simple, cheap, high melting rate, continuous operation. Demerits: only for cast iron, poor control of composition, loss of Si and Mn, pick-up of sulphur, dust and smoke.

Q5. With a neat sketch, explain the resistance furnace.

MT QB Q13

Principle, sketch Fig. 1.6 with labels, working, merits, demerits.

Model answer

Principle. When current passes through a conductor of high resistance, it produces heat: . The resistance furnace uses this effect.

Construction (Fig. 1.6). A steel casing holds a thick layer of refractory and insulating brick. Heating elements (nichrome wire up to 1100 °C, silicon carbide rods up to about 1500 °C) line the walls and floor. A crucible with the charge stands on a refractory stand in the chamber. A thermocouple measures the temperature. A controller switches the supply on and off.

Working.

  1. Load the charge in the crucible and close the lid.
  2. Switch on the supply. The elements become hot.
  3. Heat passes to the crucible by radiation and convection, and the metal melts.
  4. The thermocouple and controller hold the set temperature.
  5. Tilt or lift out the crucible and pour.

Merits: clean, no fuel gases, close temperature control, even heating. Demerits: slow, high power cost, limited maximum temperature, short element life.

Applications: melting Al, Zn, Mg and Sn alloys; holding furnaces for die casting; heat treatment.

Q6. With constructional details, explain the working of the coreless induction furnace. Write a note on the crucible furnace.

Jun-Jul 2025 Q3(b) · Syllabus topic

Model answer

Construction (Fig. 1.7). A refractory crucible holds the metal. A water-cooled copper coil surrounds it. Laminated yokes guide the magnetic flux, and a lid and spout complete the furnace. A medium-frequency AC supply feeds the coil. There is no iron core, so the furnace is “coreless”.

Working.

  1. Put the clean charge in the crucible.
  2. Pass AC through the coil. It sets up an alternating magnetic field.
  3. The field induces eddy currents in the charge. The heat is , and it forms inside the metal.
  4. The same field stirs the bath. This mixes the alloy and keeps the temperature even.
  5. Tilt the furnace to pour through the spout.

Merits: fast melting, clean, close composition control, good stirring, little melt loss. Demerits: no refining, so the charge must be clean; high capital cost; thin lining wears fast. Uses: steel, cast iron, Cu and Al alloys.

Crucible furnace. A crucible of clay–graphite or silicon carbide stands in a chamber. Coke, oil or gas burns round it. The furnace may be pit, tilting or lift-out type. It melts small batches (up to a few hundred kg) of brass, bronze, aluminium and zinc. It is cheap and simple but slow, and the crucible life is short.

Definition. CO₂ moulding is a sand-moulding process in which sand mixed with sodium silicate is hardened by passing CO₂ gas through it.

Sand mix. Clean silica sand with 3 to 5 % sodium silicate (water glass). No clay is added.

Steps (Fig. 1.8):

  2. Press a gassing head on the mould. Pass CO₂ at 1 to 2 bar for 10 to 60 s.
  3. CO₂ reacts: . The silica gel binds the sand grains into a hard mould.
  4. Draw the pattern from the hard mould. Add cores, close the mould and pour.

Q8. Illustrate the different steps involved in the shell moulding process.

MT QB Q3

Six steps with Fig. 1.9, then merits, demerits and applications.

Model answer

Definition. Shell moulding (Croning process) makes a thin hard shell mould of sand and thermosetting resin on a heated metal pattern.

Steps (Fig. 1.9):

  1. Heat the pattern (metal, 175 to 370 °C) and spray it with silicone as a parting agent.
  2. Clamp the pattern to the dump box. The box holds sand coated with 2.5 to 4 % phenolic resin.
  3. Invert the box. The sand falls on the hot pattern. The resin melts and forms a shell, 5 to 10 mm thick, in 10 to 30 s.
  4. Turn the box back. Loose sand falls off. Cure the shell with the pattern in an oven at about 300 °C for 1 to 3 min.
  5. Strip the shell from the pattern with ejector pins.
  6. Join two shell halves with clamps or adhesive. Back them with steel shot or sand in a flask, and pour.

Merits: smooth surface, accuracy about ±0.25 mm, high production rate, low sand use, thin sections possible. Demerits: costly metal pattern, resin is expensive, size limit (small and medium castings). Uses: gear housings, cylinder heads, connecting rods, camshafts, small valve bodies.

Q9. What is die casting? With a neat sketch explain the hot chamber die casting process. Add a note on the cold chamber process.

MT QB Q4 · Syllabus topic

Definition, Fig. 1.10 with steps, merits and limits, then Fig. 1.11 for the cold chamber and a comparison table.

Model answer

Definition. Die casting is a permanent-mould process in which molten metal is forced under high pressure into a steel die. The metal freezes under pressure, and ejector pins push out the casting.

Hot chamber process (Fig. 1.10). The furnace pot is part of the machine. The cylinder and plunger sit in the molten metal. A gooseneck and nozzle lead to the die.

  1. Close the die halves. The plunger is up, and metal fills the cylinder through the port.
  2. The plunger moves down. It seals the port and forces the metal through the gooseneck and nozzle into the die at 7 to 35 MPa.
  3. Keep the pressure until the metal freezes (a few seconds).
  4. The plunger rises. The moving die half opens, and the ejector pins push out the casting.
  5. Trim the gate and flash. Close the die and repeat.

Merits: very fast (several hundred shots per hour), thin walls, close tolerance (±0.1 mm), fine surface, low labour. Demerits: only for low-melting alloys (Zn, Sn, Pb, some Mg); high die and machine cost; size limit; air may be trapped as porosity. Uses: carburettors, toys, small housings, fittings.

Cold chamber process (Fig. 1.11). The furnace is separate. A ladle pours a measured dose into the shot sleeve through the pour hole. A hydraulic plunger forces it into the die at 20 to 150 MPa. It is used for Al, Mg and Cu alloys. It is slower, because metal must be ladled each cycle. The plunger is not in the metal, so aluminium cannot attack it.

Q11. Explain with a neat sketch the centrifugal casting process. State its advantages and disadvantages.

MT QB Q2 · Jun-Jul 2025 Q4(b)

Definition, Fig. 1.13 for true centrifugal casting, steps, speed formula, advantages, disadvantages.

Model answer

Definition. In centrifugal casting, molten metal is poured into a rotating mould. Centrifugal force throws the metal outwards, so it fills the mould and solidifies under pressure. There are three types: true centrifugal, semi-centrifugal and centrifuging.

True centrifugal casting (Fig. 1.13). A cylindrical mould of steel or cast iron rotates about its own axis, which is horizontal for long pipes. Rollers support it, and a motor drives it.

  1. Heat the mould and coat it with refractory wash. Start the rotation.
  2. Pour the metal through a trough at one end. Centrifugal force spreads it over the wall.
  3. The outer layer freezes first and the lighter impurities move towards the bore.
  4. Stop the rotation after solidification. Remove the casting.

N = speed (rpm), r = inside radius (m), g = 9.81 m/s², G = gravity factor (60 to 80 for horizontal pipes)

Advantages: no core, no gates and risers (high yield), dense fine grain, good strength, impurities are removed in the bore, good for pipes and liners.

Disadvantages: only symmetrical hollow shapes, poor bore accuracy, segregation of heavy elements, high machine cost. Uses: cast-iron pipes, cylinder liners, bushes, gun barrels.

Q12. With a neat sketch, explain the semi-centrifugal casting process. Add a note on centrifuging.

MT QB Q5 · Syllabus topic

Sketch Fig. 1.14 and explain how the central sprue acts as a riser; then Fig. 1.15.

Model answer

Semi-centrifugal casting (Fig. 1.14). The mould spins about a vertical axis. The metal enters through a central sprue. The casting is usually solid, or has a core in the centre, and the cavity is filled under centrifugal force.

  1. Fix the sand or metal mould on the table with its axis on the spindle axis.
  2. Start the rotation at a medium speed (lower than for true centrifugal casting).
  3. Pour the metal into the pouring cup. It flows down the central sprue and outwards into the cavity (rim, web and hub).
  4. The central sprue acts as a riser. It feeds shrinkage and is cut off after solidification.
  5. Stop the table, break the mould and clean the casting.

Advantages: dense rim, sound casting, many moulds can be stacked and spun together. Limitations: symmetrical shapes only, a large sprue is wasted. Uses: wheels, pulleys, gear blanks, flywheels.

Centrifuging (Fig. 1.15). Several small cavities are arranged round a central sprue, away from the axis. The mould spins and the force fills each cavity. The shape need not be symmetrical. It is used for small and thin parts such as jewellery, dental work and valve trim.

Q13. With a neat sketch explain squeeze casting. Mention its merits and demerits.

MT QB Q9 · Jun-Jul 2025 Q4(a)

Sketch Fig. 1.16 in three stages; give pressure; merits, demerits, applications.

Model answer

Definition. Squeeze casting (liquid metal forging) is a process in which molten metal solidifies in a die under a high applied pressure. It joins casting and forging.

Steps (Fig. 1.16):

  1. Heat the die to 200 to 300 °C and spray it with lubricant.
  2. Pour a measured dose of molten metal into the lower die.
  3. Close the upper die (punch). Apply 50 to 150 MPa. The metal fills the cavity.
  4. Hold the pressure until the casting solidifies.
  5. Raise the punch. The ejector pushes out the casting.

In the direct method the punch presses on the metal. In the indirect method a shot plunger injects the metal into the die.

Merits: almost zero porosity, fine grain, high strength, near-net shape, good surface, heat-treatable parts, can make metal matrix composites. Demerits: slow cycle, costly die, accurate dosing needed, simple shapes only, die wear. Applications: aluminium pistons, wheels, suspension knuckles, brake parts.

Q14. With a neat sketch, explain the continuous casting process. Mention its merits and demerits.

MT QB Q10

Sketch Fig. 1.17 with ladle, tundish, mould, sprays, rolls and torch; then merits and demerits.

Model answer

Definition. In continuous casting, molten metal is poured without a break into a water-cooled mould that has no bottom. A solid strand is pulled out of the mould continuously and cut to length.

Working (Fig. 1.17):

  1. The ladle pours the molten steel into the tundish, which feeds the mould at a steady rate.
  2. Steel flows through the nozzle into the copper mould. Water cools the mould, and the mould oscillates to stop sticking. A solid skin forms.
  3. Rolls pull the strand out at a fixed speed. Water sprays and support rolls cool the strand until the core is solid.
  4. The strand bends, then straightens, and a gas torch cuts it into slabs, blooms or billets.
  5. The cut pieces go to the rolling mill.

Merits: high yield (95 to 98 % against 80 to 85 % for ingots), no ingot moulds and no soaking pits, uniform structure, low labour and energy. Demerits: high capital cost, fixed shapes and sizes, needs a steady supply of metal, risk of breakout, centre-line segregation.

Q15. List the advantages and limitations of the casting process.

Jun-Jul 2025 Q6(c) · Syllabus topic

Four or five points on each side, with one reason each.

Model answer

Advantages

  • Complex shapes, with internal cavities, are made in one piece.
  • Sizes range from a few grams to over 100 t.
  • Almost every metal and alloy can be cast.
  • Low cost for large quantity, and near-net shape saves machining.
  • Properties are nearly the same in all directions.
  • Heavy parts that cannot be forged are cast.

Limitations

  • Surface finish and accuracy are lower (sand casting).
  • Defects occur: porosity, shrinkage, blowholes, hot tears.
  • Strength is usually lower than for wrought metal.
  • Pattern and mould cost is high for a few parts.
  • Casting is labour-intensive, and it needs fettling and cleaning.
  • Hot metal, dust and fumes are a safety and health risk.
