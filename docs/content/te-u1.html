1. Thermodynamics, macroscopic and microscopic approaches

Thermodynamics is the science of energy and its transformation. It deals with heat, work and the properties of substances that connect them. Engines, boilers, turbines, compressors and refrigerators are all studied with it.

A substance can be studied in two ways. In the microscopic (statistical) approach we follow every molecule: its position, velocity and energy. In the macroscopic (classical) approach we look at the whole body and use a few measurable properties such as pressure, volume and temperature.

Macroscopic (classical)Microscopic (statistical)
Behaviour of the matter as a whole (bulk)Behaviour of each individual molecule
Few properties: p, V, TMany variables: position and velocity of every molecule
Properties can be measured directly with instrumentsProperties cannot be measured directly; they are averaged by statistics

This course uses the macroscopic approach and treats the substance as a continuum (a property such as density has a value at every point).

2. System, surroundings and boundary

A thermodynamic system is a definite quantity of matter or a region of space chosen for study. Everything outside it is the surroundings. The real or imaginary surface that separates them is the boundary. System plus surroundings together make the universe.

  • The boundary can be fixed (a rigid tank wall) or moving (a piston face).
  • The boundary can be real (the metal wall of a cylinder) or imaginary (a dashed line round the steam that enters a turbine).
  • The boundary has zero thickness, so it has no mass and no volume. Heat and work are the energy that crosses it.

In a closed system the same mass stays inside, so it is also called a control mass. The volume may change, as in the cylinder above.

An open system is studied with a control volume (CV): a fixed region of space chosen around the device. Its boundary is called the control surface (CS). Mass flows through the control surface. The control volume is fixed in space, but it can sometimes deform.

A homogeneous system has the same composition and phase throughout (water only). A heterogeneous system has more than one phase (water plus steam). An adiabatic boundary does not allow heat to cross; a diathermal boundary does.

4. Properties: intensive and extensive

A property is any measurable characteristic that describes the state of a system, for example p, V, T, u, h. A property depends only on the state, not on how the system reached it.

  • Extensive property: depends on the size (mass) of the system. It is additive: if we divide the system, the property divides. Examples: volume, mass, energy, weight.
  • Intensive property: independent of the size of the system. It does not change if we divide the system. Examples: pressure, temperature, density.

The ratio of an extensive property to the mass is a specific property, for example specific volume v = V/m. A specific property is always intensive.

5. State, path, process and cycle

  • State: the condition of a system at an instant, given by its properties. Two independent properties fix the state of a simple compressible substance.
  • Path: the series of states the system passes through.
  • Process: the change from one equilibrium state to another along a path.
  • Cycle: a series of processes that returns the system to its initial state; all properties return to their original values.

6. Thermodynamic equilibrium

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium if no change of its properties occurs when it is isolated from the surroundings. Properties are then uniform and do not change with time. It needs three kinds of equilibrium together:

  • Mechanical equilibrium: no unbalanced force. Pressure is uniform inside the system and equal to the outside pressure across the boundary.
  • Thermal equilibrium: no temperature difference. The temperature is uniform and equal to that of the surroundings, so there is no heat flow.
  • Chemical equilibrium: no chemical reaction and no transfer of matter between regions or phases. The chemical composition does not change.

Properties of a system have meaning only in an equilibrium state.

7. Quasi-static (quasi-equilibrium) process

A quasi-static process is one that is carried out so slowly that the system passes through a series of equilibrium states, each very near the next. The system is at every instant nearly in equilibrium, so it has a definite p and T and the path can be drawn as a smooth curve on the p–V diagram.

Example: gas in a cylinder is held down by a pile of tiny weights. We remove them one at a time. After each removal the piston rises a little and the gas settles at a new equilibrium. If we remove all the weights at once, the gas expands violently. Its pressure is not uniform, and no path can be drawn.

Characteristic feature. A quasi-static process is made of a succession of equilibrium states, and it can be shown by a continuous line on a property diagram. Every reversible process is quasi-static, but a quasi-static process with friction is not reversible. The work of a quasi-static process is .

8. Temperature and temperature scales

Temperature is the property that decides the direction of heat flow: heat flows from a body at higher temperature to a body at lower temperature. It is a measure of the average molecular kinetic energy. We measure it by a thermometric property, a property that changes in a regular way with temperature.

9. Thermal equilibrium and the Zeroth Law

Two bodies are in thermal equilibrium when they have the same temperature, so no heat flows if they are brought in contact through a diathermal wall.

Zeroth Law. If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
  • The law is the basis of temperature measurement: the third body C is the thermometer. If it reads the same when in contact with A and with B, then TA = TB.
  • It defines temperature as a property: the quantity that is equal for bodies in thermal equilibrium.
  • It was stated after the first and second laws were already numbered, so it is called the zeroth law.

10. Thermodynamic work

Work is energy transfer across the system boundary when a system interacts with the surroundings by a force through a distance. In thermodynamics, work is done by a system if the sole effect on the surroundings could be the raising of a weight. Work is energy in transit, a path function, written δW.

Sign convention. Work done by the system on the surroundings is positive (expansion). Work done on the system is negative (compression). Unit: joule (J = N·m); power in W.
Mechanics versus thermodynamic work. In mechanics, work needs a force moving through a distance. In thermodynamics, work is any energy crossing the boundary that is not heat. Example: a battery that heats a resistor does thermodynamic work on the system (electrical work), although no force moves through a distance. Another example: a gas expanding into a vacuum moves the boundary but does no work because there is no resisting force.

Other forms of work

  • Shaft work: a torque T turns a shaft through an angle θ; W = Tθ, and power = Tω = 2πNT/60 (N in rpm). Turbines and compressors deliver and take shaft work.
  • Paddle-wheel work: a paddle stirs a fluid, driven by a falling weight (Joule's experiment). The system receives work (negative); the fluid warms.
  • Electrical work: W = VIt. The system receives work (negative) when current flows into it.
  • Others: spring work (½kx²), stretching of a film, magnetic work.

11. Heat transfer

Heat is the energy that crosses the boundary of a system because of a temperature difference between the system and the surroundings. It is energy in transit, a path function (δQ), measured in joule (J) or kJ. A process with no heat transfer is adiabatic (Q = 0).

Heat added to the system is positive; heat rejected by the system is negative. Work done by the system is positive. Heat rejected and work done on the system are negative.

Heat and temperature rise. Heat does not always raise temperature: during melting or boiling it is latent heat and T stays constant. Temperature can also rise without heat, by compression or stirring (work). A body stores internal energy, not heat.
HeatWork
Energy transfer due to a temperature differenceEnergy transfer due to any driving force other than temperature difference (pressure, voltage, torque)
Low-grade energy: cannot be completely converted to workHigh-grade energy: can be completely converted to heat
Heat added is +ve (by convention)Work done by the system is +ve
Similarities: both are boundary phenomena; both are transient (they exist only while crossing the boundary); both are path functions (inexact differentials, δ); both are energy in transit, not properties; the system does not possess heat or work; unit joule.

12. Specific heats

The specific heat is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K. The value depends on the process, so there are two for a gas:

  • cv, specific heat at constant volume: heat added per kg per K while the volume is constant.
  • cp, specific heat at constant pressure: heat added per kg per K while the pressure is constant.

Unit of c: kJ/kg·K. For air: cp = 1.005, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.4. For water c ≈ 4.18 kJ/kg·K.

Why cp > cv. At constant volume the heat only raises the internal energy. At constant pressure the gas also expands and does work p dV, so more heat is needed for the same ΔT.

Relation cp − cv = R (ideal gas). For an ideal gas, internal energy and enthalpy depend only on T: du = cvdT and dh = cpdT. Since h = u + pv = u + RT, we get dh = du + R dT. Hence

R = specific gas constant (kJ/kg·K). Air: R = 0.287, γ = 1.4, so cv = 0.7175 and cp = 1.0045 kJ/kg·K.

13. Forms of energy

Energy is the capacity to do work. A system can have it in two ways:

  • Stored energy is a property of the system: kinetic energy , potential energy , and internal energy U (molecular energy: kinetic, intermolecular potential, chemical, nuclear). Total .
  • Energy in transit exists only while it crosses the boundary: heat Q and work W. They are path functions.

Internal energy U is the sum of all molecular energies. For an ideal gas it depends only on temperature. Unit 2 links U, Q and W.

Q3. What do you mean by thermodynamic equilibrium? What is a quasi-static process and what is its characteristic feature?

Feb-Mar 2022 (CBCS) Q2(b) · Apr 2022 (CBCS) Q3(a) · Jun-Jul 2024 Unit 1

Three equilibria in three bullets, then a quasi-static sketch (weights removed one by one) with the p–V path.

Model answer

Thermodynamic equilibrium. A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when its properties do not change with time when it is isolated from the surroundings. It needs:

  • Mechanical equilibrium: no unbalanced force; pressure is uniform inside and balanced at the boundary.
  • Thermal equilibrium: no temperature difference, so no heat flow.
  • Chemical equilibrium: no chemical reaction and no mass transfer between phases; composition is steady.

Quasi-static process. A process in which the system deviates only infinitesimally from equilibrium at every instant, so it passes through a succession of equilibrium states. It is done infinitely slowly.

  • Small weights on a piston are removed one by one. After each removal the gas settles to a new equilibrium state.
  • Characteristic feature: every state on the path has a definite p, V, T, so the process can be drawn as a continuous curve on a property diagram. A non-quasi-static process (all weights removed at once) cannot be drawn: the intermediate states are not equilibrium states.
  • Work of a quasi-static process: W = ∫p dV.
  • A quasi-static process is the limit of a reversible process (no friction); the actual process is only approximately quasi-static.

Q4. State and explain the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics and mention its significance. Differentiate between diathermal and adiabatic walls. Do an alcohol and a mercury thermometer, both marked 0 °C at the ice point and 100 °C at the steam point, read exactly the same at 60 °C?

Aug-Sep 2023 Unit 1 · Apr 2022 (CBCS) Q2(b) · Jan 2023 (CBCS) Unit 1 · Jun-Jul 2024 Unit 1 · Nov-Dec 2025 Q1(b)

Statement, sketch with A, B, C, three points of significance, then the thermometer argument in 3 lines.

Model answer

Statement. If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

Explanation. Take bodies A and B and a third body C (a thermometer). Put C in contact with A until there is no change: T_A = T_C. Then put C in contact with B: T_B = T_C. The law says that A and B, if brought together, show no heat flow, so T_A = T_B.

Significance.

  • It is the basis of temperature measurement: a thermometer (C) can compare any two bodies without bringing them together.
  • It proves that temperature is a property, so a temperature scale can be defined.
  • It defines thermal equilibrium: equal temperature means no heat flow. It must come before the first and second laws, so it is called the zeroth law.

Diathermal and adiabatic walls. A diathermal wall (thin copper plate) allows heat to pass, so bodies on both sides reach thermal equilibrium. An adiabatic wall (thick insulation) does not allow heat to pass; the temperatures on the two sides can differ.

Alcohol and mercury thermometers. No, they will not read exactly the same at 60 °C. The two instruments agree only at the fixed points (0 and 100 °C), because both are marked there. In between, each scale is divided equally, but alcohol and mercury do not expand in the same way with temperature. So the readings differ slightly at 60 °C. The result depends on the thermometric substance; the gas thermometer is used as the standard.

Answer: the thermocouple thermometer reads 77.0 °C when the gas thermometer reads 70 °C
