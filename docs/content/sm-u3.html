Bending and Shear Stresses in Beams

A beam carries load by bending. This unit finds the stresses inside the beam: the bending stress that stretches one face and compresses the other, and the shear stress that tries to slide the layers over each other. With these two results you can check a beam for safety, choose its size, and see why steel beams have an I shape.

Concepts

Exam pattern. In the old papers this topic is "Unit IV": two full questions (choose one), 20 marks each. Almost every paper asks (a) a 10-mark derivation — the bending equation M/I = σ/y = E/R, or the shear stress formula and its distribution — and (b) a 10-mark numerical on a T-, I- or rectangular section. Learn the three derivations (bending equation, τ = FAȳ/(Ib), shear distribution) and the four numerical types in this unit.

1. Beams and types of beams

A beam is a structural member that carries loads at right angles (transverse) to its axis. The loads cause shear force F and bending moment M at every section (Unit 2). This unit uses F and M to find the stresses inside the beam.

Beams are classified by their supports (Fig. 3.1):

  • Cantilever: one end fixed, the other end free.
  • Simply supported: rests on a hinge (pin) at one end and a roller at the other end.
  • Overhanging: simply supported, but one or both ends extend past the supports.
  • Fixed (built-in): both ends fixed, so the ends cannot rotate.
  • Continuous: more than two supports.
  • Propped cantilever: a cantilever with an extra support (prop) at the free end.

The first three are statically determinate: the equations of equilibrium alone give the reactions. Fixed, continuous and propped beams are statically indeterminate.

Beams are also named by their cross-section: rectangular (timber), circular (shafts, pipes), I-section, T-section, channel and box sections (steel and cast iron).

2. Pure bending and simple bending

Pure bending (simple bending) is the state of a beam, or a part of it, where the bending moment is constant and the shear force is zero. Then the beam carries bending stress only, with no shear stress.

Fig. 3.2 shows the classic example. A simply supported beam carries two equal loads W at equal distances a from the supports. Between the loads (BC), F = W − W = 0 and M = W·a is constant. So part BC is in pure bending. A beam with equal and opposite couples at its ends is also in pure bending along its full length.

When a beam bends, it takes the shape of an arc. The top layers become shorter (compression) and the bottom layers become longer (tension) for a sagging beam. Between them is one layer that does not change length. This is the neutral layer (neutral surface). The line where the neutral layer cuts the cross-section is the neutral axis (N.A.).

3. Assumptions of the theory of simple bending

  1. The material of the beam is homogeneous (same composition everywhere) and isotropic (same elastic properties in all directions).
  2. The beam is initially straight, and every layer is free to expand or contract on its own.
  3. The stress is within the elastic limit, so Hooke's law holds.
  4. Young's modulus E is the same in tension and in compression.
  5. A transverse section that is plane before bending stays plane after bending (plane sections remain plane).
  6. The radius of curvature R is very large compared with the depth of the beam.
  7. The beam has a symmetrical section, and the loads act in the plane of symmetry, so it bends in that plane only.
  8. The beam is in pure bending: no shear force, no axial force.

7. Strength of sections: why I-sections are economical

For a given material, the strength of a beam in bending depends on Z. The weight and cost depend on the area A. So a good (economical) section has a large Z for a small A.

The bending stress is small near the N.A. and large at the extreme fibres (Fig. 3.4). Material near the N.A. does little work. An I-section moves most of the material into the flanges, far from the N.A., where the stress is high. The thin web holds the flanges apart and carries the shear force.

Fig. 3.7 compares two sections of the same area, 8000 mm². The square (89.4 mm side) has Z = 0.12 × 10⁶ mm³. The I-section 150 × 240 has Z = 0.66 × 10⁶ mm³, about 5.5 times more (Numerical N7). For the same reason a hollow circular shaft is stronger than a solid shaft of the same weight (N9).

  • Good for bending: I-section, box section, hollow circle, deep rectangle on edge.
  • Poor for bending: solid circle, square, flat rectangle.
  • Cast iron is strong in compression but weak in tension. So cast iron beams use an unequal I or T shape. Put the larger flange on the tension side. This moves the N.A. towards the tension face and keeps the tensile stress low (N8).
Beam of uniform strength. In most beams only the section at maximum M reaches the permissible stress. A beam of uniform strength has its section changed along the length (depth or width varies with M) so that the maximum bending stress is the same at every section. Examples: leaf springs, tapered cantilever brackets. It saves material.

8. Shear stress in beams: why it exists

In most beams the shear force F is not zero. F produces shear stress on the cross-section. By the principle of complementary shear (Unit 1), an equal shear stress acts on horizontal planes. So the layers of a beam try to slide over each other.

Fig. 3.8 shows this. Two loose planks under a load slide at the interface: their ends no longer match. If you glue them together, the joint must resist this sliding. The glue carries horizontal shear stress. In a solid beam, the material itself carries it.

Common mistake. The average value F/A is not the shear stress at every point. The shear stress is zero at the top and bottom faces and maximum at (or near) the N.A. Find it with the formula below.

Simple (pure) bending. A beam, or a part of it, is in simple bending when it carries a constant bending moment and no shear force. The stresses then caused are called bending (flexural) stresses.

Model answer

Bending stress. When a beam bends under load, the layers on the concave side shorten and those on the convex side lengthen. The resulting normal stresses on the cross-section are called bending stresses. They vary linearly from zero at the neutral axis to a maximum at the extreme fibres: σ = My/I. For a sagging moment the top is in compression and the bottom in tension.

Neutral axis passes through the centroid. The bending stress at distance y is σ = Ey/R. The force on a strip dA is (E/R) y dA. In pure bending there is no axial load, so Σ forces = (E/R)∫y dA = 0. Since E/R ≠ 0, ∫y dA = 0. The first moment of area is zero only about an axis through the centroid. Hence the neutral axis passes through the centroid.

Section modulus. The ratio of the moment of inertia of a section about its neutral axis to the distance of the extreme fibre from the neutral axis: Z = I/ymax. Unit mm³. Then M = σmaxZ.

Moment of resistance. The moment of all internal bending forces about the neutral axis. It balances the external bending moment. Its maximum safe value is MR = σperm × Z.

(a) The shear stress is maximum at the neutral axis (for rectangular, circular and I-sections) and zero at the extreme fibres. (For some shapes, such as a triangle, the maximum is a little away from the N.A.)

(b) Pure bending: the state of a beam where the bending moment is constant and the shear force is zero, e.g. the middle part of a beam with two equal symmetrical loads. Only bending stresses act.
