Concepts

Exam pattern. Unit IV is a 20-mark question with two parts of 10 marks. Part (a) is usually theory: phase change of a pure substance, Rankine cycle, effect of operating conditions, reheat or regeneration. Part (b) is a numerical: dryness fraction or internal energy of steam, a throttling calorimeter, or a Rankine cycle with pump work. Steam tables are allowed in the hall. Draw the sketch first: it carries half the marks.

1. Pure substance

A pure substance has a fixed chemical composition throughout. It may exist in one phase (solid, liquid or gas) or in a mixture of phases. Water, ice and steam together are still one pure substance, because all three are H2O. Air is a pure substance only while it is a gas (a mixture of gases with a fixed composition).

Steam is the working fluid of a vapour power plant. It is cheap, safe, easy to pump as a liquid, and it has a large latent heat. That is why this unit studies water.

2. Formation of steam at constant pressure

Take 1 kg of water in a piston-cylinder. The weight on the piston keeps the pressure constant. Heat the water and watch the temperature. Fig. 4.1 shows the five stages.

  1. (a) to (b), sensible heating. The water temperature rises until it reaches the saturation temperature Tsat for that pressure. Water at (b) is saturated liquid. The heat added is the enthalpy of liquid, hf.
  2. (b) to (d), evaporation. The temperature stays constant at Tsat while the liquid turns into vapour. The heat added is the latent heat hfg. In between, the mixture is wet steam (c). At (d) the last drop has evaporated: dry saturated steam.
  3. (d) to (e), superheating. More heat raises the temperature above Tsat. The steam is superheated. The heat added is cp(T − Tsat).

If the pressure is higher, Tsat is higher and hfg is smaller. At the critical pressure (22.06 MPa for water) the latent heat becomes zero: liquid turns into vapour with no boiling stage. Fig. 4.2 and Fig. 4.3 show this on T–v and T–h axes.

3. Terms used with steam

  • Saturation temperature, Tsat. The temperature at which water boils at a given pressure. At 1 bar it is 99.6 °C; at 10 bar, 179.9 °C.
  • Saturated liquid. Water at Tsat that has not started to evaporate. Dryness fraction x = 0.
  • Wet steam. A mixture of saturated liquid and saturated vapour at Tsat. 0 < x < 1.
  • Dry saturated steam. Vapour at Tsat with no liquid drops. x = 1.
  • Superheated steam. Vapour at a temperature above Tsat for its pressure. Its properties come from the superheated table. Degree of superheat = T − Tsat.
  • Dryness fraction, x. Mass of dry vapour in 1 kg of wet steam: . It has no unit.
  • Latent heat, hfg. Heat needed to change 1 kg of saturated liquid into saturated vapour at constant pressure.
  • Critical point. The top of the saturation dome. For water: 22.06 MPa and 374 °C. Here hfg = 0 and liquid and vapour look the same.
  • Triple point. The one state where solid, liquid and vapour exist together in equilibrium. For water: 0.01 °C and 0.611 kPa.

A phase diagram (p–T, Fig. 4.4) shows the regions. The fusion line separates solid from liquid. The vaporization line separates liquid from vapour; it ends at the critical point. The sublimation line separates solid from vapour. The three lines meet at the triple point. For water the fusion line slopes up to the left, because ice is less dense than water.

4. Property diagrams: p–v, T–s and h–s

On every diagram the saturation dome has the saturated liquid line (x = 0) on the left and the saturated vapour line (x = 1) on the right. They meet at the critical point. Inside the dome the steam is wet. Left of the dome is compressed liquid. Right of the dome is superheated vapour.

  • p–v diagram (Fig. 4.5). Inside the dome the constant-temperature line is horizontal (boiling at constant p). The T = Tc line passes through the critical point with a point of inflection.
  • T–s diagram (Fig. 4.6). The constant-pressure line is horizontal inside the dome. Lines of constant x run from the dome to the critical point. Area under a reversible path equals heat transfer.
  • h–s (Mollier) diagram (Fig. 4.7). Enthalpy against entropy. Inside the dome a constant-pressure line is a straight line of slope T. An ideal (isentropic) turbine expansion is a vertical line, so h1 − h2s is read directly.
Memory trick. On a Mollier chart, ideal = vertical. A turbine always moves down (work taken out). The real path leans to the right because entropy increases.

5. Steam tables and property formulas

Steam tables give the properties of water. There are two kinds.

  • Saturation table (by pressure or by temperature). Columns: Tsat (or psat), vf, vg, hf, hfg, hg, sf, sfg, sg, and often uf, ug.
  • Superheated table. Look up pressure and temperature; read v, u, h, s together. Interpolate linearly between rows.

Subscripts: f = saturated liquid, g = saturated vapour, fg = change on evaporation (g − f). Some values from your notes:

Example. Wet steam at 10 bar, x = 0.9. Then h = 762.7 + 0.9 × 2014.4 = 2575.7 kJ/kg. To raise it to 300 °C at 10 bar: q = 3051.7 − 2575.7 = 476.0 kJ/kg.

Finding x from a measured value. Rearrange: x = (h − hf)/hfg, or x = (s − sf)/sfg, or x = (v − vf)/vfg. For an isentropic expansion s is the same before and after, so x2s = (s1 − sf2)/sfg2. This formula is used in every Rankine numerical.

Common mistake. Use the hf, hfg, sf, sfg of the condenser pressure row for the turbine exit, not of the boiler pressure row.

Measuring the dryness fraction: throttling calorimeter

Wet steam is bled from the main through a throttle valve into an insulated chamber at low pressure (about 1.2 bar). Throttling is a constant-enthalpy process, h1 = h2. If the pressure after the valve is low enough, the steam becomes superheated and its temperature t2 is measured. Then h2 = hg2 + cp(t2 − tsat2) and x1 = (h2 − hf1)/hfg1. If the steam is too wet, it stays wet after throttling and the method fails (then use a separating and throttling calorimeter).

6. The Rankine cycle

The Rankine cycle is the ideal cycle for a steam power plant. It has four steady-flow devices and four processes. Fig. 4.9 shows the plant, Fig. 4.10 the T–s diagram and Fig. 4.11 the h–s diagram.

4–1BoilerConstant-pressure heat addition: liquid heated, boiled, superheated
1–2TurbineIsentropic (reversible adiabatic) expansion; work out
2–3CondenserConstant-pressure heat rejection to saturated liquid
3–4PumpIsentropic compression of liquid; work in

Work ratio is close to 1 (about 0.99) because pump work is tiny. Specific steam consumption (SSC) = steam needed for 1 kWh of net output; wnet in kJ/kg, since 1 kWh = 3600 kJ. Lower SSC is better.

Mass flow and power. For a steam flow of ṁ kg/s: turbine power = ṁ wT, net power = ṁ wnet, boiler heat = ṁ qin, condenser heat = ṁ(h2 − h3).

Rankine cycle against the Carnot cycle

The Carnot cycle has the highest efficiency between two temperatures. In a wet-steam Carnot cycle (Fig. 4.12) the pump would have to compress a wet mixture, which is difficult and unsafe, and the turbine would have wet steam at its exit. The Rankine cycle condenses the steam fully, so the pump handles only liquid. The price is that the liquid is heated over a range of temperatures (4–a), so the mean temperature of heat addition is lower and η is below the Carnot value.

7. Actual Rankine cycle

Real turbines and pumps have friction and leakage. The expansion is not isentropic: entropy increases (1–2 in Fig. 4.13). The real turbine gives less work. The real pump needs more work.

ηT is the turbine isentropic efficiency (typically 0.85–0.92). ηP is the pump isentropic efficiency (0.70–0.85). 2s and 4s are the ideal end states.

The real exhaust steam is drier (higher x) than the ideal exhaust, although the work is smaller. Pressure drops in the boiler, pipes and condenser reduce the work a little more.

Reheating (introductory)

The steam expands in the high-pressure (HP) turbine, goes back to the boiler, is reheated at constant pressure to a high temperature (usually the original), and then expands in the low-pressure (LP) turbine. Fig. 4.15 and Fig. 4.16 show the plant and the T–s diagram.

State numbers as in the figure. h6 = h5 + wP is the boiler feed.

  • Main benefit: the exhaust steam is much drier, so blades are not eroded by water drops.
  • It lets the plant use a higher boiler pressure and still keep x > 0.88–0.90.
  • Work per kg increases. The gain in η is small and depends on the reheat pressure.
  • Cost: extra piping, reheater, and pressure drop.

Regeneration with one open feedwater heater (introductory)

Part of the steam is bled from the turbine and used to heat the feedwater before it enters the boiler. In an open feedwater heater the bled steam mixes directly with the feedwater and leaves as saturated liquid at the bleed pressure. The boiler then receives hotter water, so the mean temperature of heat addition rises. See Fig. 4.17 and Fig. 4.18.

Take 1 kg of steam at the turbine inlet. A fraction y is bled at state 2. The remaining (1 − y) goes to the condenser. Energy balance on the heater (adiabatic, no work):

h4 = condensate (saturated liquid at condenser p), h5 = after pump 1, h6 = saturated liquid at bleed pressure (heater outlet), h7 = after pump 2 (boiler feed).

Why does η rise although less steam does work in the LP turbine? The bled steam gives up its latent heat to the feedwater instead of to the cooling water. Heat added in the boiler starts from a higher temperature, and less heat is rejected in the condenser.

9. Introduction to steam turbines

A steam turbine turns the energy of high-pressure steam into shaft work. Steam first expands in a nozzle (or in fixed blades) and gains velocity. Moving blades then take momentum from the jet and turn the rotor.

Impulse turbineReaction turbine
Pressure dropOnly in the fixed nozzlesIn both fixed and moving blades
Pressure over moving bladesConstantFalls
Force on bladesImpulse of the jet onlyImpulse plus reaction of the expanding steam
Blade shapeSymmetric, curved like a bucketAerofoil, like a nozzle
ExamplesDe Laval, Curtis, RateauParsons
Steam admissionOften partial, by nozzlesFull, all round the rotor

A single row cannot use a large pressure drop, because the blade speed would be too high. Compounding divides the drop: velocity compounding (Curtis: one nozzle, several moving rows), pressure compounding (Rateau: several nozzle and blade stages) and a mix of the two. Large power-station turbines use many reaction or impulse–reaction stages. Wet exhaust steam erodes the last blades, which is why exit dryness is kept above 0.88.

10. Introduction to condensers

The condenser turns exhaust steam into water. It also keeps a vacuum at the turbine exit (0.04–0.1 bar), which raises the turbine work. Cooling water takes away the latent heat. An air pump removes air that leaks in, because air would spoil the vacuum.

  • Surface condenser (Fig. 4.21). Steam condenses on the outside of tubes carrying cooling water. Steam and water do not mix, so the pure condensate returns to the boiler. Used in all large plants.
  • Jet (direct-contact) condenser (Fig. 4.22). Cooling water is sprayed into the steam and they mix. Simple and cheap, but the condensate is contaminated and cannot be reused as boiler feed. Used only for small plants.

Q1. With a neat sketch, explain the phase change of a pure substance (formation of steam) at constant pressure. Define wet, dry saturated and superheated steam and the dryness fraction.

Piston-cylinder with five stages, then the T–v (or T–h) curve with a, b, c, d, e marked. Give the heat added in each stage.

Model answer

Introduction. A pure substance has a fixed chemical composition. Consider 1 kg of water at 1 bar (any fixed pressure) in a piston-cylinder with a constant weight on the piston. Heat is added slowly.

  1. Compressed (sub-cooled) liquid. Water below Tsat. Heating raises the temperature. Heat added = hf (sensible heat).
  2. Saturated liquid. Water at Tsat (99.6 °C at 1 bar). The first vapour bubble forms. x = 0.
  3. Wet steam. Liquid and vapour together at constant Tsat. Heat goes into evaporation, so the temperature stays constant. Volume rises. 0 < x < 1.
  4. Dry saturated steam. The last drop has evaporated. x = 1. Total heat since saturated liquid = latent heat hfg. Enthalpy = hg = hf + hfg.
  5. Superheated steam. More heat raises the temperature above Tsat. Heat added = cp(Tsup − Tsat). The difference Tsup − Tsat is the degree of superheat.

Q2. Using the phase (p–T) diagram, explain the sublimation line, the vaporization line and the line of fusion (melting). Mark the triple point and the critical point.

Nov-Dec 2025 Q8(a)

Draw p–T with three lines meeting at the triple point; name the three regions; define each line in one sentence.

Model answer
  • Fusion (melting) line. The boundary between solid and liquid. Crossing it at constant pressure, the solid melts (or the liquid freezes). For water the line slopes up to the left, because ice is less dense than liquid water (it floats).
  • Vaporization line. The boundary between liquid and vapour. It shows the saturation pressure for each temperature (boiling point against pressure). It starts at the triple point and ends at the critical point.
  • Sublimation line. The boundary between solid and vapour. Along it a solid changes directly into vapour, without becoming liquid (dry ice, naphthalene). It exists only below the triple-point pressure.
  • Triple point. The one point where the three lines meet and all three phases coexist. For water: 0.01 °C and 0.611 kPa. It is used to define the kelvin.
  • Critical point. End of the vaporization line (22.06 MPa, 373.95 °C for water). Above it there is no sharp boundary between liquid and vapour: the fluid is supercritical.

Why not Carnot?

Q4. Explain with T–s diagrams the effect of (i) increasing the average temperature of heat addition and (ii) decreasing the temperature of heat rejection on the Rankine cycle efficiency. How does reheating help?

Model answer

Principle. Since , efficiency rises when the mean temperature of heat addition goes up, or the temperature of heat rejection goes down.

  1. Higher boiler pressure. Tsat rises, so Tm,H rises and η rises. But the exhaust steam becomes wetter (lower x). Limit: boiler strength and exhaust wetness (x should be above 0.88).
  2. More superheat. The turbine inlet temperature rises, so Tm,H rises and η rises. The exhaust is drier. Limit: creep strength of metals, about 540–620 °C.
  3. Lower condenser pressure. Tm,L falls, so η rises. Limit: the temperature of the cooling water (Tsat must be above it) and air leakage. The exhaust becomes wetter and the last blades longer.

  • It allows a higher boiler pressure.
